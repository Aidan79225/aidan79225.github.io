*27 December*

Su Qi found out at the end of December.

Not because of the tin box, not because of the smell of liquor on his clothes, but because of a uniform.

27 December, a Saturday. Past noon only half the stalls were left in the South Slope market. The fish stall had packed up, and the water on the flagstones had dried to a white mark. Su Qi’s stall was still open. In the basket under the cutting table lay two pieces of other people’s work, finished; in the cloth bundle one last thing was left, Xiaoman’s dark blue winter uniform from last year. On the night of the Winter Solstice Su Qi had measured the sleeve and said it was short. The next day she had brought it to the stall, and it had lain at the bottom of the bundle for four days. Other people’s work came first.

She spread the uniform on her knee and began with the right cuff. The little unpicking knife lifted one stitch, the thread went slack, and the edge of the cloth turned out. She had made this uniform last year. The cloth was still crisp, and in the lining of the cuff she had left an inch and a half to spare, waiting to be let down today. The thread of the seam in that lining had worked loose last year. She remembered it. She had kept saying she would mend it, and had never mended it. Her fingers felt along the seam, measuring what there was to spare.

They touched a hard corner.

There should be nothing hard inside cloth. She turned the cuff right back. Into the loose stretch a little square had been pushed, paper, folded very small, the folds pressed with a fingernail. With the point of her needle she worked it out of the seam, laid it on the cutting table and opened it. One fold, two folds, three. On the paper was one line of writing, in fountain pen.

Nanji Teahouse, investment, three thousand dollars exactly, received by Liang Henian.

It was in Qiusheng’s hand.

She knew it. The two characters in the margin of Xiaoman’s textbook, the word for teacher: the strokes finished in just this way; every paper in the house that wanted a signature had been signed with this one pen. The name Liang Henian she knew too. It was in the newspaper every day. No case too lost. The Nanji, the teahouse beside the Tin Hau Temple in the Old Town: half its signboard had burned in November, and the market had talked of it for days.

“Ah Qi, packing up?”

Ah Gui leaned over from the greens stall next door, one hand propped on the edge of the cutting table. Su Qi’s palm lay over the paper.

“Packing up.”

“Early today.”

“No customers.”

Ah Gui glanced at the uniform on her knee, glanced at her hand, and went back to her own stall. On the right the owner of the sundries stall was putting up his shutter boards. Su Qi folded the paper back along its folds, folded it in eight, and put it in her apron pocket. The cuff was half unpicked and the needle was still stuck in the lining. She did not pull it out. She folded the uniform and put it in the cloth bundle, needle and all. She drew the cloth cover over the treadle sewing machine and tipped the coals from the charcoal iron into the iron bucket. She packed up an hour earlier than usual.

At the foot of the stairs Mrs Chen’s door was open.

“You’re early today, Mrs He.”

“No customers.”

A little after four Xiaoman came home from school. She had not yet put down her satchel. Su Qi took twenty cents from her apron pocket and put it in her hand.

“You’re sleeping at a school friend’s tonight.”

“Mama?”

“Come back first thing tomorrow.”

Xiaoman looked at the table. There was nothing on the table. She put the twenty cents in her skirt pocket, shifted her satchel to the other shoulder, and went. Down the stairs two at a time.

Su Qi did not cook. She went to the chest of drawers and pulled open the top drawer. The ticket for the school play was at the very back, red paper, the stub still joined to the ticket. She took it out and put it on the table. Then she took the little square from her apron pocket, opened it, smoothed it flat fold by fold, and laid it beside the ticket. She lit only one lamp in the front room, the one on the table. She sat down in the rattan chair, her face to the door, both hands on her knees, empty.

---

At half past five the footsteps on the stairs stopped at the second floor. The door opened.

Qiusheng stood in the doorway. He did not take off his shoes. From half the front room away he could tell that the writing was his own.

“Where’s Xiaoman?” he asked.

“I’ve sent her to sleep at a school friend’s,” said Su Qi. “Qiusheng. Sit down.”

He sat down. The rattan chair was hers. He sat on the square stool by the table, and the table was between him and her. The kitchen tap had not been turned off tight: a drop, then a drop.

“Three thousand dollars,” said Su Qi, her voice very level. “How much does your night shift pay a month?”

Qiusheng looked at the lamp.

“A night shift checking cargo at a trading house is sixteen dollars a month. Ah Gui’s husband says so,” said Su Qi. “Three thousand dollars. Fifteen and a half years.”

Qiusheng’s hands lay on his knees.

“Do you know what I make in a day at the tailor’s stall?” Su Qi asked. “Two dollars. Three on a good day. I have done it for twenty years, and all I have put by does not come to three thousand.” She pushed the receipt across in front of him. “Qiusheng, what is this?”

He looked at the receipt, at his own handwriting. He had assayed liquor for twenty years, and he knew that whatever a glass of spirit hid, it could not hide it over a flame.

“I’m making liquor,” he said.

Su Qi’s hand stopped at the edge of the receipt.

“Since when?”

“September.”

“August,” said Su Qi. “The old women at the spring have worked it out. Four buckets a trip, a wedding that went on a month, a feast of forty tables.”

He did not take it up.

“White Dew,” she said.

He raised his head and looked at her.

“The one who drives the truck,” said Su Qi. “He stood in the doorway and called you Teacher, and the smell on him was the same as on your coat.”

“Ah Yue.”

“Everyone on the docks is talking of it,” said Su Qi. “Ah Gui, who sells greens at the market: her husband used to get drunk once a week, on the kind that blinds you. These last months he has gone over to White Dew. Ah Gui says a schoolteacher makes it, and on the docks they all call him Teacher.”

Her voice began to shake.

“Qiusheng, when I heard all this at the market, I thought to myself: that Teacher is a good man.”

“Qi—”

“And then I thought of the smell on your clothes,” said Su Qi. “At Mid-Autumn it was fish. With the winter it turned to soy sauce. Qiusheng, I have washed clothes for twenty years.” Her finger pressed once on the receipt. “I thought of you coming home every day at first light. I thought of you saying at Mid-Autumn, Xiaoman’s school fees we’ll pay ourselves.”

She put out her hand, picked up the receipt, folded it in half, and in half again.

“I kept telling myself it wasn’t you. Because you are an assayer. All your life you have been catching the people who make bootleg.”

“I’m saving lives,” said Qiusheng. “Firewater killed forty-one people in one month. Qi, forty-one. The liquor I make won’t kill anyone.”

“Then why didn’t you tell me?”

Qiusheng opened his mouth, and shut it again.

“Because you know it’s wrong,” said Su Qi. “You can tell the men on the docks you’re saving lives. You can tell Ah Yue. But you didn’t dare tell me. Because you knew I would ask you: Qiusheng, what about Qiao Zheng? What about Xiaoman? If you are caught, what is Xiaoman to do?”

“Qiao Zheng is after liquor.”

“He is after you,” said Su Qi. “At Mid-Autumn, by the rattan chair, he said there was only one who could make it as well as this. I was standing in the kitchen doorway with the fish. He was looking at you when he said it.”

Qiusheng’s hand lay on the edge of the table.

“Three thousand dollars, in a teahouse. In the teahouse’s books, whose name?”

“Mine.”

“So when he comes, he goes to the teahouse first, and then he comes here.”

She glanced at the ticket on the table.

“The twenty-seventh of November, a Thursday. She stood on the stage for three lines.”

Qiusheng looked at the ticket. Red paper, the words “The Landing”, the stub joined.

The front room was very quiet. The tap, a drop, then a drop.

“I’m ill,” said Qiusheng.

Su Qi raised her head.

“Consumption,” said Qiusheng. “It was diagnosed in July. There is a cavity in the right lung. Dr Lin says hospital, then up to the hills. Three hundred dollars, and fifty a month in the hills. We can’t pay it.”

“July,” said Su Qi. “The prescription in your coat pocket, with no stamp on the top line. I found it doing the washing. It has been in the biscuit tin at the stall for five months.”

Qiusheng’s hand tightened on the edge of the table.

“I thought it was a cough,” she said.

Su Qi looked at him, and at last the tears fell.

“Is that why?”

“At first it was,” said Qiusheng. “Later…”

Later it was because he was very good at it. He did not go on.

Su Qi wiped away the tears, stood up and went into the bedroom. Qiusheng heard her open the wardrobe, take out the suitcase, fold clothes one by one. She did not fold fast. One, a pause, then another. He began to cough, his handkerchief pressed to his mouth, his other hand pressed to the right side of his chest. In the bedroom the sound of folding stopped for a moment. When the coughing was over the sound began again.

“Where are you going?”

“To my mother’s,” said Su Qi from the bedroom. “I’m taking Xiaoman.”

“Qi, the village is too far, Xiaoman has school—”

“The village has a school. She can go there until the summer holidays, and come back on the ferry for the examination days.” Su Qi came out carrying the suitcase. It was not fastened. She put it on the floor by the door. She stood beside the suitcase and looked at him.

“From the day of White Dew to today, a hundred and eleven nights. Xiaoman asleep and you not yet back. Xiaoman awake and you only just back. The night of Mid-Autumn was the only one you were at home, and you stood on the balcony all night,” she said. “I’ve counted. Every time, you chose something other than us.”

Qiusheng’s hand was still on the edge of the table.

“Qiusheng, I won’t go to the police. I won’t tell Qiao Zheng. I won’t tell anyone. But I can’t have Xiaoman living in a house with three thousand dollars hidden in the wardrobe. I can’t have her not knowing, every night, whether her father is coming home.”

She crouched and fastened the suitcase. Two catches. One, then the other.

“Your illness is yours to see to. Your money is yours to keep.” She paused. “Xiaoman’s school fees you can pay. It is the one thing you can do for her.”

She stood up. The suitcase stood by the door, and she pushed it straight against the wall.

“There is still other people’s work at the stall. When it’s handed back, we go. The ferry on New Year’s Day.”

“Does Xiaoman know?”

“I’ll tell her tomorrow.”

Qiusheng asked nothing more. He picked up the twice-folded receipt from the table, folded it once more along its old folds, in eight, and put it in his shirt pocket. Su Qi picked up the ticket, went to the chest of drawers, pulled open the top drawer, put it in and pushed the drawer shut.

She went into the kitchen and turned the tap off tight. The dripping stopped.

Qiusheng stood up. The door shut, and his steps went down the stairs one at a time, to the bottom. At the foot of the stairs Mrs Chen’s door stood open a crack, and a voice behind it said Mr He was early today. He did not answer. Su Qi came out of the kitchen and stood by the table. The table was bare. Only the lamp was left. She took the uniform out of the cloth bundle and spread it on the table, the cuff half unpicked, the needle stuck in the lining. She looked at it a moment. She did not touch the needle.

---

That night there was only the sound of the pot in the cellar. Ah Yue took the truck out at ten, and Old Qi rode with it. At the workbench Qiusheng opened the notebook, turned past the filled pages, and came to the last page, a blank one. The first line he left empty. Under it he wrote three lines: *Rice wine, forty-fifth vat, ninety-three. Sorghum, fifth pit, ninety-one. Rum, fourth vat, ninety.*

Three small glasses stood in a row under the lamp. He smelled them one by one, poured into the measuring glass, read the scale, smelled again. Poured it back. Changed a figure and poured again. He coughed for a while, one hand holding the handkerchief over his mouth, the other still steadying the measuring glass. When the coughing was done he poured what was in the measuring glass back into the jar, rinsed the glass and stood it upside down. The first line was still empty.

Ah Yue came back at twelve, got to the foot of the stairs and stopped. The Teacher still had on the coat he had worn by day, and there was a layer of mud from the slope on his shoes.

“Teacher, you haven’t changed your clothes today.”

“Mm.”

Ah Yue glanced at the three glasses under the lamp. “Blending again?”

“Mm.”

“Teacher, if it’s a little off, the docks can’t taste it.”

“I can.”

Ah Yue opened the stove door a crack and squatted to look at the fire.

“Ah Lian?” Qiusheng asked.

“Still in hospital.” Ah Yue looked at the fire. “Half the gauze is off. She asks when you’ll go on with her characters.”

“When she’s out, we’ll see.”

Ah Yue nodded once. He did not turn round.

---

New Year’s Day was five days away. For those five days, all of Fog Harbour was waiting for White Dew.

Every day at half past five he went back to South Slope.

Sunday, half past five. Su Qi sat in the rattan chair with a pair of Xiaoman’s padded trousers on her knee, letting down the legs. The suitcase stood by the door, fastened. On the corner of the table lay the dark blue uniform, the right cuff half unpicked, the needle stuck in the lining, not touched since Saturday. Xiaoman’s door was shut, a line of light under it. Su Qi did not speak. Nor did he. Qiusheng changed his clothes and went out at six.

On Sunday Old Lu had come by. He came straight from work, squatted by the three bricks a quarter of an hour, patted the bricks, stood up, walked past the workbench, glanced at the three small glasses under the lamp, did not stop, went up the stairs and was gone.

At eleven at night only two tables were still taken in the main room of the Nanji.

Qiusheng went round by the square. The gate of the Tin Hau Temple was shut, and a point of red was left in the incense burner. Under the eaves of the arcade the half signboard hung, one character, Nan. At night it could not be made out, and by day it was still the only character left. He went in by the alley behind.

The waiter was upending the chairs onto the empty tables one by one. The little stage in the corner was empty, the stool upended on it, the lamp unlit. The table by the stairs was empty, the bowl cleared away, Old Qi riding with the truck tonight. At a table by the window sat two men: a young man in a suit, his tie loosened, with a pad, a fountain pen and a pot of tea before him; and facing him Counsel Liang, his handkerchief spread on his knee, his fountain pen lying across the table. Qiusheng sat at the next table with his back to them, a bowl of tea before him, undrunk. Ah Yue had brought the message that afternoon: Counsel Liang said it was the reporter’s third night of waiting, and Teacher should come and listen.

The reporter spoke first.

“Mr Liang, three nights, and I have drunk six pots of tea.”

“Tea at the Nanji is ten cents a pot,” said Counsel Liang. “Mr Reporter, sixty cents.”

“The paper pays.” The reporter opened his pad. “Mr Liang, I want only three things. Who makes it, how it’s made, where it can be bought. What the readers want is the secret.”

“The secret.” Counsel Liang picked up the handkerchief and wiped his forehead. “Mr Reporter, the Nanji sells tea. The tea has a secret, handed down in the proprietress’s family. If you care to write it, Mr Reporter, I shall have the waiter recite it to you. Liquor, the Nanji does not have.”

“The docks have called it White Dew for four months. This month Upper Town has started calling it that too. It’s even printed on the Paramount’s New Year menu,” said the reporter. “A dollar fifty a bottle on the docks, fifty cents a glass in Upper Town. Mr Liang, a business this size, and nobody is running it?”

“In Fog Harbour there is a great deal of business that nobody is running.”

“Some on the docks say there’s more than one spirit in it.”

“What the men on the docks say,” said Counsel Liang. “Mr Reporter, would your paper dare print half of it?”

“Some say it’s a schoolteacher.”

Counsel Liang’s fountain pen tapped once on the table. “Schoolteachers in Fog Harbour. There are twenty in a single street of Upper Town. Go and ask them, Mr Reporter. Find one who will own to it, and I shall stand you tea for a month.”

The reporter laughed, not for long. He shut the pad and put the fountain pen back in his pocket.

“I’ll be back tomorrow night.”

“The tea will still be ten cents.”

The reporter went. His leather shoes sounded under the arcade as far as the roundabout. The waiter upended the chairs onto his table. Counsel Liang folded the handkerchief, turned round and looked at Qiusheng’s back.

Qiusheng turned his bowl half round.

“The receipt,” he said.

Counsel Liang waited.

“She has seen it.”

Counsel Liang shook the folded handkerchief out again, wiped his forehead twice over, and kept it clutched in his hand.

“Mrs He.”

“Mm.”

“Teacher, will she—”

“No.”

Counsel Liang looked out across the main room. The waiter was behind the counter turning off the wireless, his back to them.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset: if the reporter writes nothing, it is because there is tea enough to drink. If your wife says nothing, it is not because of tea,” he said. “That paper. You have it back?”

Qiusheng pressed his hand to his shirt pocket.

“Keep it safe,” said Counsel Liang. “There is a copy in the Nanji’s books. That is enough.” He changed the handkerchief to his left hand. “When does Mrs He go?”

“New Year’s Day.”

“The ferry?”

“Mm.”

“The Saltflat ferry. The ferryman’s tongue is quicker than his boat,” said Counsel Liang. “Over at Headman Ah Lu’s they will know in three days that Mrs He has come home to stay.”

“He knows.”

Counsel Liang looked at him a moment and did not ask what it was he knew. He stood up and put the handkerchief back in his pocket. Qiusheng went out by the back door. The bolt dropped behind him, iron, one sound. Three stone steps in the alley behind, and the patch where the moss was worn away was still there.

---

On Monday morning Su Qi handed back the last two pieces of other people’s work.

A skirt from the girls’ school, twenty cents; a postman’s uniform trousers, ten cents. The money went into the biscuit tin under the cutting table, and she pressed the lid on. The cutting table was bare. The cloth bundle was empty. The dark blue uniform was on the table at home. She had not brought it.

Ah Gui leaned on the edge of the cutting table. “Ah Qi, that one of your Xiaoman’s, not let down yet?”

“I’m not letting it down.”

“It’s a good bit short.”

“I’m not letting it down,” said Su Qi. She took the tape measure from her neck, rolled it up and put it in the sewing box.

“That man of mine says the union’s giving out a glass a head on New Year’s Eve,” said Ah Gui. “White Dew.”

“Mm.”

Ah Gui watched her pack the sewing box, and asked nothing more.

At eleven Su Qi packed up. The market was still busy, and the ice water from the fish stall was still running. She did not cut through the market. She went round by the road outside, and up the slope. Halfway up the slope, at the turn of the stairs, hung a wooden board: Physician · Diseases of the Chest. Two people sat on the waiting bench. The electric fan was still. It was not run in winter. She took the seat nearest the wall, the sewing box held on her knees. The nurse asked her surname.

“Mrs He,” she said.

The nurse wrote one character in the book and went in.

---

At three on Monday afternoon Ah Yue came in by the iron door at the goods-yard end. The sound of the chain came first.

“Teacher, Boss Ding. His car followed me in from the goods yard. It’s outside the door.”

There was no fire in the cellar. Qiusheng was at the workbench washing the measuring glass. Old Qi was on the upturned vat in the corner, his tea bowl on the floor at his left hand. Old Qi was the first on his feet. He glanced at the iron door at the goods-yard end, and only then walked towards it.

Outside the iron door was the goods track. Ah Yue’s truck stood beside the track, ten crates in the bed, covered with sacking. Beyond it was the gravel of the goods yard, and on the gravel a black motor car, its door open. Boss Ding stood by the car door, a camel-hair overcoat over his white suit, unbuttoned, a gold watch showing below the cuff. The floor manager stood half a pace in front of him with a book in his hand and a folded card tucked into it.

Qiusheng stood inside the iron door. The chain hung across the doorway, waist high. Old Qi stood behind him, his back against the door frame, his eyes on the railway. Ah Yue stood by the bed of the truck, one hand resting on the sacking.

The floor manager spoke first.

“Boss Ding wants a hundred bottles for New Year’s Eve.”

“That’s settled,” said Ah Yue. “The afternoon of the thirty-first, handed over in the goods yard, two dollars a bottle.”

The floor manager did not look at Ah Yue. “A hundred and twenty seats on New Year’s Eve, every one booked. On the wine list White Dew is fifty cents a glass, cheaper than the imported liquor, and the guests order a good deal of it.” He held the card out across the chain. “Boss Ding says, would Teacher kindly look it over.”

Qiusheng took it. Card, gilt-edged, the Paramount’s name in foreign lettering at the top, and under it the menu for New Year’s Eve: cold dishes, soup, roast meat, dessert, line by line. The drinks were at the very bottom, and two kinds were printed. One was imported liquor, twelve dollars a bottle. One was two words, White Dew, fifty cents a glass.

Boss Ding came over from the car door and stopped in front of the chain. He looked Qiusheng over once, from his head to his shoes.

“Teacher,” he said.

Ah Yue wiped his hand down his trousers.

“The Paramount’s guests. Upper Town people. Consulate people,” said Boss Ding. “The dollar-fifty stuff from the docks, they can taste it. Those hundred bottles for New Year’s Eve are to be the real White Dew, not the stuff the docks drink.”

“The real one,” said Qiusheng.

“You make it, Teacher. You know,” said Boss Ding. He put a hand out towards Ah Yue. Ah Yue lifted the sacking, drew a bottle from a crate, came over and handed it to him. He pulled the cork and smelled it once. The nails on the fingers that held the cork were trimmed and very clean. He passed the bottle to the floor manager. “This is the stuff the docks drink.”

The floor manager put the bottle in the car.

“Teacher,” said Boss Ding, “the guests ask me what the real White Dew tastes like. I have to give them some answer.”

Qiusheng looked at the card in his hand. The words White Dew were printed at the very bottom, in gilt, a size smaller than the names of the dishes.

He did not answer.

Boss Ding waited a moment. He pushed his cuff up and glanced at his watch. “The thirty-first,” he said, and got into the car. The floor manager got in after him. The car backed out over the gravel of the goods yard and went on as far as the level crossing. The barrier was up, and it went through.

Old Qi spoke from the door frame. “Next time hand over in the goods yard.” He watched the car go over the level crossing. “The car stays out.”

“He didn’t come in the door,” said Ah Yue.

“The car came in.”

Ah Yue pulled the sacking back over the crates. “Teacher, the stuff the docks drink is the stuff he smelled just now. He’s been drinking it four months.”

Qiusheng folded the card and put it in his shirt pocket, on top of the receipt.

“Teacher, what is it he wants?”

“We’ll see.”

“We’ll see,” said Ah Yue. He climbed onto the truck and pulled the rope on the bed a turn tighter. “Teacher, every time you say we’ll see, two days later there’s a new pot. Which one is it this time?”

Qiusheng did not answer. Old Qi gave the chain on the door ring a pull, looked first at the railway, and only then went back down to the cellar.

The docks had called it White Dew for a long time. Qiusheng’s notebook did not have the words yet.

Monday, half past five. Su Qi was in the rattan chair. On her knee now was the sewing box, its lid open. She was winding thread, one reel wound even, then another. The suitcase stood by the door, a hemp rope tied round it. Xiaoman was doing her homework at the far end of the table. She looked up at him once.

“Papa.”

She bent her head again. Her pencil scratched in the arithmetic book. The uniform was still on the corner of the table. Qiusheng went into the bedroom to change. Through the door he heard Xiaoman speak.

“Mama, the school at Grandmother’s, do they have English?”

“They have a teacher.”

“One?”

“One,” said Su Qi. “Do your homework.”

The pencil scratched again. Qiusheng came out, and at six he left. Xiaoman did not look up.

---

At midday on Tuesday the ice truck made an extra trip.

It backed in through the side gate, its tail to the iron door at the head of the stairs. Sawdust, crushed ice, and under the ice the empty crates. This time the driver got down. He stood at the tail of the truck and did not come in at the iron door.

“Madam Wen’s orders. The New Year goods go first.”

The crates were lifted on. Ah Yue was at the goods yard. It was Ah Cai and Ah Shui who lifted, Ah Shui steadying the corner of each crate with his left hand. The driver watched the crates buried in the ice and slapped his hand once on the side of the bed.

“Madam Wen asks when the new spirit will be ready.”

Qiusheng stood on the stairs inside the iron door, the notebook in his hand.

“Which new spirit?”

“It’s Madam Wen asking,” said the driver.

He got in. The truck drove out by the side gate. Water dripped from its tail onto the flagstones, and dripped all the way to the docks.

Ah Shui stood at the foot of the stairs with his head down. “Teacher, which vat is the new spirit?”

“There isn’t one.”

Qiusheng turned the notebook to that last page. The first line was still empty. He walked over to the pot. The tear-off calendar was turned to the page for the thirtieth, with the day’s tides printed at the bottom.

---

On Tuesday at half past five he went up the stairs.

Su Qi was in the rattan chair, nothing on her knee. The suitcase was by the door, tied with the hemp rope. On the table the uniform, the needle stuck in the cuff. Xiaoman’s door was shut, a line of light under it.

He stood in the doorway. He did not go to change his clothes.

“Qi,” he said, “before you go, come down and see it once.”

Su Qi looked at him. He thought she would refuse.

She stood up and took her coat. She went to Xiaoman’s door and said through it, “Supper’s in the pan. Lock the door.” There was an answer from inside. She felt in the cloth bundle by the door, took something out and put it in her coat pocket. Xiaoman’s door opened a crack.

“Mama?”

“I’m going out with your father for a while.”

The crack closed. She went down the stairs.

---

The Futai cellar was very quiet at night. The big stove of the soy works above had gone out, and the smell of the sauce vats seeped down through the cracks in the floorboards. The truck had gone out at seven, and Old Qi was riding with it. Fourteen stone steps, three lamps.

Halfway down Su Qi stopped, her hand on the wall.

“Soy sauce,” she said.

“They make soy sauce above.”

She stood on the last step and did not come down. She looked at the row of fermenting vats, the pots, the crocks, the temperatures and dates chalked all over the wall. On the workbench the notebook lay open, every page in his hand. At the foot of the wall at the very back of the cellar, three bricks lay flat on the earth.

“So this is where you are every night,” she said.

“This is it.”

“Ah Yue?”

“Out with the truck.”

She came down the last step. She did not go to the workbench. She went first to the foot of the wall by the stairs. Chalk, line upon line, written from one end of the wall to the other, and the first line was 12 October, eighteen degrees. Her finger went down the dates, not touching the writing. October. November. The finger stopped at one line: 27 November, and after it a temperature. She looked at it a moment.

“Thursday,” she said.

The finger came away. Further down, in a corner of that wall, two separate lines had been started: one was Wen, and under it a thousand; one was union, and under it two hundred, and under the two hundred one word. She looked at the word.

“Owed,” she said. “Owed to whom?”

“The docks.”

She walked to the workbench.

Qiusheng lifted three small jars down from the shelf and set them in a row under the lamp: rice wine, sorghum, rum. He poured a little of each into small glasses and pushed them in front of her.

“I want to put them together,” he said. “But I have been blending for a month, and it is always a little off. Too much rice and there is no backbone. Too much sorghum and it sits on the rice. Too much rum and it is too sweet, like foreigners’ liquor.”

Su Qi did not ask why he had come to her. She set the three glasses in a line, the way she set out three swatches of cloth at the stall. She picked up the first, held it under the lamp, tilted it, put it down, and took the next. Then she smelled them one by one, and tasted them one by one. She had altered other people’s clothes for twenty years. Sleeve length, shoulder width, waist: half an inch out, and when the customer put it on it was simply wrong.

“Bring the measuring glass,” she said.

Qiusheng fetched the measuring glass from the far end of the workbench, the lines of its scale drawn in again. He handed it to her, then took a clean glass and set it beside the measuring glass. The row of vats made its small sound.

She poured rice wine, seven parts; sorghum, two; the rum she picked up and put down again, and in the end poured only a very little, less than one.

“The rum is the lining,” she said. “You can’t see it, but leave it out and the whole garment won’t hang right.”

She poured what was in the measuring glass into the clean glass and pushed it across.

Qiusheng took the glass and held it up under the lamp. It was so clear it could hardly be seen, and fine beads clung to the inside of the glass. He drank a small mouthful and shut his eyes. The sweetness of the rice came first, the backbone of the sorghum held it up in the middle, and last, at the root of the tongue, it left a taste of caramel, very faint, very warm.

He opened his eyes. Su Qi was watching him.

“Is that it?” she asked.

Qiusheng did not answer. He picked up the pen, opened the notebook and, at the very top of that page, stroke by stroke, wrote two words: White Dew. Then beside them, in smaller writing, he wrote her name.

“It has to rest,” he said, his voice a little hoarse. “The three spirits have to live together for a time before they become one. It must rest until Frost’s Descent to be the best it can be.”

Su Qi put out a hand and turned the notebook a little straighter. At the top of the page two words, and beside them a name; below, three lines, three spirits. She turned back a page. Rice wine, rice wine, rice wine. She turned back another, and it was the same. She turned the notebook forward again to the page with her name on it, and laid her hand on the edge of the page.

“Qiusheng,” she said, “this is the best thing you have made in your life.”

She paused.

“I’m still going.”

“I know.”

She turned to the three small jars. “This one. How many bottles?”

“It won’t be bottled,” said Qiusheng. “One crock.”

Qiusheng lifted an empty crock down from the shelf, rinsed it three times, stood it upside down, then set it upright again. The rice wine he poured by the measure, and Su Qi counted beside him, very low: one, two, three, up to seven. Sorghum, two measures. The rum. He picked up the jar and glanced at Su Qi. She put out a hand and pressed the jar a little lower. What came out was less than one measure. He smelled the mouth of the crock and nodded. The mouth was sealed with cloth, the cloth sealed with clay, the clay smoothed flat with the palm.

Su Qi took the tailor’s chalk from her coat pocket, three-cornered, its edges worn round. She crouched by the crock, and the chalk drew on its belly: first a straight stroke, short, with a small ring at the top; then a line drawn out through the ring, curving, running round to the other side of the crock.

A needle, with a thread through its eye.

She put the chalk back in her pocket, stood up and dusted her hands on her coat.

It was midnight when the chain sounded. Ah Yue and Ah Chang came down from the iron door at the goods-yard end, the fog wet on the soles of their shoes, Old Qi behind them. Halfway down Ah Yue saw someone standing under the lamp, and stopped.

“Ma’am.”

“Ah Yue.”

He came down to the bottom. Ah Chang stayed on the stairs and pushed the watch on his wrist up into his sleeve. Old Qi went past them, glanced once under the lamp, made no sound, and sat down again on the vat in the corner, his tea bowl on the floor at his left hand. Ah Yue glanced at the crock. A white line on its belly. The pick was leaning against the wall by Qiusheng’s hand. Ah Yue went over and took it.

“Where?”

Qiusheng pointed to the wall at the very back. Beside the three bricks.

Ah Yue and Ah Chang prised up bricks and dug the hole. The earth was yellow, and the pick went in stroke after stroke. Qiusheng squatted beside them, watching the depth of the hole. Su Qi stood by the workbench, her hand on its edge, looking at the three bricks. Old Qi was in the corner. He did not lift his tea bowl. When the hole was dug the crock was set down in it, the white line uppermost.

“Teacher, when does this crock get opened?” Ah Yue asked.

“Frost’s Descent.”

“Frost’s Descent is past.”

“Next year’s.”

The earth was filled back, shovel by shovel, and trodden flat. Ah Yue fetched a brick from the foot of the wall, laid it beside the three bricks and patted it once with his palm, very lightly. His hand rested on the brick before he took it away.

Four bricks, lying flat on the earth.

Su Qi looked at the four bricks. She did not ask whose the other three were.

Towards dawn Ah Yue drove Su Qi in the truck to the foot of South Slope. Before she got in she looked back once at the iron door. Qiusheng stood inside the door. He did not get in.

That night he did not go back to South Slope.

---

Ah Yue came back at daylight. He came down to the foot of the stairs first and hung the key back on its nail.

“Teacher’s wife said one thing when she got down.”

“What?”

“She told me to drive slower.”

Qiusheng picked up the chalk and wrote today’s date on the wall. 31 December. He wrote the temperature after it, slower than usual.

In the afternoon the hundred bottles went to the goods yard, still the stuff the docks drank. The floor manager counted ten crates, made a stroke in his book and said nothing. Ah Yue came back and slapped the money down on the lid of the pot, two hundred dollars, not a note short.

That night the fire in the cellar was lit as usual. At twelve a whistle came from the goods-yard end, a long one, then a second, a third, the ships in the harbour sounding one after another, one on top of another, drowning the sound of the pot. Old Qi sat in the corner, his tea bowl halfway up.

“Not a train,” he said. “Ships. Twelve o’clock.”

The whistles went on for a minute and stopped. Ah Yue squatted in front of the stove door and did not turn round.

“Teacher, what time’s the ferry tomorrow?”

“The first crossing.”

“I’ll run them to the ferry landing—”

“No need.”

Ah Yue closed the stove door by a hair. Qiusheng opened the notebook and wrote down this vat’s figures, and before the reading two words: rice wine. He turned to the last page. It was that page. White Dew, and beside it a name. He closed the notebook and put it under the thermometer case.

---

Early on the morning of New Year’s Day, Su Qi left and took Xiaoman with her.

It was before daylight when Qiusheng got back to South Slope. The door stood open a crack, and every lamp in the flat was lit. Su Qi was in her outdoor coat, the suitcase in her hand, tied with the hemp rope. Xiaoman had her satchel on her shoulder and was hugging the dictionary Qiao Zheng had given her. The uniform on the table neither of them had touched. Beside the uniform lay a key, the door key. The cloth was piled in the corner. Su Qi was not taking it. The sewing box was not on the table.

Qiusheng put out his hand for the suitcase. Su Qi let him take it.

Downstairs Mrs Chen’s door opened a crack and shut again. Three people’s steps on the stairs, one heavy, two light. There was no one on the slope, and the flagstones were wet. The first bus left the roundabout at six, and the conductor asked through a yawn, Saltflat or the Docks.

“The Docks,” said Su Qi.

Three tickets, thirty cents. Xiaoman sat by the window, the dictionary held on her knees. The bus went down the slope and over the level crossing, the barrier up. When it reached the fish-market gate on the docks the sky had only just turned pale.

The ferry landing was beside the fish-market gate. Stone steps went down, and there was the water. The ferry lay at the foot of the steps, a wooden boat, its awning painted green, the ferryman standing in the bows taking in the mooring rope. Five or six people were sitting in it already, with what they had bought at the fish market in their baskets.

“Mr He,” said the ferryman. “Mrs He going home to her family to stay?”

“For a while,” said Su Qi.

“Does Uncle Ah Lu know?”

“Get in the boat,” said Su Qi.

She went down the stone steps first, took the suitcase from Qiusheng’s hand and carried it aboard. At the side of the boat she stopped and looked back.

“Keep your bowl and chopsticks separate. Scald your handkerchiefs in boiling water.”

Qiusheng looked at her.

“I went and asked Dr Lin on Monday,” she said. “He said nothing else.”

She looked at his cuff, looked a moment, and took her eyes away.

Xiaoman had stopped on the stone steps. She was pretending not to know what had happened. Twice on the way she had asked whether there was a cat at Grandmother’s, and neither time had anyone answered her. She took the dictionary from her arms and pushed it into her father’s hands.

“Papa, you have to learn your words at home too.”

Qiusheng caught hold of it. Hard covers, a size bigger than his hand.

Xiaoman looked at her mother in the boat, then at him.

“Does Godfather know?”

The ferryman cast off the rope and gave a shout from the bows: anyone for the boat, be quick.

“Xiaoman,” said Su Qi from the boat.

“Mama, is there a cat at Grandmother’s?”

“Get in the boat.”

Xiaoman went down the stone steps, got into the boat and sat in the stern. The ferryman gave one push with his pole, and the boat left the steps.

As the ferry drew away Xiaoman waved from the stern. Qiusheng waved too, and kept waving until the boat was lost in the fog.

---

He went back to the flat on South Slope. The rooms were very quiet. The cloth from the tailor’s stall was still piled in the corner of the front room, and on the table lay the half-altered uniform, the needle still stuck in the cuff.

He put the dictionary down beside the uniform and sat. The lamps in the flat burned all day, and he did not go to turn them off. Downstairs Mrs Chen’s wireless gave twelve o’clock, played opera, was silent for a while, gave six o’clock, read the news. The sounds from the foot of the slope came up, and went down again. The needle was still stuck in the cuff. He did not pull it out. Only when it was quite dark did he stand up, put on his coat and take the bus to the Futai Soy Works.

Vat No. 4 in the cellar was due to be distilled.
