## How leaders develop

This model describes how skill changes over time. The same growth curve takes a completely different shape depending on the scale you observe it at. Below, from the most distant macro view all the way in to the micro view that matches reality.

### Macro: it looks linear
As long as you keep improving every day, pull the time scale back far enough and the rate of growth looks like a steady rising **straight line** — you accumulate what you put in.

### Zoom in: it's a staircase
Shrink the time scale and look closer, and the growth turns out to be uneven: fast-growth stretches alternate with slow ones. The skill line isn't a smooth slope — it's a **staircase** going up one step at a time.

### Before each step, the curve digs down
Here's the key: a fast-growth stretch rarely appears out of nowhere. It comes from **bringing in a new technique or method** — and before you're fluent with the new thing, your performance actually **drops first**. You're temporarily slower and clumsier than you were. Only after you push through and get fluent do you break past the old ceiling and land on a higher step.

So before every step up, the curve **digs down a corner**: one step back, then two forward.

### Micro: a jagged saw
Back to reality: how well you perform is tightly coupled to **the state you're in right now** — a tool you know well and you look good, a tool you just switched to and you look bad. So at the smallest scale, hugging what actually happens, the growth curve is a **jagged saw**, not a clean line.

## Reflection

### League of Legends
I used to be good at *League of Legends* — I've played since 2010 and still get a few games in. Looking back, the fastest growth happened at exactly the points where I **changed how I played**: at first I played all five roles and was mediocre at all of them; then I specialised in ADC and my rating jumped a long way; then I rode the patch and practised whatever champion was strong at the time, another jump; then I ground one champion until I really knew it, another step — and eventually hit Master.

That maps straight onto the staircase. Every "specialise in a new way of playing" is a step up, and before the jump there's almost always a stretch of being unfamiliar and watching the rating go **down** instead of up.

Master wasn't the end, though. Holding it is harder than getting there. Stop practising for a while and your game sense dulls, your muscle memory fades — and the environment itself keeps moving: fewer new players, the ones who stay are strong, the whole water level rises, and staying near the top gets harder. So up close, skill isn't a clean rising line. It's a saw that moves with your current state and your environment — and it can recede.

### Vannise
The model is simple to describe, but the people inside it rarely notice it — least of all the dip that comes before the growth.

At Vannise we built a storefront and live-stream toolset to replace the old workflow. For a while after the live-stream assistants and the hosts switched over, things kept going wrong: they couldn't find anything, a viewer called out an order and it looked like it hadn't gone through, and support had no idea how to pull an order up. But fluency came with time — assistants listed products fast, bundle promotions became intuitive to configure, buyers found it easier to pull quantities — and every link in the chain moved up a gear.
