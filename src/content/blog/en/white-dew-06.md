*3 October*

Madam Wen’s invitation came on a gilt-edged card, tucked inside a box of lotus-paste mooncakes from the Nanji Teahouse.

The box arrived on 1 October. The Nanji’s waiter brought it to the union door early in the morning and handed it to Ah Yue, saying Madam Wen’s driver had left it at the teahouse before first light, for Mr He by name, and had said not a word more. Ah Yue carried the box to the boat in his arms and did not open it. He set it on the lid of the pot, took it off again and set it on a crate. A red cardboard box, a red string, the knot pulled very tight.

“Teacher. Madam Wen.”

“Mm.”

“A porter on the docks can go his whole life and never get a thing from Madam Wen,” said Ah Yue. “Counsel Liang sent word. Three this afternoon, the Nanji. He says don’t open the box. Bring it.”

Qiusheng glanced at the box and put the thermometer back in the vat.

---

The private room at the Nanji was the same one as last time. The old newspapers on the board partition had yellowed another shade.

Counsel Liang sat in the host’s seat with his back to the window, the mooncake box by his hand. Qiusheng sat opposite, his back to the partition. Ah Yue leaned by the door. The door stood open a crack, and the erhu from downstairs squeezed up through the crack. On the table one pot of tea, three cups. The waiter poured and withdrew.

Counsel Liang spoke first. “Teacher, the box has not been opened?”

“No.”

“Good.” He drew the box in front of him, untied the red string and lifted the lid. Red paper lined the bottom, and four mooncakes lay side by side, their oil already come through into the paper. His fingers went in under the first mooncake, pinched, and drew out a gilt-edged card. He did not look at it. First he pushed the mooncake box towards Ah Yue. “Mr Chen, have one. The Nanji’s lotus paste. The last box left from Mid-Autumn.”

Ah Yue did not move. Counsel Liang took one himself, bit into it and held the bite in his mouth.

The card carried a single line: “The Fog Harbour Chamber of Commerce requests the pleasure of Mr He Qiusheng’s company at the Wen residence, 3 October, at seven in the evening.” Beneath it a red seal had been stamped. The seal was a junk under sail.

Counsel Liang held the card to the light of the window, looked at the front, turned it over to look at the seal, and turned it back. He swallowed the mooncake, drew the pink handkerchief from his pocket and wiped his forehead three times. When he had done, the handkerchief lay spread on his knee. He did not put it away.

“Teacher,” he said, “this is one you can’t refuse. And you can’t go.”

“Meaning what?”

“Madam Wen is the Shipping Queen. Half the ships in Fog Harbour are hers, half the warehouses are hers, a whole street of teahouses is hers. Even the Nanji has to buy from her rice merchants.” Counsel Liang put the card down, and his fountain pen tapped once on the table. “She invites you because she wants White Dew. Stay away, and you have slighted her. In Fog Harbour the people who slight Madam Wen all find in the end that their boats leak, their warehouses catch fire and their business cannot go on.”

“And if I go?”

“Go,” said Counsel Liang with a wry smile, “and you put your life in her hands.”

Ah Yue straightened up from the door. “Teacher, don’t go. She has whatever she wants. The Shipping Queen. What does she want with you?”

“The liquor,” said Counsel Liang. “Mr Chen, what do you suppose Madam Wen’s bookkeeper is for? He takes a copy of every account on the docks. Old Hai, two hundred bottles a week. The Paramount, three hundred. Boss Ding brought a box of egg tarts. Headman Ah Lu of Saltflat, fifty bottles, seven dollars forty paid down and the rest to wait for spring—Teacher, that boat of yours is clearer in Madam Wen’s book than it is in your own.”

Qiusheng lifted his teacup. It was tea sweepings, broken leaf floating on top. He drank a mouthful and put it down.

“The Chamber of Commerce’s card, with her private seal on it.” Counsel Liang touched the junk with the nib of his fountain pen. “The Chamber is the front. The junk is the principal. This box of mooncakes went out of the Nanji, round in a circle and back into the Nanji. Teacher, she has chosen even the road her invitation takes. It comes through me.”

“Why through you?”

“To let me know.” Counsel Liang put the pen down. “And to let me know that she knows.”

Ah Yue reached out and pulled at a corner of newspaper that showed under the mooncake box. The newspaper lined the bottom of the box, folded, and the side that showed was a column of small advertisements. Fog Harbour Daily: the fog lifts this week; the Nanji Teahouse unveils a new lotus-paste mooncake. He tucked the newspaper back.

“If I go, what do I say?” Qiusheng asked.

“Teacher, Madam Wen’s tea you may drink. Do not be the first to speak.” Counsel Liang folded the handkerchief and put it in his pocket. “She asks one thing, you answer one thing. Every word over is capital spent. She names her terms, you listen. You do not nod and you do not shake your head. Come back and tell me, and I will reckon it for you.”

“What percentage do you take?” Ah Yue asked.

“This time, nothing.” Counsel Liang’s gold tooth flashed. “In Madam Wen’s affairs, the man who takes a fee does not live long. That’s the going rate.”

He pushed the card back to Qiusheng. Qiusheng picked it up. The red paste of the junk still had a little oily shine. He put the invitation in his shirt pocket, on top of Counsel Liang’s card.

“What does he wear?” Ah Yue asked.

“A suit.” Counsel Liang looked at the old shirt Qiusheng had on. “You have a suit?”

“One.”

“Have Mrs He alter it.” Counsel Liang stood up. “When Madam Wen looks at a man, she looks first at his cuffs.”

Downstairs the pipa sounded twice, stopped, and was tuned. Ah Yue’s ear turned that way. Counsel Liang went to the door and looked back once at Ah Yue. The gold tooth flashed again. He went downstairs.

---

The suit was dark grey, made the year Qiusheng joined the Office, and in twenty years he had not replaced it. He had worn it three times: the day he joined the Office, the day he married, the day the director’s father was buried. Su Qi dug it out from the very bottom of the wardrobe, shook it twice and hung it over the back of a chair. The smell of camphor filled the room.

The night of 2 October, one lamp in the front room. Xiaoman was at the far end of the table learning her English words, the dictionary open, the one Qiao Zheng had given her, her finger pressed under a line. Qiusheng stood under the lamp with the suit on, his hands hanging at his sides. Su Qi had the tape measure round her neck and three pins in her mouth, and she walked halfway round him.

She measured the shoulders first. The tape ran from the point of the left shoulder to the point of the right, and her fingers pinched the tape at a figure. Then the waist. The tape went round, drew tight, was pinched. She pulled the tape away and looked at it, put it round once more and looked again.

“You’ve lost an inch and a half,” she said. The pins were still in her mouth, and the words came out between her teeth, one at a time.

“The suit is old.”

“Cloth doesn’t lose weight.” She took the tape measure from her neck and laid it over the back of the chair, and felt in her apron pocket for a piece of tailor’s chalk. The chalk was flat, three-cornered, its edges worn round. She pinched up the side seam of the jacket, pinched out an inch and a half of slack, and the chalk went down along the fold, one white line from the armpit to the hem. When the line was drawn she took the pins out of her mouth one by one and pinned them into the white line. Three pins.

“I’ll take the sleeves in a little as well,” she said. “Lift your arm.”

He lifted his arm. She turned the cuff back and looked inside. The cloth was worn thin. The lamplight showed through it.

“The cuffs will hold another year.” She turned it down again and drew a short line at the edge with the chalk.

Xiaoman looked up from the dictionary. “Papa, where are you going?”

“To dinner.”

“To dinner in a suit?”

Su Qi touched the point of the chalk to his back to make him stand straight. She came round to the front and pulled the two sides of the collar even, and her hands stayed on the collar.

“Dinner where?”

“The owner of the trading house is giving a dinner.”

She took her hands away and patted his shoulder, patting off a little dust that could not be seen. “What time will you be back?”

“I don’t know.”

“Take your key,” she said. “I’ll carry the suit to the stall in the morning and alter it. When I bring it home in the evening it will be ready to put on.”

She helped him out of the jacket, careful of the pins, folded it and put it in the cloth bundle by the door. The chalk went back into her apron pocket. Xiaoman bent her head and went on with her words. She read one out, stopped, and read it again. Qiusheng stood under the lamp in his shirt, his hands still hanging at his sides.

“Papa, *invitation*.”

“A card that asks you to come,” he said.

Xiaoman wrote two characters in the margin of the dictionary. Su Qi turned the lamp down a notch.

---

The delivery tricycle got as far as the foot of the Upper Town hill and would go no further. Ah Yue got down and pushed. After a dozen paces he leaned it against the wall at the mouth of an alley, passed the chain through the wheel and locked it.

“On this hill I get down and push even with a load of coal briquettes,” he said.

The two of them walked up. On both sides of the hill road were Western-style houses behind iron railings. Behind the railings, at every other house, a dog barked once, and then nothing. Ah Yue walked on the outside. He glanced at Qiusheng’s cuff and did not touch it.

“Teacher, the cuffs have been altered.”

“Mm.”

The Wen residence stood halfway up the hill in Upper Town, a white three-storey house in the Western style, with two doormen in long gowns at the gate. Qiusheng held out the invitation. One doorman took it, read it and gave it back; the other put out an arm across Ah Yue’s chest.

“The card says one,” said the doorman.

“I’m his driver.”

“Drivers wait outside.” The doorman glanced down the hill and said no more. Ah Yue stepped back under the eaves.

The fog had climbed all the way up from the docks, and by the time it reached the hillside it was fine rain. A doorman opened a black umbrella and saw Qiusheng from the gate to the foot of the steps of the house. At the top of the steps was another door, and inside the door a staircase laid with red carpet. The doorman closed the umbrella and held it point down. Water dripped on the carpet. He came as far as the foot of the stairs and went no higher.

“Madam Wen is in the study on the first floor,” he said. “Go up. First door on the left.”

Eighteen stairs. Qiusheng went up them one at a time. In the first-floor corridor the first door on the left stood open, and the lamplight came out of it and lay on the floorboards. He reached the doorway, and someone inside spoke first.

“Come in, Mr He.”

The speaker was a man of fifty or so, thin, gold-rimmed spectacles, a long gown, standing at the side of the desk with a book held to his chest, its corners bound in brass. He did not move from the desk.

Madam Wen sat behind the desk. She was a small, thin woman, her hair combed without a strand out of place, in a qipao of dark green, a string of jade at her wrist. She did not rise. She only motioned him to a seat, and poured him a cup of tea with her own hand.

The study was large. One wall was books, one wall was windows, and outside the windows was fog. On the desk a lamp with a green shade, a pot of tea, two cups, a map rolled up with a piece of jade holding down one end. In the corner a small table, a black telephone. Qiusheng sat. The chair was soft, and a man who sat in it was a few inches lower. The desk top came level with his chest.

Madam Wen pushed the cup of tea in front of him. The tea was hot and the cup was thin, and the heat came through the side of the cup to his fingers.

“Mr He, I have drunk your liquor,” she said. “No one else in Fog Harbour can make liquor like this.”

“You flatter it, Madam Wen. It is only rice wine.”

Madam Wen gave him a look. A page turned in the bookkeeper’s brass-cornered book.

“Mr He,” she said, her voice very soft, “when my husband was young he carried cargo on the docks. Twelve hours a day, and at night he came home and drank two cups of rice wine. He said those two cups were the only part of the day that was his own. Later he died. Not of the work. Of drinking firewater. That year Fog Harbour did not yet have the Temperance Act, and firewater was not yet called firewater. It was called cheap liquor.”

Qiusheng lifted his tea and did not drink.

The telephone in the corner rang. It had rung once and Madam Wen was already on her feet. She walked over and lifted the receiver.

“Tomorrow.”

She put the receiver back, walked back and sat down. The jade at her wrist turned half a turn.

“I spent thirty years making his docks into my docks.” Madam Wen pushed the teacup another half inch towards him. “In those thirty years I have sold rice, sold coal, sold ships, and sold liquor. The liquor I sold was always dear, because good liquor is hard to make. But your White Dew—” She paused. “Mr He, it is cheap, and it is good. What that means in Fog Harbour, you know better than I do.”

Qiusheng looked at the tea in the cup. The leaves were whole. They stood upright, every one.

“It means you are a threat.” Madam Wen smiled. “Or an opportunity. I have not decided yet.”

She moved the jade paperweight aside and spread the rolled map flat on the desk. The paperweight had been holding down something else as well, a telegram from the provincial capital, and in the half line that showed was the word “vote”. She laid a corner of the map over it. The map was of Fog Harbour. A dozen or more places on the docks had been ringed in red.

“These are my warehouses,” she said. “This one,” and she tapped a fingernail on a ring at the east end of the docks, “is the Futai Soy Works. Three years ago the owner ran off. I bought the works and kept the old foreman to go on making soy sauce. The signboard was not changed. No one has been down into the cellar underneath.”

The fingernail stayed on the ring.

“Ten times the size of that fishing boat of yours. Running water, electricity, ventilation. Soy sauce is made above, and the smell of soy covers any smell of liquor.”

Madam Wen asked, “How much a week on the boat?”

Qiusheng did not answer. The bookkeeper turned a page of the book. “Three hundred bottles or thereabouts, Madam Wen. Three vats and two pots.”

“Three hundred bottles,” said Madam Wen. “Mr He, the men queuing on the docks want more than that in a week.”

Qiusheng looked at the map. The ring was very near the railway of the goods yard, and a thin line ran out of the ring and joined the railway.

He coughed, once. He pressed his handkerchief to his mouth and the palm of his other hand to the right side of his chest. Madam Wen waited for him to finish and did not hurry him.

“The cellar is dry,” she said.

Qiusheng put the handkerchief back in his pocket.

“I will give you Futai,” said Madam Wen. “You make your liquor there, ten times what you make now. I buy everything you make, at twice what the dockworkers pay you. Boss Ding dodging his bill, the Black Flag robbing a delivery: from now on none of that is your concern. And—” She paused. “The Prohibition Bureau.”

“And the conditions?”

“Three conditions.” Madam Wen raised her fingers. “First, quality. Every batch of your liquor must be ninety or above. Ninety-six or above is best. I sell to the Consulate and to the rich of Upper Town, and they can taste the difference. Second, quantity. A thousand bottles every week, and not one fewer. Third—”

She folded her fingers down and looked up at him.

“You sell to no one but me.”

The bookkeeper’s pen moved three times in the book.

Madam Wen lifted the teapot and filled his cup to the brim again. She did not wait for him to speak. When the rich play host, the tea always comes before the talk; by the time the talk comes, the tea is cold.

Madam Wen turned and took a bottle from the bookcase, a square glass bottle, its label printed with English lettering and a stag. She poured a small measure and pushed it in front of him.

“The people at the Consulate like this best,” she said. “Twelve dollars a bottle.”

Qiusheng did not touch the glass. He only bent his head and brought his nose to the rim. Caramel, a great deal of caramel; wood, fresh sawdust, not an oak barrel ten years old; and under it a thread of a sharp sweetness he knew better than anything.

“This is not whisky,” he said.

“I know,” said Madam Wen. “It is spirit, caramel colouring and wood chips. My factory in the South Seas blends it. Half the imported liquor is made this way. Last month a guest at the Consulate drank it and had a headache for three days.” Her hand rested on the neck of the bottle. “Mr He, I want you to make it for me. On your spirit, made like the real thing, and made clean. I will pay three times what White Dew fetches.”

“That’s cheating people.”

“That’s business,” said Madam Wen. “I cheat them of a label. The Black Flag cheat the docks of their lives. Which do you choose?”

Qiusheng did not answer. The small measure of spirit in front of him slowly went still. The caramel colour sank to the bottom, and a layer of water parted from it.

Madam Wen watched the glass separate. She reached out, took the glass and poured it back into the bottle to the last drop, and pushed in the stopper. The bottle went back in the bookcase, the stag facing out.

“One more thing.” Madam Wen turned the map over. On the back were shipping lanes, running south all the way from Fog Harbour. “My sugar ships come back from the South Seas every month, and the molasses left in the bottom of the holds is tipped into the sea, drum after drum. If you want it, there will be ten drums a week at the side gate of Futai.” She smiled. “I hear that Mr Smith at the Consulate made rum on a ship in the Caribbean when he was young.”

The bookkeeper closed the book.

The cup of tea in front of Qiusheng was still, its steam long gone. He looked at it. Madam Wen rolled up the map and set the jade paperweight back on it. The telegram was nowhere to be seen.

“Mr He,” she said, “three days from now, three in the afternoon, Futai. I shall go and see it for myself.”

She pressed the bell at the edge of the desk. Outside the door footsteps came up the stairs. She did not see him out, and she did not pour more tea.

---

It was near midnight when Qiusheng came out. Ah Yue was squatting under the eaves of the doormen’s lodge, his shoulders wet with fog, a packet of cigarettes finished. He saw Qiusheng and stood up, and a knee cracked.

“Well? What does she want?”

The two of them walked down the road along the hillside. Every other street lamp was lit, and in the fog each one was a ball of light. The Western-style houses of Upper Town stood dark, one after another. Only that one window on the first floor of the Wen residence was still lit. Ah Yue’s tricycle was still locked at the mouth of the alley at the foot of the hill. The stretch of hill he had not been able to ride up at dusk they now walked down.

Qiusheng went through Madam Wen’s conditions. He did not mention the bottle of whisky. At the third, Ah Yue’s steps slowed.

“No one but her? What about the union? What about Old Hai? What about Headman Ah Lu?”

“She’ll sell to them.”

“At what price?” Ah Yue stopped. “Teacher, Madam Wen’s imported liquor is twelve dollars a bottle! Can the men on the docks afford that? If White Dew can only go to her, the men on the docks go back to drinking firewater!”

Qiusheng walked a few steps and coughed twice.

“Futai is dry,” he said. After a while: “The hold is damp.”

Ah Yue caught up and walked on the outside. They had passed two street lamps before he spoke.

“Teacher, your illness…”

“It’s nothing.” Qiusheng patted him on the shoulder. “I’ll talk to her. The docks’ share we keep ourselves.”

“Will she agree?”

“She needs me. She said it herself. No one else in Fog Harbour can make liquor like this.”

The words were out, and he heard them himself. Ah Yue heard nothing in them. He only nodded.

At the mouth of the alley at the foot of the hill, a layer of water had gathered in the tricycle’s cargo bed. Ah Yue swept the water out with the flat of his hand. He could not get it dry, and he took off his jacket and spread it there. Qiusheng sat on it. The tricycle went through the Old Town roundabout. The checkpoint table at the temple gate had been taken in, and there was one lamp under the Nanji’s arcade. Beyond the town the water of the salt pans was black, pan after pan going by.

That night they got back to the boat. High tide was at three. The fire was lit under the pot, the spirit came, the fire was drawn, and before daybreak he wrote *rice wine* in the notebook as always, pressing harder than usual. *4 October, tenth vat, rice wine, ninety-one.* He closed the notebook and put it under the thermometer case.

Ah Yue lay on the canvas. He was not asleep.

“Teacher, where is Futai?”

“East end of the docks.”

“The soy works? I’ve delivered coal briquettes there.” Ah Yue turned over. “There’s a dog at the gate. Doesn’t bite. After the owner ran off the workers carried on. I thought it was because they were owed wages and nobody dared leave.”

“Mm.”

“Teacher, a thousand bottles.”

“Mm.”

“A week.”

“Sleep.”

---

The next afternoon Counsel Liang was waiting for him at the Nanji. No private room this time. A small table by the stairs in the main room, one pot of tea, two cups. As he listened Counsel Liang wrote figures on the back of a takings sheet, the nib of his fountain pen moving fast.

“A thousand bottles, three dollars a bottle, three thousand dollars a week.” He stopped the pen. “Teacher, of all the money I have washed for people, not one sum was three thousand a week.”

“Mm.”

“She gives you the cellar. Not to you. To the liquor.” Counsel Liang turned the sheet round for him to see, the figures set out in a column. “The cellar, the water and electricity, the ice truck, the Prohibition Bureau. She takes care of all of it. The more she takes care of, the more you owe.”

His fountain pen tapped twice on the figure six hundred.

“There is one more account. The Nanji washes six hundred a week. That is the limit I gave you. Any more and the bottom burns out of the teapot. Three thousand dollars a week, Teacher. The two thousand four hundred above the six hundred is more than the Nanji can swallow, and no other teahouse in Fog Harbour would dare.” He put the pen down. “For what is over, Madam Wen will find you a way, of course. Any way she finds is her way.”

Qiusheng looked at the figure.

“And the docks’ share?”

Counsel Liang wiped his forehead once with the handkerchief.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset: Madam Wen goes by paper, not by talk,” he said. “When you go, she will bring out a bill. The docks’ share, you write on the paper. If she puts her seal to it, she has agreed; if she does not, you will know what she wants.”

“Will she seal it?”

“I don’t know.” Counsel Liang folded the takings sheet. “I only know that nothing she ever put her seal to has failed to hold. That’s the going rate.”

Qiusheng drank the tea. Tea sweepings, the same as last time.

---

On 6 October it rained all day, a fine rain that fell on the road without a sound.

The Futai Soy Works was at the east end of the docks, past Warehouse No. 9, where the railway threw off a siding, and grass grew on the siding. The works was a yard inside a ring of brick wall, and over the top of the wall showed the bamboo lids of a row of sauce vats. Over the main gate hung an old board with the name Futai on it, half the gold paint flaked away. There was no doorman at the gate. A dog lay by the threshold and did not bark when anyone came.

Qiusheng and Ah Yue arrived at half past two. The tricycle stopped outside the wall and Ah Yue pushed it in. The sauce vats stood in the yard row on row, their mouths covered with bamboo lids, and the rain rustled on the lids. The smell of sauce was heavy, salt, with a layer of sweetness underneath. A middle-aged man in a short jacket came out of the works building, bean pulp on the bottoms of his trousers, asked whether this was Mr He, and said Madam Wen had left word. He said his name was Wu, he had been here twenty years, and when the owner ran off he had not left.

“The cellar’s over there.” The foreman pointed to an iron door behind the works building. “The bookkeeper has the key. I’ve never been down.”

“Has anyone from the works been down?”

“No,” said the foreman. “In the old owner’s time the cellar held empty vats. After he ran off the door was locked. Three years.”

Through the rain a black motor car came along the siding, its wheels crushing the grass, and stopped outside the main gate. The doorman got out first and opened an umbrella. The bookkeeper got out holding the brass-cornered book, wrapped in oilcloth. Madam Wen got out last, all in dark green, the umbrella covering her to the shoulders, her heels striking the flagstones of the yard, one, then the other. She did not look at the sauce vats.

Ah Yue stood by the tricycle. The doorman glanced at him. Madam Wen did not. Ah Yue stayed in the yard.

The lock on the iron door had rusted. The bookkeeper had to try twice before it opened. Behind the door a flight of stone steps went down, fourteen of them. At the head of the steps the foreman felt for a switch and pressed it. It lit up below. Three lamps, and one of them did not light. He stepped back to the door, bowed, and said the steps were steep. Madam Wen did not answer. With one hand on the wall she went down a step at a time. The doorman closed his umbrella at the head of the steps, and water dripped on the stone. The bookkeeper followed Madam Wen, and Qiusheng came last.

The cellar was ten times the size of the hold of the Fair Wind. Brick walls, a brick floor, the roof very high, and in the ceiling square vents fitted with iron pipes that led up to who knew where. Along one wall a row of empty sauce vats, six of them, their mouths filmed with dust. On another wall a tap, with a stone trough beneath it. On the floor two rails, narrow ones, running out at the far end of the cellar through a small iron door. A wooden workbench, a pair of benches. The air was cool and dry. It smelled of earth and of bean pulp. There was no mould.

Madam Wen stood on the first brick at the foot of the steps and went no further. She looked up at the lamp that did not light, then at the row of dust-filmed sauce vats. The jade at her wrist turned half a turn.

“Master Wu, whose vats are these?” she asked, up the steps.

“The old owner’s,” the foreman answered from above. “He didn’t take them.”

“They are mine now,” said Madam Wen. She asked nothing more. The bookkeeper went to the workbench, wiped the top once with his sleeve, undid the oilcloth and laid the brass-cornered book on it, then drew a bill out of the book and spread it flat, a brass paperweight on one corner and a small square seal box on another.

Qiusheng did not go to look at the bill. He walked along the wall. He laid his palm on the brick. Cool. Dry. He took it away, and there was nothing on his palm. He came to the sauce vats and rapped his knuckles round the wall of one. The sound was dull. There was water in the bottom. He came to the tap and turned it on. The water ran yellow at first, then clear. He caught a little in his palm, tasted it and spat it into the trough. Chlorine. He turned the water off. He came to the rails, squatted and felt along a rail, and felt a coat of oil. He looked up at the vents. Air was coming down out of them, cool. He walked the long side of the cellar to the end, counting his paces. Thirty-two paces. The width, fifteen.

Madam Wen stood where she was and watched him walk. The bookkeeper waited.

Qiusheng walked back to the workbench.

“The water won’t do,” he said.

The bookkeeper pushed up his spectacles. “The running water comes off the town hall’s mains, Mr He—”

“There’s chlorine in it,” said Qiusheng. “We cart water in.”

Madam Wen said nothing at all. The bookkeeper made a note in the book.

“Mr He, the bill,” said the bookkeeper, one word at a time, with no rise or fall. “One: every batch of spirit to have an assay reading of ninety or above, and to be assayed again bottle by bottle by Wen & Co.’s men. Two: one thousand bottles a week, to be collected once a week by Wen & Co.’s ice truck at the Futai side gate. Three: the entire output to go to Wen & Co., three dollars a bottle, cash, settled monthly. Four: the Futai cellar to be at Mr He’s disposal, water and electricity to be paid for by the works, the works’ men not to go down into the cellar. Five: whatever other apparatus Mr He requires, he is to write me a list.”

He laid a fountain pen beside the bill.

Qiusheng did not take that pen. He drew his own from his shirt pocket, the one the Office had issued, its barrel worn pale. He pulled off the cap and wrote one line in the blank margin on the left of the bill. He wrote small, stroke by stroke.

Two hundred bottles a week go to the docks, at the union’s price.

The bookkeeper leaned over to read. When he had read it he straightened and turned his face to Madam Wen.

“Madam Wen, what the bill says is the entire output,” he said. “Two hundred bottles at the union’s price, a dollar fifty a bottle, is three hundred dollars short a week, and in a year—”

Madam Wen did not look at the bookkeeper. She walked over, stood at the workbench and looked down at the line. She read it once. The jade at her wrist was still.

She put out a hand and opened the seal box. The paste was red. She took up the seal, pressed it once on the paste, then pressed it beside the line of small writing. The seal was a junk under sail, the same as on the invitation.

“Mr He, I respect a man of principle. Principles are expensive.”

The bookkeeper’s mouth closed.

Madam Wen put the seal back in the box and held out a hand towards the head of the steps. The doorman came down the steps, walked to the workbench, took something from inside his long gown, wrapped in cloth, and handed it over. She undid the cloth. It was a square glass bottle with the stag label, and the liquor in it was caramel-coloured. She set the bottle on the bill, squarely on the line of small writing and the junk-sail seal.

“The end of next month,” she said.

Qiusheng’s hand stopped over the bill. The nib was half an inch from the paper. He looked at the stag. Under the stag was the junk, and under the junk was his own writing. He put the cap back on the fountain pen and put it away in his shirt pocket.

He did not say no.

Madam Wen had already turned towards the steps. The bookkeeper drew the bill out from under the bottle, blew on the seal paste, folded it in three and handed it to Qiusheng; a second copy, from the book, he put away himself in the oilcloth. The square bottle stayed on the workbench.

At the top of the steps the rain had not stopped. The foreman was waiting at the head of the steps with a wooden sign in his hand, a new one, the ink still black. On the sign was one line: PICKLING · NO ADMITTANCE.

“The bookkeeper wrote it yesterday,” said the foreman. “Told me to hang it here.”

The bookkeeper took the sign and hung it himself on the nail beside the iron door, and set it straight. The doorman opened the umbrella. Madam Wen walked into the rain, past the sauce vats, to the main gate, and stopped.

Outside the main gate, beside the motor car, stood an old man. Sixty-odd, not tall, a little stooped, in a railway uniform washed nearly white, with no umbrella, the rain dripping from the peak of his cap. He did not look at the people in the yard. His eyes were on the railway at the far end of the siding.

Madam Wen pointed at him with her chin.

“From now on Mr Qi goes with your truck,” she said.

Ah Yue stepped out from beside the tricycle. “We haven’t got a truck.”

“The truck I will see to,” said the bookkeeper.

Madam Wen got into the car. The doorman closed the umbrella and got in. The bookkeeper got in last, the oilcloth parcel held to his chest. The car backed out along the siding. The old man did not get in. He stood where he was and watched the car go, looked once more at the main gate, then turned and walked along the railway towards the docks, not fast, and in the rain only his back could be seen.

Qiusheng went back down to the cellar and picked up the square bottle from the workbench. The bottle was cool and weighed a catty. He put it in the outside pocket of his jacket. The bill was in the inside pocket. There was a key on the workbench too, left by the bookkeeper, and Qiusheng put it in his trouser pocket. Before he went up the steps he looked up once at the lamp that did not light. At the head of the steps he pressed the switch, and the three went out together. He pulled the iron door to and hung the lock back on its ring.

The foreman stood in the doorway of the works building and watched him lock the door. “Mr He, what time tomorrow?”

“We’ll see.”

---

On the way back Ah Yue pushed the tricycle and Qiusheng walked beside it. The rain had eased. The gravel of the railway was wet and crunched at every step. At its end the siding joined the main line. The barrier of the level crossing at the goods yard was up, the red lamp unlit. Over at the docks the cranes had stopped, their chains hanging, and the rain ran down the chains.

Ah Yue pushed for a stretch without speaking. Only at the corner of Warehouse No. 7 did he speak.

“She agreed?”

“Three dollars a bottle.”

Ah Yue’s hands tightened once on the handlebars. “Three dollars. Old Hai a dollar fifty, her three.” He walked a few steps. “A thousand bottles, three thousand dollars, a week. Teacher. A week.”

“Mm.”

“And the docks?”

“Two hundred bottles.”

“Two hundred bottles?” Ah Yue stopped the tricycle. “She’s willing?”

“It’s written on the bill.”

Ah Yue looked at him. Rain dripped from the peak of Ah Yue’s cap. He wiped his face with the back of his hand, then wiped the hand on his trousers. He pushed the tricycle on, went a dozen paces and stopped again.

“Then what about the thousand bottles she wants?”

Qiusheng put his hand into the inside pocket and found the bill folded in three. He took it out, folded it once more and put it back. He did not answer.

The pocket on the other side hung heavy. He stepped forward, and in the pocket the square bottle knocked once against his ribs.
