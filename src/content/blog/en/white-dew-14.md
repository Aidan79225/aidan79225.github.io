*March · After the Spring Equinox*

Old Qi went first.

He did not walk on the gravel. He walked on the sleepers. One to a step, not fast, a stride thirty years old, and in the fog nothing to be seen but the two rails underfoot. Qiusheng walked on the gravel beside him, and every step sounded. Halfway there he began to cough, and stopped, his handkerchief pressed to his mouth. Old Qi stopped too, his back to him. He did not turn round. He waited until the coughing was over and only then walked on. They passed the open space where the Lu crew had their iron pot. A little red was left in the cinder fire, and a man lay on the sleepers under a sack and did not move. From the goods yard came a whistle, a long way off, drawn out over two beats. Old Qi did not raise his head.

“Eleven-ten, down,” he said. “Late.”

Further on, the railway threw off the Futai siding, and the grass on the siding was wet. Old Qi stopped at the mouth of the siding and looked west. The red lamp of the goods yard was no more than a point in the fog. He stepped down off the sleepers.

The side gate was not locked. The truck stood outside it, its bed empty. The wooden sign by the iron door had a film of fog on it, and the writing was still black. Old Qi went in first and Qiusheng followed. Fourteen steps. A press of the switch at the head of the steps, three lamps.

There was no one in the cellar. The fire was banked, a line of red in the crack of the stove door. By the stone trough a row of bottles stood upside down, washed by Ah Shui, and the water on the board had not dried. The truck had gone out at seven, by way of the goods yard, and come back at half past nine, and Ah Yue and Ah Chang had gone home when the delivery was done.

Old Qi did not sit on his upturned vat in the corner. He went to one end of the workbench and sat down on the bench, his back to the brick wall, his face to the stairs. His tea bowl was on the floor in the corner. He got up, fetched it and set it at his left hand. It was still half full, and cold. He glanced at the iron door at the head of the stairs, and only then took the little book from the inside pocket of his uniform and laid it on the workbench.

Cloth-covered, the size of a palm, the corners worn round, a grease stain on the cover. He opened it at the first page, put a finger on the first line and pushed the book across.

The writing was the bookkeeper’s, thin, no stroke joined to the next. A name to a line, and after the name a place and a sum. The first line: No. 3 Wharf, the ship’s master, held; wife, Old Town, eight dollars. Qiao Zheng had spoken of this man at table at Mid-Autumn: an order from a chemist’s in the provincial capital, the order real, the wine false. The second line was the undertaker’s case, the man with the twelve bottles in the coffin: Upper Town, five dollars. Ten lines to a page, and after every line small ticks, in pencil, one a month.

“These men keep their mouths shut inside. Their families need money. It’s a cost of doing business.”

Having said it, he stopped a moment.

“I’ve delivered it ten years. One round a month.”

Qiusheng’s finger went back to the row of ticks. He turned the pages. Page after page, he did not know one of the names, and he knew every one of the places: the edge of the Old Town roundabout, the head of the sea wall at Saltflat, behind No. 3 Wharf. After some lines the ticks broke off, broke off at a certain month, and below a new line began, the same place, the sum cut by half.

“The door opens a crack,” said Old Qi. “The money goes in, the door shuts. Ten years, and not one household has asked me who I am.”

Qiusheng turned back to the first page, and through to the end again. There was not one Black Flag name.

“The Black Flag’s men aren’t in it,” said Old Qi. “When her people go inside, she keeps them.”

Qiusheng turned to the last page with writing on it. This page was only half written, and blank below. The last line was new, the ink blacker than the rest.

Qiao Zheng.

After the name there was no place and no sum. Only one word, Transfer, and a line drawn through it, drawn straight. After the line, nothing. No tick.

Qiusheng’s finger stopped on that line.

Old Qi drew the book out from under his hand, shut it, and pressed his palm on the cover.

“I don’t kill people.”

The vats made their small sound.

“She wants Qiao Zheng out of the way,” said Old Qi. “If he can’t be transferred, they lay hands on him. Before the tenth of April.”

His palm was still pressed on the cover.

“Two men. Down from the provincial capital. They don’t live in Fog Harbour. They’re at a hotel in Upper Town. Been here three days.”

“When?”

“Her people don’t keep to train times.”

The line of red in the crack of the stove door dimmed a little. Qiusheng pressed his palm to the right side of his chest, held it there, and took it away. He looked at Old Qi. Old Qi put the book back in the inside pocket of his uniform and did up the button.

“The book goes back at daylight,” he said. “The bookkeeper asked me which road Inspector Qiao walks at night. I said I didn’t know.”

He lifted the tea bowl, took a mouthful of the cold tea and set it back at his left hand.

Qiusheng stood up and went to the foot of the wall by the stairs. The chalk lay at the end of the row of temperatures, and today’s line had not been written. He picked up the chalk and did not write the temperature. Below the row he began a new line and wrote a date: 10 April. Having written it he stepped back half a pace, looked at it, and went over it once more.

Old Qi looked at the date.

“Sixteen days,” he said. “A Friday.”

He glanced at the iron door.

“The bookkeeper says that day I’m at the gate of the cold store.”

The chalk was still in Qiusheng’s hand.

“Teacher, on the tenth of April, where do you want me?”

---

Old Qi left at one. At the foot of the stairs he looked back at the date on the wall and went up, and the iron door sounded once. Qiusheng did not go up. He sat down at the workbench and took the tin box down from the shelf. From under the reports he drew a stack of the old forms and laid them with the ruled boxes down and the backs up.

He wrote. How many hours the rice soaks, until a grain breaks under the fingernail. Steam the rice, spread it until the back of the hand laid on it does not burn. How to choose the yeast, an even colour is not always clean, break it open and smell. Fill the vat, twelve days, not in summer, wait for the wind. Light the fire two hours before high tide, draw it before the tide goes out. Sixty-four and a half degrees, put it down when it stops. Past seventy-eight, cut. Sorghum is not soaked, steam it through and mix in the daqu, bury it in the pits, the first two will sour, the third is slow. Molasses one part, water three, a handful of dregs, seven days, on the second night the vat will make a noise. He wrote it down item by item, in a small hand, stroke by stroke, the hand he filled in reports with. At the ninth sheet there was a sound outside the vent windows, bamboo rakes scraping the sides of the vats, the men in the sauce-vat yard lifting the bamboo lids. At the eleventh sheet it was grey outside the vent windows. At the last sheet he stopped a while, the nib half an inch from the paper. He wrote three spirits, three figures, and under them a line in small writing.

When he had done he read it through from the beginning. In the box was a length of hemp thread, left from the year it had held label paper. With a nail he pricked three holes in the corner of the eleven sheets, passed the hemp thread through, the way the counterfoils were threaded at the Office, and knotted it.

One more sheet, by itself. On it he wrote only one line: White Dew: made over to Wen & Co. Under it his signature and the date, 26 March.

The notebook was in the inside pocket of his coat. He did not take it out.

---

On Thursday the ice truck came as usual.

It backed in through the side gate, its tail to the iron door. Sawdust, crushed ice. The driver said through the window that it was Madam Wen’s orders, every bottle assayed. Qiusheng stood on the stairs inside the iron door and read out the figures, and the driver nodded. Ah Yue lifted crates at the tail of the truck, one step heavy and one light, his right foot not yet mended; Ah Shui steadied the corner of each crate with his left hand.

The crates were buried in the ice. Qiusheng stepped out through the iron door, walked to the cab window and passed in a folded sheet of paper. The driver took it, opened it, read it, folded it again and put it in his breast pocket.

“Eight o’clock tonight,” said Qiusheng.

The driver did not answer. He drove off. Ah Yue stood where the tail of the truck had been, looking at the line of water on the flagstones, and wiped a hand down his trousers.

Ah Yue went back down to the cellar and stood a moment at the foot of the wall by the stairs. He looked at the date that had been added under the row of chalk, and then at the Teacher by the workbench. He did not ask.

At six the fire was lit. Qiusheng put the thermometer into the vat and nodded once to Ah Yue, and Ah Yue went and crouched at the stove door. Old Qi was riding with the truck tonight, and was at the goods yard.

At seven Qiusheng went up to South Slope.

Nothing in the room had been moved. On the table the half-altered uniform, the needle still stuck in the cuff; beside the uniform a key and a dictionary, and on the dictionary a layer of dust. The door of Xiaoman’s room was shut. He did not open it. On the chair by the door was the cloth bundle, the blue one Su Qi used for carrying clothes to the stall, empty these three months, folded. He shook it out, and the dust fell.

He stepped up on the square stool. At the back of the top shelf of the wardrobe, three tin boxes, all of a size, one against another. He lifted them down in three trips and put them in the cloth bundle, each heavier than the last. He tied the mouth of the bundle with its tape, twice round. The eleven sheets threaded with hemp and the other sheet went into the inside pocket of his coat, against the notebook.

He lifted the bundle, with both hands. He pulled the door to. The key was in his pocket. He did not take it out.

At the foot of the stairs Mrs Chen’s door was open a crack, and inside the wireless was playing opera.

“You’re early tonight, Mr He.”

He nodded once and went off down the slope.

---

The Upper Town hill took him half an hour.

Down South Slope, past the roundabout; under the Old Town arcades the shops had put up their shutter boards one after another, and there was light inside the Nanji’s door, the half signboard hanging under the eaves with the light behind it, not a character to be made out. Past the Tin Hau Temple square was the Upper Town hill. At the corner of the ring road the town hall was dark, not one lamp in the row of windows on the first floor. On both sides of the hill road were Western-style houses behind iron railings, and the dogs inside the railings barked once at every other house. He shifted the bundle to his shoulder, and the cloth tape cut into the flesh. At the third house he began to cough. He put the bundle down on the stone steps at someone’s gate, pressed his palm to the right side of his chest, coughed it out, folded his handkerchief and took the bundle on his shoulder again. A dog barked once more.

The fog climbed up from the docks, and by the time it reached the hillside it was fine rain. At the gate of the Wen residence stood two doormen in long gowns, one carrying a storm lantern. The bundle was on Qiusheng’s shoulder, and neither of them put out a hand to take it.

“Mr He, Madam Wen is waiting.”

The storm lantern saw him from the gate to the foot of the steps, and the shrubs on either side of the flagged path dripped. Up the steps, and the clock inside the door struck eight. The doorman saw him to the foot of the stairs and went no higher. Red carpet, eighteen stairs. In the first-floor corridor the first door on the left stood open, and the lamplight lay on the floorboards.

The study was as it had been in October. The lamp with the green shade, a pot of tea, two cups, a rolled map with the jade paperweight holding down one end. The black telephone in the corner. The bookkeeper stood at the side of the desk with the book held to his chest, its corners bound in brass. Another doorman stood by the door; when Qiusheng went in he withdrew outside it, and the door was not shut. Madam Wen sat behind the desk, in a qipao of dark green, the jade at her wrist. She did not rise. Her eyes rested a moment on the bundle on his shoulder, then went to his cuffs, and only then did she lift the teapot and pour him a cup.

“Mr He, sit down.”

Qiusheng did not sit. He put the bundle on a corner of the desk and undid the tape, both turns. The cloth folded back: three tin boxes. He lifted the lids off one by one and laid each lid upside down beside its box. In all three boxes were wads tied with rubber bands, wad on wad. In two of the boxes most of the notes were old and few were new, and one wad still smelled of fish; the third was all new notes, and the wads did not yet reach the rim.

A page turned in the bookkeeper’s book. His hand went out towards the first box, and his fingertips touched a rubber band. Madam Wen did not look at him. The hand stopped a moment, drew back, and returned to the book.

From the inside pocket of his coat Qiusheng took the sheets threaded with hemp and laid them on top of the middle box. The bookkeeper reached out, picked them up and turned two pages. Three holes in the corner, a length of hemp thread. He put them back. Qiusheng took out the single sheet and laid it on the very top, writing uppermost.

“It’s all here,” he said. “On the tenth of April I go into the cold store.”

Madam Wen looked at that sheet of paper. She did not touch the money, and she did not touch the threaded sheets. She put out one finger and turned the top sheet half round, towards the bookkeeper.

The bookkeeper bent his head and looked. “White Dew: made over to Wen & Co.,” he read, in a voice with no rise or fall. “He Qiusheng, 26 March.”

Madam Wen’s eyes lifted from the paper and came to rest on Qiusheng’s face.

“Spare Qiao Zheng,” said Qiusheng.

In the study there was only the ticking of the clock outside the door.

“Inspector Qiao,” said Madam Wen, “is a man you trained, Mr He.”

The bookkeeper’s pen stopped over the book and did not come down. He glanced at Madam Wen. Madam Wen did not look at him. There were footsteps in the corridor outside the door. They passed. Qiusheng was still standing.

Madam Wen lifted the teapot and filled his cup to the brim again. She did not wait for him to speak.

“I look after my investments.”

Qiusheng looked at the cup of tea. The steam went straight up from the rim. He lifted the cup and drank a mouthful. It was good tea, the leaves whole. The cup in October he had not drunk. He put the cup back.

The bookkeeper’s pen came down and wrote three lines.

“The cup Mr Wen drank years ago,” said Qiusheng. “Which house sold it?”

For a long time Madam Wen did not answer. The light of the desk lamp fell on her hand. The jade turned half round on her wrist and stopped. She was looking at the three open boxes, not at what was in them, at the boxes. The bookkeeper’s pen hung in the air.

“Mr He,” she said, “your spirit bears the name He. Once it’s in the cold store, it bears the name Wen.”

She pressed the bell at the edge of the desk. The doorman came in and stood by the door.

The bookkeeper put down the book and came to the corner of the desk. First he picked up the sheets threaded with hemp and slipped them inside the book. Then he put the lids back on the three boxes, and put them on very square. The single sheet he picked up, and glanced at Madam Wen again. Madam Wen’s hand was on the handle of the teapot. He put the sheet under the lid of the first box, put the three boxes back in the bundle, folded the cloth over and tied the tape twice round, the way Qiusheng had tied it when he came.

“We shall settle after the tenth of April,” said Madam Wen.

She poured no more tea.

The bookkeeper went ahead, carrying the bundle in his arms. Eighteen stairs. At the foot of the steps the doorman stood with the storm lantern. The bookkeeper gave the bundle into Qiusheng’s hands, with both of his own. The weight was no less.

“Mr He, the hill is slippery,” said the bookkeeper.

The doorman held the storm lantern high and lit him down the steps. Qiusheng carried the bundle down the hill. Outside the gate he looked back. The window on the first floor was still lit, and on the window was a shadow that did not move. The dogs barked once at every other house. At the foot of the hill the fine rain was fog again. Past the Tin Hau Temple square, past the Old Town arcades, and the street lamps of the South Slope roundabout hung each in its ball of light. He sat for a while on a stone bench at the edge of the roundabout with the bundle on his knees. A patrol car went once round. A face in its window gave him a look, and the car drove off. He stood up and went up the slope.

---

As it fell out, the door on South Slope was not locked.

There was no one on the slope, and the flagstones were wet. Mrs Chen’s wireless downstairs had stopped. He climbed to the second floor, put the bundle down at his feet and got out the key. Before the key had touched the lock, the door swung inwards a crack by itself. He stood still. The stairwell lamp was on the half-landing below, and by the second floor only a little yellow was left of it. That little yellow went in at the crack and fell on the floorboards by the door.

On the floorboards, a pair of leather shoes. Side by side, toes pointing out, the uppers polished to a shine.

Qiusheng pushed the door open. The room was dark. The balcony door was shut, and outside the glass were the lamps at the foot of the slope, a few points of yellow. The table was in the middle of the room, and at the far end of the table was the rattan chair. In the rattan chair sat a man, not moving.

Qiusheng picked up the bundle, walked in and put it on the table. The tin boxes knocked once inside the cloth.

“Zheng,” he said, “you’re safe now.”

He reached for the switch of the lamp on the table.

“In the verdict column, you always wrote ‘Drinkable’.”

Qiusheng’s hand stopped a moment, went on, and turned the lamp on.

Qiao Zheng sat in the rattan chair. The khaki uniform, the badge at his collar. He was not wearing his coat. It hung on the hook behind the door, where Qiusheng’s own coat usually hung. His right hand lay on the arm of the rattan chair, and in it was a sheet of paper that had been folded, the folds pressed very flat. His left hand lay on his knee. On the table, in front of the square stool where Qiusheng usually sat, a cup of tea, full, gone cold, a tide mark dried round the rim. Qiao Zheng’s own cup was on the corner of the table by the arm of the rattan chair, half drunk. The teapot was the family’s own. The kitchen door was open, the stove door not quite shut, a line of red.

On the table too was the uniform, the needle stuck in the cuff; a key; a dictionary. Qiao Zheng’s cup of tea stood an inch from the dictionary.

“I’ve waited over an hour,” said Qiao Zheng. “The door wasn’t locked, so I came in. Teacher’s wife isn’t here. I boiled the water myself. Teacher, I’m telling you that first, to save you asking.”

Qiusheng stood by the table.

Qiao Zheng opened the paper in his right hand, laid it on the table and pushed it over beside the cup of cold tea.

“Fifteen years ago. A crate of imported liquor seized on the docks, no duty receipt, no brand, methanol three parts in ten thousand. I wrote Fail. You drew a line through it, wrote one word beside it and signed on the ‘Checked by’ line,” he said. “This page I tore out in the records room, along a rule. It tore very straight. It has been in my pocket over a month. At Mid-Autumn, on the balcony, I told you that what you think goes last in a report, and that the part for what I think I had still left blank. In February it was filled in.”

Qiusheng looked at the page. Under the lamp, a line through the word Fail, and the word could be read underneath. Beside it one word, in another pen. Checked by: He Qiusheng.

“The typed stack in December had only one word in the last column too. I saw it at the first glance, put it at the very bottom and went and sealed the chemical supplier. In February I was at the town hall, lecturing on your report. I looked up, and you were sitting in the back row.” Qiao Zheng’s finger pressed once on the badge at his collar. “In March they were saying on the docks that Teacher had gone into the Wen cold store.”

His hand came down from his collar.

“I have sent a man to the docks once a week to buy a bottle, and assayed what he brought back. Twenty-odd times, and every one nil. What they say on the docks, the branch chief has heard too. He says the cold store is Wen & Co.’s property, someone in the provincial capital stands behind it, and the branch office does not go there.”

“She has agreed,” said Qiusheng.

“Agreed to what?”

Qiusheng did not answer. He undid the tape of the bundle, both turns.

“Teacher’s wife’s cloth bundle,” said Qiao Zheng.

The cloth folded back: three tin boxes. Qiusheng lifted the lids off one by one. As the first lid came up, the sheet of paper under it slid out and fell on the table, writing uppermost.

Qiao Zheng did not get up. He put out one finger, turned the sheet round and read it. White Dew: made over to Wen & Co. He took his finger away.

“Teacher, there is no one in all Fog Harbour who can read a glass of spirit as you can,” he said. “The glass tube goes into the measuring cylinder, and where the bubbles are, at what degree the mercury stops, you know with your eyes shut.”

He stopped a moment.

“But you are too much of a fool to read a person. The day she poured you tea, her mind was made up before the tea was cold.”

Qiusheng’s hand lay on the edge of the middle box.

“No hurry. Put it down when it stops. You taught me that,” said Qiao Zheng. “The moment she stopped was the day she poured you the first cup of tea. Everything after it was mercury climbing. It looked as though it was moving, and the figure had been there all along. Teacher, she has sent this paper back, and not because she doesn’t want it. She isn’t buying. She has no need to buy.”

Qiusheng picked up the sheet, folded it along its folds and put it in his shirt pocket.

At the foot of the slope a car went by. Its lights swept across the glass of the balcony door, and swept across the cup of cold tea.

“Last November the Chamber of Commerce had the branch office to tea,” said Qiao Zheng. “She poured a cup for me too, and asked where my family came from, and asked when I meant to marry. That cup of tea I did not drink. At the beginning of March an official letter came from the provincial capital, transferring me to the Bureau’s head office to look after the files. The branch chief held it back. He wants a name, and I am the only one at the branch office who can find it by assay.”

He looked at Qiusheng.

“Teacher, the name she wants and the name the branch chief wants are the same name. The letters from the provincial capital keep pressing, one after another. I have not written down a single name. How much longer I can go on not writing one, I don’t know.”

He reached out and pressed the folds of the counterfoil page flat once more, drew his hand back and rested it on the edge of the table.

“The day Scarface Cheng was taken away, he looked back at the door and said one thing: Think about who is really doing your business for you. I have thought about it for over a month.”

Qiusheng pushed the middle box across, past the edge of the counterfoil page, all the way to Qiao Zheng’s hand.

“Take it,” he said. “Leave Fog Harbour.”

Qiao Zheng looked down at the box. Tied with rubber bands, wad on wad. His right hand was an inch from the edge of the box. It did not move.

“Fifteen years ago you pushed the counterfoil back, and I took a fresh form and wrote it again,” he said. “That was when I made up my mind what kind of assayer I would be. Teacher, it was you who taught me. This box I won’t take. At Mid-Autumn I said Godfather will pay, and you said no need. Teacher, it’s the same.”

Qiusheng sat down on the square stool. The cup of cold tea was in front of him, and his hands lay on the edge of the table.

“Zheng—”

“But rules are rules, Teacher: the man who assays signs his name,” said Qiao Zheng. “This page has your name on it, and mine too.”

He looked at the page.

“This page I have not given to the branch chief. If I did, they would match the sample numbers, and one afternoon would be enough. You want me to leave Fog Harbour, Teacher. If I go, the provincial capital sends another inspector. He can’t find nil by assay. All he can do is write Pass.”

His hand rested a moment on the dictionary on the table. Hard covers, the one given at Mid-Autumn, and the dust left a streak on his fingers. He looked at the uniform, at the needle in the cuff. He did not touch it.

“Xiaoman has not been for her English since January. The clerk waited three Sundays,” he said. “Mrs Chen says Teacher’s wife left on New Year’s Day, with a suitcase. I did not ask where.”

Qiusheng began to cough. Cough after cough, the handkerchief pressed to his mouth, the other hand still on the edge of the table. Qiao Zheng waited for him to finish.

He stood up. The rattan chair creaked once. He picked up the counterfoil page and with his right hand folded it in half, and in half again, each fold on the old fold, and put it in the left pocket of his uniform. From the right pocket he took another paper, folded in four, laid it beside the cup of cold tea and pressed it flat.

“Dr Lin wrote it. Twelve injections,” he said. “He says you will know how to read it, Teacher.”

He did not take out the handcuffs, and he did not ask Qiusheng to hold out his hands.

He went behind the door, took his coat down from the hook and put it on. At the door he crouched and put on his shoes. He tied the laces once and they held. He stood up, a hand on the door frame, his back to the room.

“I’m Xiaoman’s godfather. I’ll still come for dinner at Mid-Autumn. Teacher, think it through.”

The door opened and shut again. The leather shoes went on down, step by step, slower than on the night of Mid-Autumn. At the half-landing, under the lamp, they stopped a moment. Then on down. The street door below sounded once, and after that there was no sound on the slope.

Qiusheng did not go out to the balcony. The slope was outside the balcony door, below, and could not be seen through the glass.

He reached out and turned off the lamp on the table.

In the dark he put the lids on the tin boxes one by one. One. Two. Three. The sound of each lid going on was the same.

The cup of tea had not been touched.

---

On Friday, before it was light, he carried the bundle down the slope. The three boxes went into the cellar, onto the top shelf, beside the one with Assay printed on it, one against another. The cloth was folded and put under the boxes. This time the door on South Slope was locked.

Six in the evening. Above, they had knocked off, and the fire was just lit.

Ah Yue crouched at the stove door, his right leg stretched out straight to one side. Ah Shui was washing bottles at the stone trough, the brush in his left hand. Ah Chang sat on the third step of the stairs with one foot on the step above. Qiusheng was at the workbench, the thermometer just drawn from the vat. On the wall, under the row of chalk, the date was still there.

The iron door sounded. Old Qi came down, fourteen steps, not fast, a film of fog on the shoulders of his railway uniform. At the bottom he first glanced back at the door behind him, and only then walked towards the corner. He reached the upturned vat and did not sit.

“Right hand,” he said.

Ah Yue turned his head from the stove door. “Whose right hand?”

Old Qi did not look at him. He looked at Qiusheng by the workbench, looked a moment, and sat down, and put his tea bowl on the floor at his left hand.

Qiusheng wiped the thermometer dry and put it back in the vat. He took his hand away and held on to the rim of the vat.

Ah Yue looked at Old Qi, looked at the Teacher, and wiped a hand down his trousers. Ah Shui’s brush turned in a bottle, stopped, and turned again. Ah Chang looked down at the watch on his wrist.

Ah Yue closed the stove door by a hair and asked nothing more.

The truck came back at midnight. Ah Yue came down to the foot of the stairs, his shoes wet. Old Qi was behind him, and sat back down on the vat in the corner.

“Teacher, there are patrol cars at the foot of South Slope,” said Ah Yue. “Two. Lights on, not moving.”

Qiusheng shut the stove door.

After the fire was banked he still sat at the workbench, the notebook open, the page blank. Old Qi was in the corner, his tea bowl on the floor, gone cold. Neither of them looked at the date on the wall.

---

On Saturday morning Ah Yue brought back a copy of the Fog Harbour Daily from the fish-market gate, a cent. He put the newspaper on the workbench and did not open it out.

Under the masthead was printed the date, 28 March. Qiusheng turned to page three. The local page, a small box in the bottom corner, the type a size smaller than elsewhere.

PROHIBITION BUREAU INSPECTOR ATTACKED AT NIGHT—From our own reporter: An inspector named Qiao, of the Fog Harbour branch of the Prohibition Bureau, was set upon late on the night of the 26th on the slope of South Slope by two men with clubs. His right hand is broken, and he is now in the mission hospital. The assailants made off. The branch chief states that it was an act of revenge, and the case has been passed to the detective squad for investigation.

He read the box twice. The second time, at the slope of South Slope, his finger stopped on the words. Late on the night of the 26th. That night the leather shoes had gone step by step to the bottom, the street door had sounded once, and from there on down was the slope.

Ah Yue stood behind him and saw the headline of the box.

“Teacher, Qiao—”

Qiusheng folded the newspaper and put it under the notebook. There was one under the notebook already, October’s, the one Old Hai had slapped down on the workbench. The two lay one on the other.

He picked up his coat. The notebook went into the inside pocket.

“Teacher, where are you going?”

Qiusheng did not answer. Ah Yue watched him climb the stairs. One step, one step, to the top, the iron door.

He walked along the railway. Past the level crossing at the goods yard, the barrier up, the red lamp unlit. Past the door of the branch office, the sign two deep, the strip underneath wet, a ring of rust round each nail. The doorman sat inside the door and did not look up. Qiusheng did not turn his head, and he did not stop.

---

Counsel Liang’s chambers were still on the first floor of the same red-brick building. In the shop selling Western dresses below, the gramophone was playing, still the same woman’s voice. Another layer of advertisements had been pasted up in the stairwell, and of the little man on the bottommost even the one hand was covered now. It was Saturday afternoon. The lady typist’s typewriter had its cloth over it, and the small desk by the door was empty. Counsel Liang sat alone behind the big desk, his fountain pen lying across a stack of papers, his handkerchief in his pocket with one pink corner showing.

He looked up, saw Qiusheng, and got to his feet.

“Teacher,” he said. “These stairs. This is the first time you have come up them by yourself.”

Qiusheng sat down in the chair before the desk, still out of breath. Counsel Liang poured a glass of water and pushed it across. Qiusheng did not touch it.

“I have read the box in the paper,” said Counsel Liang. “The slope of South Slope, the 26th. I had someone ask at the mission hospital. Plaster to the elbow, he is awake, and he can hold a bowl in his left hand. Mr Qi sat three hours in the Nanji yesterday afternoon, said not one word, and had his tea topped up four times. Teacher, on the night of the 26th—”

“On the tenth of April,” said Qiusheng, “Qiao Zheng will need a search warrant. He will need it that same day.”

Counsel Liang’s hand went towards his pocket. At the edge of the pocket it stopped.

“Needed that same day means signed the night before,” he said. “A judge is not at work at night. His doorbell is.”

His hand went on into the pocket. The handkerchief came out and stayed gripped in his hand. He did not wipe his forehead. He gave the handkerchief a look and put it back in his pocket.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset: a search warrant is three things. Applicant, grounds, place. The applicant is an inspector, the grounds are evidence, and the place must be one that can be written down. A judge is not a teahouse waiter. He does not go by tea. He goes by paper,” he said. “The tenth of April is thirteen days off. Inspector Qiao has his right hand in plaster and is lying in the mission hospital. What is he to apply with?”

“He’ll have it.”

“A judge goes by paper,” said Counsel Liang. “Have you paper in hand, Teacher?”

Qiusheng looked at him.

“You needn’t say,” said Counsel Liang. “Have you been to the hospital, Teacher?”

Qiusheng’s breathing had eased a little.

“Just as well not to. There are branch office men sitting at the door of the ward, two to a shift,” said Counsel Liang. “Search where?”

“The cold store.”

Counsel Liang’s fountain pen was on the desk. He did not reach for it. Downstairs the record on the gramophone was turned over.

“Teacher, the tenth of April,” he said. “Last time all I knew was the end of March. Where you had the date from, I do not ask.” He picked up the fountain pen and put it down again. “The branch’s own warrant the branch chief can hold back with one word. The court’s paper the branch chief cannot hold back, and so it must be the court. But Madam Wen has the Director-General in the provincial capital. Which judge in Fog Harbour would sign a paper to search the Wen cold store? And having signed it, would he still be sitting in that chair on the eleventh of April?” His finger tapped once on the edge of the desk. “Fog Harbour has three judges. Two are sent from the provincial capital, sit two years and are transferred. One is Fog Harbour’s own, who went away to study. He entered school the same year I did, the same year—”

There the words stopped. The finger did not tap again.

“You have a schoolmate who is a judge.”

Counsel Liang did not answer.

His hand went towards the fountain pen, reached it, and stopped.

He looked at Qiusheng, looked a while, moved his eyes to the stack of papers on the desk, and moved them back.

“Teacher, if Inspector Qiao were to learn who went to ask for this paper—”

“He won’t know.”

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset, a second time: the paper the judge wants, if it goes out from your hand, has your writing on it,” said Counsel Liang. “The stack in December was done on a typewriter. This time it is not. Inspector Qiao goes by handwriting.”

Qiusheng stood up.

He swallowed the cough, pressed his palm once to the right side of his chest and walked to the door. He went past the brass plate on the door, LIANG HENIAN, BARRISTER, and down the stairs. The sound of the gramophone came near, and fell away again. On the advertisements in the stairwell the little man in the top hat held his thumb up, layer on layer, thumb up all the way down to the street.

Counsel Liang sat alone.

He drew the handkerchief from his pocket, spread it on the desk and smoothed it flat with his palm. Pink, four corners. He folded it in half, and in half again, folded it in four, and pressed his finger along the folds.
