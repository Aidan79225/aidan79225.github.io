*End of March · Ebb Tide*

At the end of March, in the days when the tide went out furthest, Ah Yue said he was leaving.

On the night he said it, he drove the last run first.

31 March, a Tuesday. At six the fire was lit. Qiusheng crouched by the pot, and Ah Shui was washing bottles at the stone trough, the brush in his left hand. Ah Yue came down the stairs, one step heavy and one light. He walked to the pot, took the tear-off calendar down from its nail on the wall, turned it to today’s page and slapped it down on the lid of the pot.

“Teacher, the tide’s ebbing at Saltflat,” he said. “Twelve-twenty, dead low. We can take the flats tonight. The Bureau’s cars can’t get in.”

Qiusheng watched the thermometer. The mercury was above sixty-five, climbing slowly.

“Headman Ah Lu’s fifty bottles. All winter his people have fetched them by day, at the head of the sea wall,” said Ah Yue. “Tonight we go by the flats and take them to the gap. His people meet us. Back the same way.”

“Your foot hasn’t mended.”

“So let me drive this one. The last run.”

The mercury passed seventy-eight. Qiusheng said one word, and Ah Shui pushed the jar under the mouth of the pipe. Qiusheng stood up, took the tear-off calendar off the lid of the pot and hung it back on its nail.

“Teacher, ride in the truck this time,” said Ah Yue. “Have a look at the flats.”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He took his coat from the back of the chair.

At ten Old Qi came down from the iron door. He glanced first at the door behind him, walked to the pot, looked at the tides printed at the bottom of the tear-off calendar, and then at the wall. Under the row of chalk the date was still there.

“Two hours,” he said. “The mud’s hard for two hours.”

“Enough,” said Ah Yue.

“If it’s enough, we go,” said Qiusheng.

Old Qi looked at Ah Yue’s right foot.

“Can you drive?”

“I’ve driven all winter.”

Old Qi asked no more.

Ah Shui stayed to mind the fire. Qiusheng pointed at the stove door. “As it is. Don’t touch it.”

“Teacher, what time will you be back?”

“Two.”

Ah Shui nodded, his left hand pressed on his knee.

---

The truck went out by the side gate at half past ten.

Ah Yue drove. Qiusheng sat beside him, in what was usually Ah Chang’s seat. Ah Chang climbed onto the bed of the truck and sat among the five crates, held the watch on his wrist up to the light of the headlamps, and lowered it again. Old Qi was the last onto the bed and sat at the very back, against the tailboard.

The truck passed the siding and passed the level crossing at the goods yard, the barrier up, the red lamp unlit. Along the railway. At the door of the branch office the sign two deep, the strip underneath black, one lamp lit where the doorman sat. Ah Yue did not look at it. Past the corner of Warehouse No. 7, past the fish-market gate, the ferry lying dark at the foot of the stone steps. Beyond that the docks came to an end, there were no more warehouses, and the road turned to dirt and sloped down, down to the sea.

A clear sky. There was no fog that night, the stars were thick, and on the sea lay one white line. Ah Yue stopped the truck at the end of the dirt road and put out the lights.

Below were the mudflats.

With the tide out the mud spread away in one great sheet, black, oily, with a little light of its own on the surface, from the stars or from the water, there was no telling. Far off the sea was going out, the sound of the water very soft, beat after beat, outwards. The holes of the fiddler crabs lay close together, hole after hole. A long way off to the left the sea wall of the salt pans was a black line, and on the metalled road beyond the wall stood two pairs of headlamps, yellow, not moving.

“Over by the Saltflat stop,” said Ah Yue. “Two. Lights on. They can’t get in.”

He got down. His right foot touched the ground first and his body lurched. He did not put out a hand. He walked to the edge of the mud and put his good foot down on it, held it there, and trod again. The mud sank half an inch and sank no further. He bent and pressed it with his hand, took the hand away, and wiped it down his trousers.

“Hard.”

He got back in. Old Qi sat on the bed of the truck. Ah Chang looked at his watch.

“Eleven-thirty-five.”

Ah Yue started the engine. The lights he did not turn on.

The truck went down off the dirt road onto the flats. The front wheels went on first, and the body dropped. The wheels pressed half an inch into the mud. The mud did not give. The truck went on.

The mud sounded under the wheels, not the sound of gravel, a dull sound, unbroken. Ah Yue held the wheel, both hands, not driving fast. On the right the sea was going out a hundred feet away, out a little, then still. On the left the two pairs of yellow lamps, with the whole of the salt pans between, stayed where they were, coming no nearer, and not going away. Behind there was nothing. The only thing chasing them was the hour of the tide, minute by minute.

Qiusheng coughed once, his handkerchief pressed to his mouth. Ah Yue’s foot paused on the accelerator and pressed down again.

On the bed of the truck Old Qi looked back at the sea, and then ahead.

“Mr Qi,” said Ah Yue, not turning his head, “the Black Flag waited on this road once.”

“Everyone can see the tide,” said Old Qi.

“No one’s waiting tonight.”

“Not tonight.”

In the middle of the flats a boat lay tilted in the mud.

The Fair Wind. Her hull was towards them, the barnacles scraped, half of them scraped off, the colour of the wood showing; below the waterline two new planks had been let in, pale, the nails bright. The hatch cover was new. Ah Yue drove up alongside her. The truck slowed and did not stop.

“My uncle’s boat,” he said. “Mended this month.” He was looking ahead. “The hull will still float.”

Qiusheng looked at the two new planks. The truck went past, and the boat fell behind. He did not ask what she had been mended for.

Old Qi looked back at the boat, and looked longer than he had at the sea.

“Who mended her?”

“I did,” said Ah Yue. “Sundays. On my own.”

“She’ll float?”

“She’ll float.”

Old Qi kept his head turned until the boat could not be seen.

Ahead, at the foot of the sea wall, there were lights. Three, hurricane lanterns, spaced out in a line, at the gap onto the flats. The truck’s lights were still off, and Ah Yue steered for the middle one. When they came up to it three men were standing behind the lanterns, their trouser legs rolled to the knee, white lines of salt down their calves.

“Ah Yue,” said the one in the middle.

“Five crates.”

The crates came down off the bed of the truck. Ah Chang carried, Ah Yue carried, crate by crate into the hands of the three men. At the third crate Ah Yue’s right foot turned. The crate did not touch the ground. He shoved it into the other man’s arms. Old Qi sat on the tailboard and did not get down, his eyes on the metalled road beyond the sea wall.

“Uncle Ah Lu says Teacher’s truck goes where it likes on the flats,” said the man in the middle. “He’s up on the wall. His legs are bad. He isn’t coming down.”

“The last run,” said Ah Yue.

The man did not understand. Ah Yue did not explain. He looked at Qiusheng in the truck, lifted the last crate and handed it over.

“Next week?” the man asked.

“Thursday, the head of the sea wall, by day,” said Ah Yue. “The truck will come. It won’t be me.”

The man glanced at Old Qi on the bed of the truck, and nodded. From behind the lantern he brought out a basket covered with sacking and lifted it up to the bed. “From Uncle Ah Lu, for Teacher.” Ah Chang took it and put it on his knees. A smell of dried fish.

The three lanterns went away up the sea wall, one after another, reached the top, and turned into the dark.

“Twelve-twenty,” said Ah Chang.

“The tide’s coming back,” said Old Qi.

On the way back the mud was a shade softer. The wheels pressed down, the mud gave a little to either side and closed again. Ah Yue drove a little faster. Old Qi’s voice came from the bed of the truck:

“Lighter.”

Ah Yue’s foot eased by a hair.

The Fair Wind came up again, this time on the left. The mud under her was brighter than before. The water had come creeping in from the stern end, a thin sheet of it, and her stern was already afloat. Ah Yue’s eyes were ahead. Qiusheng looked.

The truck came up onto the dirt road, the wheels struck hard ground, and the body jolted. Ah Yue turned on the lights. The light fell on the dirt road ahead and did not reach behind. Behind, two wheel tracks ran from the dirt road all the way back into the mud, down to the water’s edge. At that end the water was creeping up.

---

Old Qi got down at the goods yard. He stood on the gravel, looked over towards the level crossing, and went off along the railway, into the dark. Ah Yue stopped the truck outside the side gate. Ah Chang carried the basket down the stairs, set it by the workbench, and went home. The smell of dried fish spread through the cellar.

“Tomorrow it goes to the union,” said Qiusheng.

In the cellar the fire was still in, and the heart in the jar had reached the second mark. Ah Shui got up off the sacks, his left hand pushing on his knee.

“Go home and sleep,” said Qiusheng.

Ah Shui went up the stairs. The iron door sounded once.

Of the six vats, only the one furthest in had not been distilled. Ah Yue lifted the board, put the brick to one side, and brought his nose to the mouth of the vat. He said it was ready. The mash went into the pot, liquor and all, a dipperful at a time, Qiusheng ladling, Ah Yue carrying the bucket, one step heavy and one light. At seven-tenths they added no more. The lid went on, the thermometer went into its hole, strips of wet cloth all round the rim. Charcoal went on.

Ah Yue lifted a crock down from the shelf, an empty one, turned it upside down, set it at the condenser end, and sat on it. Both hands on his knees.

The mercury climbed. Fifty-five, sixty. Sixty-four and a half degrees. It stopped. Qiusheng’s palm was pressed to the right side of his chest. The mercury went on up. The mouth of the pipe dripped. Ah Yue did not move. Qiusheng himself set the little cup under the mouth of the pipe. The cup filled, and he carried it to the stone trough, set it on the edge of the trough, and did not pour it away. The mercury passed seventy-eight.

“Cut.”

He pushed the jar under the mouth of the pipe. The spirit fell into the jar, a drop, then a drop, joining into a thread. He crouched down again by the pot.

Ah Yue was counting. What drop he had got to he did not know himself.

“Ah Lian wants to go home and grow tea.” Ah Yue sat on the empty crock, watching the condenser drip spirit, drop by drop. “I want to go with her. I can’t afford this any more, Teacher.”

Qiusheng watched the thermometer.

“Is she all right?”

“She’s been out of hospital a while. She’s at her mother’s cousin’s. She doesn’t go out,” said Ah Yue. “The scar on her face is for life. She says she won’t sing any more, and no teahouse will take her on again anyway. Back to East Ridge, in the hills to the east. Grow tea.”

Qiusheng closed the stove door by a hair.

“Teacher.” Ah Yue raised his head. “At sixteen I was carrying loads on the docks. At eighteen I met you at the night school. At twenty-two I started making liquor with you. This half-year has been the best half-year of my life. What I made was good, it was clean, and people on the docks waved to me when they saw my truck. I never once thought a man like me could do a good thing.”

His voice caught a little. He stopped, and went on.

“But Ah Lian’s face. Every time I see her face I think, this is the price of the good thing I did, and the account is written on her face. I can’t afford this any more, Teacher. I can’t afford this any more.”

Qiusheng’s hand was still on the stove door.

“Her mother’s tea hill. Three mu,” said Ah Yue. “A verse for every row you pick. She says she won’t sing, so it’s only picking. I’ve carried sacks. A basket of tea is nothing.”

He looked at the mouth of the pipe.

“On the Winter Solstice, Teacher’s wife told me at the door the slope was slippery. I told her, Teacher’s cough, I’m keeping an eye on it.” He stopped. “Teacher, I can’t keep an eye on it now.”

Qiusheng turned the fire down a little.

“Go,” he said.

Ah Yue looked at him.

“You won’t keep me?”

“What would I keep you for?” Qiusheng gave a small smile. “So you can pay a little more of the price?”

Ah Yue’s hands were on his knees. He did not wipe them on his trousers.

Qiusheng stood up, went to the shelves and stepped up on the stool. At the back of the top shelf, four tin boxes, one against another. The one from the Assay Office he did not weigh. The other three he lifted in turn and weighed in his hand, put two back, and tucked the heaviest in the crook of his arm. With the hand he had free he raised one corner of the lid of the Assay Office box, drew an old form from under the reports, and pressed the lid down again. He got down off the stool, put the box on the workbench, opened it, turned the form over, the back up, took his pen and wrote one line. When he had written it he put it in the box, on the very top. He held the box out to Ah Yue.

“It’s yours.”

Ah Yue opened the box. Inside were banknotes, wad on wad, tied with rubber bands, and on the very top lay a slip of paper in Qiusheng’s hand: Chen Yue, White Dew, fifty per cent.

“Teacher, that’s too much—”

“It’s yours,” said Qiusheng. “From the first day it was half each. You said then it was too much, and I said it’s yours. Have you forgotten?”

“Teacher, your half?”

“On the shelf.”

Ah Yue looked up at the shelves. At the back of the top shelf three boxes were left, one with printing on it, two without. He brought his eyes back and let them rest on the one in his hands. The box was heavy. He changed hands and held it from underneath.

Ah Yue held the box to his chest, and at last he wept.

He wept without a sound, his shoulders jerking, again and again. A drop fell on the banknotes. He wiped it away with his thumb, onto the slip of paper, and the writing on the slip ran a little.

Qiusheng waited for him. The thread of spirit at the mouth of the pipe went on falling.

Ah Yue wiped his face with the back of his hand, put the lid on the box and set it down at his feet, gripped between them.

“My mother and my brother stay where they are for now,” he said. “Half of this box goes to them. My brother finishes school in June. He says he’ll do the deliveries.”

“No.”

Ah Yue nodded.

“Then he brings my mother up, and I meet them at the foot of the hills. If there’s a sedan chair she rides. If there isn’t I carry her,” he said. “Ah Shui stays with Old Qi.”

“Mm.”

Qiusheng took the pick from the foot of the wall and went to the wall at the very back. Four bricks, lying flat on the earth. He lifted the outermost one and put it at the foot of the wall. Ah Yue saw, stood up, and took the pick out of his hands.

“Let me.”

Ah Yue knelt, his right leg stretched out to one side, and glanced first at the three bricks alongside.

“Old Lu’s three crocks stay?”

“They stay,” said Qiusheng. “He’ll come on Sunday as always.”

The pick went in stroke after stroke, not loud. The earth was loose, three months buried. A foot down the pick touched something, and he put it aside and used his hands. The mouth of the crock came out first, sealed with cloth, the cloth sealed with clay, the print of the palm that had smoothed it still there. He drove both hands into the earth and lifted the crock out in his arms. Earth clung to its sides, yellow, in lumps. The white line on its belly was still there, fainter, earth bedded in the line. A needle, with a thread through its eye. It could be made out.

He set the crock by the workbench and went to wipe the earth off with his sleeve.

“Don’t wipe it,” said Qiusheng.

Ah Yue’s hand stopped.

“Take this crock too,” said Qiusheng. “Don’t open it. Keep it till the autumn, and on the day of White Dew send it to Saltflat for me, to Mrs He.”

“What is it?”

“White Dew,” said Qiusheng. It was the first time Ah Yue had heard him call it that himself. “The real vat. Tell her to open it at Frost’s Descent.”

Ah Yue looked at the crock, looked a while, and looked at him.

“Teacher, a whole crock won’t go by post.”

“Fill one bottle,” said Qiusheng. “Mark the bottle. Copy the drawing on the crock.”

Ah Yue crouched and looked at the line on the belly of the crock, and at the small ring. His finger went along beside the line, in the air, and did not touch it.

“Teacher, I can’t draw.”

“Copy it.”

“I’ll find a way,” said Ah Yue.

He filled the hole in and trod the earth flat. Three bricks, lying flat on the earth. The fourth he did not put back. He left it at the foot of the wall.

---

Outside the vent windows the bamboo rakes began scraping the sides of the vats again. Ah Yue sat on the crock with his head against the shelves, slept a while, and woke. At seven the big stove above was lit, and the smell of boiling beans came in through the vent windows.

The thread of spirit thinned. Qiusheng caught a little in a small glass, held it up to the lamp, smelled it, and wafted once with his palm. He put the glass down.

He finished collecting the heart of the last vat, turned off the fire, and poured the heads away down the drain.

The cup had stood on the edge of the trough all night. The spirit ran away along the channel in the bottom of the trough, without a sound. The cup went upside down on the board.

“Ah Yue, do you remember, at the first vat, what I made you promise me?”

Ah Yue stood up.

“Pour away the heads.”

“Whether I’m here or not,” said Qiusheng. “However short of money you are.”

Ah Yue nodded, and sniffed.

“I remember.”

Qiusheng put the cloth over the mouth of the jar and tied it with string.

“This jar isn’t to be bottled.”

“Teacher?”

“It stays.”

Ah Yue took the tin box up in his arms, the crock on top of the box, both hands under it. Passing the wall by the stairs he looked at the date under the row of chalk, and did not ask. He climbed the stairs. One step, one step, the right foot slow. At the top he looked back. Under the three lamps the Teacher was at the workbench, the notebook open.

The iron door sounded once.

Qiusheng wrote: *31 March, seventy-third vat, rice wine, ninety-three.* When he had written it he turned to the last page, that page, looked at it, and closed the book. He put the notebook into the inside pocket of his coat.

On Wednesday night at eleven the Nanji closed.

The waiter upended the chairs onto the empty tables one by one, legs in the air. The wireless on the counter was off, and the cloth was over the birdcage. Two lamps were left in the main room: one at the counter, one over the table by the stairs. The little stage in the corner was dark, the stool upside down on it, the pipa in its cloth cover leaning against the leg of the stool, where it had leaned since December.

At the table by the stairs sat Old Qi, his back to the wall, his face to the main door, his tea bowl at his left hand, the lid askew. At the next table Counsel Liang sat in the host’s seat, his back to the counter, his fountain pen lying across a takings sheet, his handkerchief in the pocket of his suit, one pink corner showing. Qiusheng sat facing him, his back to the board partition, in front of him a bowl of tea and a pastry box, on the box a thin ledger, on the ledger a sheet of paper folded in four. Ah Yue stood by the door, the door shut, his back against it, both hands in the pockets of his short jacket. At the foot of the stairs stood Ah Lian, in a cotton blouse, her hair down, a blue cloth bundle held to her chest, her face turned to the dark of the stairwell, the lamp reaching no more than one shoulder.

The waiter upended the last chair, went over to the little stage, laid a hand on the cloth cover of the pipa and looked towards the foot of the stairs.

“Miss Ah Lian, this—”

“Leave it,” said Ah Lian.

The waiter took his hand away and looked at Counsel Liang. Counsel Liang nodded. The waiter went past Ah Lian and up the stairs. The boards sounded all the way to the top, and were quiet.

“Teacher,” said Counsel Liang, “today’s box is not six hundred.”

“No,” said Qiusheng. “The two boxes from the shelf.”

Qiusheng took the sheet of paper off the ledger and put it by his hand. Counsel Liang moved the thin ledger onto the table, drew the box over, lifted the lid, looked in, put the lid back and pushed the box back where it had been. He did not count. He picked up the thin ledger. Bound from takings sheets, in Qiusheng’s hand, small, stroke by stroke. He turned the pages.

“Nanji investment, three thousand,” he read. “Six hundred a week, to 11 March, twenty-two weeks; the two weeks after, short. School fees thirty-two a term, the deposit a hundred, September. The monthly dividend sent to Saltflat, as before.” He turned a page. “The remainder—”

He stopped. He picked up the fountain pen and tapped it once on the table.

“The Dockworkers’ Union co-operative,” he read. “Register the trade mark, White Dew. Half the surplus, the Dockworkers’ Mutual Aid Society.”

He put the ledger down, stood up, and sat down again. He drew the handkerchief from his pocket and spread it on his knee.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset: before repeal, liquor cannot be registered. Write liquor in the register and the Bureau men are at the door the next day,” he said. “First we register tea, in the teahouse’s name. White Dew, a tea. In May we change it.”

“Mm.”

“A trade mark needs a line of description,” said Counsel Liang, his fountain pen in the air. “What do I write?”

“Heads and tails cut away; methanol content nil.”

Counsel Liang wrote it. When he had written it he looked at the line.

“Teacher, Upper Town people won’t understand it.”

“People on the docks will.”

Counsel Liang drew a stroke under the line.

“Applicant, the Nanji. Agent, Liang Henian.” He stood up again, the handkerchief from his knee caught up in his hand, went over to the counter, came back with a kettle of water and filled up Qiusheng’s bowl. He did not sit. “Teacher, for handling a trade mark the going rate in Upper Town is fifty dollars apiece; the co-operative’s accounts go through the teahouse, and I take twenty per cent. That’s the going rate.”

Qiusheng looked at him.

Counsel Liang put the kettle down. The handkerchief was still gripped in his hand. He did not wipe his forehead. He glanced at the handkerchief, folded it and put it back in his pocket.

“No fee on this one,” he said.

He sat down. The fountain pen drew a stroke on the takings sheet.

“I made the telephone call,” he said, his voice a notch lower. “On Saturday, after you left. The man who answered said one thing only: paper.”

“Mm.”

“A judge goes by paper. Teacher, I have no paper here.”

Qiusheng’s hand was pressed on the ledger.

“One thing more,” said Counsel Liang. “Inspector Qiao went back to the branch office yesterday. Plaster to the elbow. He signed in with his left hand. The two branch office men from the door of his ward went back with him, and now they stand at the door of the branch office.”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He picked up the sheet of paper by his hand, stood, walked to the table by the stairs and put it down beside Old Qi’s tea bowl, on the right-hand side.

Old Qi looked first at the main door, and only then opened the paper.

On the paper were routes. A date to a line, 1 April to 10 April, and on every line a road, an hour, a number. The union, two hundred, Thursday; Saltflat, fifty, the head of the sea wall; the Paramount, a hundred, handed over at the goods yard. The last line was 10 April, and the hour written after it was before daylight.

Old Qi’s thumb moved to that line, and his nail scored a mark under the hour. He folded the paper back along its folds.

“Deliver until when?”

“Until no one buys.”

Old Qi put the paper in the inside pocket of his uniform and did up the button. He lifted his tea bowl and took a mouthful.

“Four boys ride with the truck,” said Qiusheng. “They’re yours. Ah Shui stays to wash bottles.”

Old Qi looked over towards the door. Ah Yue leaned on the door and did not move.

“Not bad,” said Old Qi.

“Mr Qi,” said Counsel Liang from his table, “the ice truck comes on Thursday as usual?”

“It comes,” said Old Qi. “I collect as usual.”

Counsel Liang’s fountain pen hung over the table and did not tap.

Qiusheng walked back to Counsel Liang’s table and sat down. Ah Yue straightened up off the door and walked to the foot of the stairs. Ah Lian came one step out of the dark. The lamp caught half her face, and she turned her face a little further away.

“Teacher He,” she said.

She put the blue cloth bundle she was holding into Ah Yue’s hands. Blue cloth, folded square, a mended seam at one corner.

“This,” she said, and stopped a moment, “take it to Mrs He.”

Ah Yue looked down at the bundle.

“I’ve no use for it now,” she said.

Ah Yue came over with the bundle and put it on the table in front of Qiusheng, against the pastry box. He did not speak. He went back to the door and leaned his back against it.

Ah Lian looked at the little stage in the corner. She walked over, laid her hand on the cloth cover of the pipa, pressed once, and took the hand away. She did not pick it up. She walked back to the foot of the stairs and stood in the dark again.

“Teacher He,” she said, “I can write the two characters for White Dew now.” She stopped a moment. “I’m taking the paper with me.”

The old Assay Office forms, with Lian written on the back, and White Dew, the sheets with the two birds. Qiusheng looked at the shoulder the lamp reached.

“Mm.”

Counsel Liang folded the takings sheet and put the fountain pen back in his pocket. He glanced at Ah Yue, and glanced at the foot of the stairs.

“Mr Chen, what time is your bus?”

“The first. The Old Town roundabout, six o’clock. To the foot of the hills.”

“East Ridge,” said Counsel Liang. “I have had the tea. Bitter.”

From the inside pocket of his suit he drew a card. White card, black print. He held it between two fingers across the table towards the door.

“Mr Chen, Upper Town 237. If anything comes up in the hills, go into the town and ring that.”

Ah Yue took it and put it in the pocket of his jacket.

Old Qi spoke from the table by the stairs.

“The six o’clock bus. I’ll see you onto it.”

Ah Yue looked at him. Old Qi was looking at the main door.

Ah Yue’s hand came out of his pocket and wiped once down his trousers. The pastry box on the table, the ledger, the blue cloth bundle. The next table, Old Qi’s. He looked at them one by one, and only then turned to Qiusheng.

“Teacher,” he said, “where are you going?”

No one answered him. Counsel Liang’s hand went towards his pocket, got as far as the corner of the handkerchief, and stopped. Ah Lian did not move in the dark. The filament of the lamp at the counter ticked once.

Qiusheng put the ledger back on the box and pushed the box over to Counsel Liang’s side. The bundle he took in his arms, and stood up. Old Qi lifted his tea bowl, drank it off, and set it back at his left hand.

---

Ah Yue left on Thursday at daybreak.

At six Old Hai came over from the track end. The sound of the chain came first. The fire in the cellar was banked, and Qiusheng was at the workbench, the thermometer lying on the notebook. Old Qi was not there. Old Hai ducked in under the chain, bald, in his padded waistcoat, the cigarette tin gripped in one hand, and in the palm of the other something made of iron.

He walked to the workbench and put the thing down.

The keys of the truck. An iron ring, two keys, and hanging from the ring a length of red string, the one from the box of egg tarts, faded.

“Ah Yue left the truck at five in the lane behind the union,” said Old Hai, a sentence at a time. “He stood in the lane a quarter of an hour before he knocked. Gave me the keys. Told me to bring one message.”

Qiusheng looked at the length of red string.

“Someone has to watch the vats; Teacher can’t watch them alone,” said Old Hai. “His words.”

The vats in the row along the wall were all empty, and there was no sound in the cellar.

“Hang them back up,” said Qiusheng.

Old Hai looked at him, picked up the keys, walked to the foot of the stairs and hung the ring back on its nail. The red string hung down.

He walked back. He lifted the sacking on the basket by the workbench, looked, and put the sacking back.

“Headman Ah Lu’s.”

“For the union.”

Old Hai picked up the basket, tucked it under his arm, and put the cigarette tin in his trouser pocket.

“Sunday is Qingming,” he said. “Behind the warehouse. The union is setting out tables.”

“Mm.”

Old Hai ducked out under the chain. The chain swung twice and was still.

2 April, a Thursday. The X-ray was taken in the morning, and at three in the afternoon he brought it to Dr Lin’s surgery.

The wooden board at the turn of the stairs, Physician · Diseases of the Chest, the paint flaked off one corner. There was no one on the waiting bench. The electric fan had a cloth over it, put on for the winter and not yet taken off. The nurse told him to go in and did not ask his surname. The light box was on, and the film was already up on it.

Dr Lin did not listen to the chest first. He stood before the light box with the end of his pencil on the mass of white at the top of the right lung. A sheet of white, from the top corner down, with no edge. He tapped it once. The end of the pencil moved away, moved to the left, to the top corner of the left lung, and tapped again. A small patch there, faint. He said nothing at all.

He looked at the film for a long time, a long time, then took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes.

“Mr He, you must go to the sanatorium,” he said. “Tomorrow.”

“For how long?”

“I don’t know,” said Dr Lin. “Half a year. A year. Perhaps longer.”

Qiusheng looked at the white on the light box. The blurred shadow of the better part of a year ago now filled half the right lung, and there was a small patch on the left as well.

“In December you paid for half a year,” said Dr Lin. “I wrote to them about a bed. They have written back from the hills. There is a car tomorrow.”

Qiusheng pressed his palm to the right side of his chest.

“Mrs He came in December,” said Dr Lin. “She sat on the bench outside and asked about bowls and chopsticks. I said keep them separate. She asked nothing else.”

Qiusheng turned his head towards the door, and turned it back.

“The paper I gave the Inspector,” Dr Lin asked. “Did it reach you?”

“It did.”

“How many injections have you had?”

“None.”

Dr Lin put the pencil back in his pocket. He pulled open the drawer, took out the ledger, opened it, shut it, and put it back.

“And if I don’t go?”

Dr Lin did not answer. He only took the film down, slid it into a paper envelope and handed it to him.

Qiusheng took the envelope and walked out of the surgery. Schoolgirls let out for the day were coming down the slope of South Slope in twos and threes, their blue uniform skirts turning over in the wind. He looked for Xiaoman among them, looked once, and looked again, and only then remembered that Xiaoman was not here any more.

Halfway up the slope the two copper kettles of the herbal-tea stand were turned upside down, and the old man was not there. Qiusheng tucked the envelope under his arm and walked on up the slope.

---

The door on the second floor he opened with his own key.

Nothing in the room had been moved. A lamp on the table. He turned it on. The half-altered uniform, the needle stuck in the cuff; a key; a dictionary, the dust a layer thicker. Beside the dictionary a cup, the half cup Qiao Zheng had left, dried down to a stain on the bottom. In front of the square stool the other cup, poured on the night of 26 March, which no one had drunk, a week cold, a grey skin on the tea, a ring round the rim. Beside that cup a folded paper, in Dr Lin’s hand, twelve injections.

He carried the two cups to the kitchen, poured the tea into the sink, rinsed the cups and stood them upside down. He came back, picked up the prescription, folded it once more, on the old folds, and put it in his shirt pocket.

Ah Lian’s blue cloth bundle he put beside the uniform. The envelope with the X-ray stood against the wall, unopened.

He took the notebook from the inside pocket of his coat, opened it and laid it flat under the lamp. The writing paper was an exercise book Xiaoman had left behind, the pages torn out, ruled in squares.

That night he wrote three letters.

The first was to Su Qi. He wrote a great deal, filled a sheet, and tore it up; wrote again, tore again. The torn sheets he crumpled and put on the corner of the table, a heap a fist high. In the end only a few lines were left: Qi, I have done a thing I know is wrong, and I have done it well. Xiaoman’s school fees and the deposit for the normal school, Liang Henian will send you every month. I am sorry. Qiusheng.

He folded the sheet. He picked the uniform up from the table, the cuff half unpicked, the needle stuck in the lining, its eye end outwards. He did not pull it out. He folded the uniform with the needle inside. Ah Lian’s qipao he took out of the blue cloth bundle, pale grey satin, the waist taken in by Su Qi, and laid it on top of the uniform. The few lines went on the very top. He turned the blue cloth up over them, wrapped it all, passed hemp string twice round and knotted it. On a slip of paper he wrote: Saltflat Village, Mrs He. He tucked the slip under the string. The ferry went from the fish-market gate, three crossings a day.

The second was to Ye Mingde. One line only: The side draw at the top of the column, cut to seventy-eight degrees. Cut too much rather than too little.

The third was to Qiao Zheng.

He wrote all night. The notebook lay open beside him and he copied from it, page by page. He wrote the White Dew process, from soaking the rice to the ageing, every temperature, every time; wrote where the Futai cellar was, the fourteen stone steps, the wooden sign by the iron door; wrote where the cold store was, the three layers of smell on the spiral stair, the thin tube at the head of the column; wrote Madam Wen’s plan for the tenth of April, wrote the name of the Director-General of the Prohibition Bureau, wrote everything Counsel Liang had told him; wrote of the Black Flag’s firewater, of the forty-one men dead on the docks, of Ah Lian’s face. He wrote of the square bottles with the stag label, how many crates in all, and who they were sold to. He wrote of Qiao Zheng’s right hand, the night of 26 March, the slope of South Slope.

Of the Fair Wind he did not write.

Here he stopped a moment, pressed his palm to the right side of his chest and coughed for a while, his handkerchief pressed to his mouth. When the coughing was done he went on writing.

Last he wrote:

“Zheng, you once asked me about the verdict column, and I gave you my answer then. Every bottle of the spirit I have made this half-year is drinkable. But I know too that it does not pass.

“After the tenth of April Fog Harbour will have a big lawful distillery. If the people who make the spirit do not know to cut the heads, the spirit that distillery makes will kill more people than the Black Flag’s firewater. I have told Ye Mingde how to cut, but I do not know whether he will do it. So I am writing the process out for you as well. You are the best assayer in all Fog Harbour. I have always thought so.

“On the tenth of April, please go to the cold store and assay the first batch.”

He signed his name at the end of the letter. Three characters, stroke by stroke, the same as on the counterfoil.

The two letters he folded and put into two envelopes. On one he wrote Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau, For the personal attention of Inspector Qiao Zheng; on the other, the Wen cold store, Mr Ye Mingde. The stamps he found in the drawer, bought by Su Qi, three left. He stuck on two and put one back in the drawer. Writing to someone else is always easier than writing to oneself. The heap of torn sheets on the corner of the table he took to the kitchen, struck a match, lit them ball by ball and dropped them into the stove.

Outside the window it had gone grey. Down the slope someone was taking down shutter boards. The dictionary he left on the table, beside the key. The dust he did not wipe. He turned off the lamp, put the notebook back in the inside pocket of his coat, tucked the parcel under his arm, and took the two letters in his hand. The two cups stood upside down on the rack in the kitchen, drying with the three sets of bowls and chopsticks. He locked the door.

At daybreak he dropped the letters into the box outside the South Slope post office.

Then he took the first bus to the docks.
