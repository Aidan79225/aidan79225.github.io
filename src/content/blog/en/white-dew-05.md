*Mid-Autumn*

On the afternoon of Mid-Autumn Su Qi came home from the back hill with a basket of osmanthus.

The basket was the one that held the scraps at the tailor’s stall, a bamboo one, with a white cloth laid over the mouth. As soon as she was through the door she put the basket on the table and turned back the cloth, and the whole place was full of the scent. The osmanthus was fine and yellow, the flowers crowded one against another along the twigs. The twigs still had a few leaves on them, and on the undersides of the leaves was soil from the hill. There was soil on the soles of her shoes too. She had scraped them twice at the door before she came in.

Qiusheng sat in the rattan chair with the Fog Harbour Daily spread on his knees, open at the situations vacant. The pencil had gone under several lines and then crossed them out. He looked up.

“The back hill,” said Su Qi. “The tree by the Earth God shrine. It’s flowered early this year.”

“The stall?”

“It’s Mid-Autumn. The market shut at twelve.” She rolled her sleeves down. There were crumbs of bark on the cuffs, and she flicked them off. “The old women at the spring say that tree only flowers these ten days in the year. If it isn’t picked now it drops.”

“Mm.”

“They say too that in August there was a young fellow who came every day pushing a tricycle to fetch water, four buckets at a time. He said there was a wedding in the family.” Su Qi moved the basket towards the middle of the table. “A wedding that went on a month. The old women have worked it out. That makes a feast of forty tables.”

Qiusheng looked at the newspaper. The lines the pencil had gone under were a little smudged with the sweat of his hand.

Xiaoman’s door stood open. She was saying some line over in her room. Halfway through she stopped and began again from the start.

Su Qi drew a low stool up to the table and sat. She took the osmanthus out of the basket twig by twig and stripped the flowers downward with two fingers into a white china bowl. The stalks she picked out and dropped at the corner of the table. The leaves went to one side. Her hands were quick, a tailor’s hands, and she did not look up. The flowers heaped in the bowl. A few fell by the rim and she flicked them back with a fingertip.

“When I was little my mother steeped it in spirit and brought it out for the festivals,” she said.

Qiusheng folded the newspaper once.

“There’s no osmanthus on the sandspit,” Su Qi went on. “My mother had the ferry bring it from the Old Town. Dried, ten cents a packet. She’d steep one jar. New Year, Mid-Autumn, a small cup each. My father had two cups, and when he’d drunk them he went to look at the boat.”

“Remember how it’s done?”

Su Qi finished the twig in her hand, dropped the stalk and took up the next.

“A layer of flowers, a layer of spirit,” she said. “The flowers want a night in the shade first. Not the sun. In the sun the scent goes. The spirit has to be clean, or the flowers are wasted.”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He stood up and spread the newspaper from his knees on the table, the situations vacant uppermost, and smoothed it with his palm. He took a twig from the basket and stripped it downward the way she did. Half the flowers fell. The other half broke off with the stalk between his fingers.

“Strip. Don’t pinch,” said Su Qi. “Pinch them and they go black.”

He took another twig and held his fingers a little looser. This time the flowers all fell into the bowl and the stalk stayed in his hand, whole. Su Qi tipped the flowers from the white china bowl onto the newspaper. He spread them with his fingers, one thin layer, out over the lines the pencil had gone under, and covered them. When he had done he took up a pinch, brought it under his nose and wafted once with his palm. Sweet, not cloying, a little green underneath.

“What are you smelling?”

“The flowers.”

He put the pinch back. Su Qi looked at his hands and went on stripping.

Xiaoman came out of her room with a sheet of paper in her hand. An osmanthus flower was caught in her hair, and there was no telling when it had got there.

“Papa, Mama, ‘The boat hasn’t come yet. The boat hasn’t come yet. The boat hasn’t come yet.’ Three lines all the same. How do I say them three different ways?”

“When you wait for someone you want work in your hands,” said Su Qi. Her hands did not stop. “The ladies who wait at the stall for their alterations, not one of them just stands and waits.”

“There’s no work on a stage.”

“When my mother waited for my father’s boat she had a net in her hands.” Su Qi finished a twig. “When one was mended and the boat wasn’t back, she mended another. The people at the ferry landing looked at her hands and knew whether the boat was in.”

“How?”

“When the boat was in, her hands stopped.”

Xiaoman stood there, lifted the paper and lowered it again, and thought for a moment. “Our teacher says we can’t hold anything on stage.”

“Then eat,” said Su Qi.

“It isn’t cooked yet.”

“Then go and wash the rice.”

Xiaoman put the paper down on the table and went into the kitchen. A few lines were copied on it in pencil. At the top were the words “The Landing”, and under them a line of small writing: Scene Three, the one who waits for the boat.

Su Qi stood up, patted down her apron, swept the stalks into her palm and dropped them in the kitchen dustpan. She came back and pushed the newspaper towards the wall, a little further from the edge of the table.

“Qiao Zheng’s coming tonight.”

“Mm.”

“He comes every year.”

“Mm.”

“And your night shift?”

Qiusheng looked at the flowers on the newspaper. “A bit later.”

Su Qi asked no more. She carried the basket to the door, folded the white cloth and put it in the basket, and went into the kitchen. The tap began to run. Inside, Xiaoman said the rice was washed, and Su Qi said to wash it again. Qiusheng sat back down in the rattan chair. One corner of the newspaper on the table curled up, and under it half a line of print showed, one the pencil had gone under: Warehouse accounts, sixteen dollars a month.

---

Every Mid-Autumn Qiao Zheng came to the He family for dinner, and this year was no different.

At a quarter to six there were leather shoes on the stairs, two steps at a time, slowing only at the second floor. Xiaoman heard them first and ran to open the door.

Qiao Zheng came with mooncakes, a bag of pomelos and an English dictionary for Xiaoman. He took off his shoes at the door and said the branch office had been busy this year and he had nearly not come. The mooncake box was wrapped in red paper, from the cake shop in Upper Town, the string tied in a cross; the pomelos, three of them, were in a net bag; the dictionary was in hard covers, twice as thick as the one on Xiaoman’s desk. Xiaoman took the dictionary first and opened it to look at the flyleaf. On the flyleaf was a line in fountain pen: For Xiaoman, to keep. Godfather. Mid-Autumn. The writing was very upright, each stroke separate, none joined. She said, “Godfather.”

“*Vocabulary*,” she said.

“Godfather doesn’t understand that,” said Qiao Zheng. “The man in the bookshop said everyone who sits for the normal school uses this one.”

Su Qi took the mooncakes and the pomelos and weighed the pomelos in her hand.

“From Upper Town.”

“The fruiterer’s next door to the cake shop. You knew it by the weight, Ma’am.”

“Thick skin, heavy in the hand. The ones they sell on the docks are thin-skinned.” She shifted the pomelos into the crook of her arm. “You’re here, that’s what matters. Busy with what?”

“Busy with liquor,” said Qiao Zheng. “Ma’am, the busiest trade in Fog Harbour just now is liquor.”

“And a wife?”

Qiao Zheng laughed and said that could wait until Xiaoman got into the normal school.

Qiusheng sat in the rattan chair in the front room and watched Qiao Zheng hang his coat behind the door. The hook behind the door was where Qiusheng’s own coat usually hung. Today that coat lay over the back of the rattan chair. Under the coat Qiao Zheng was all in khaki, sharply pressed, and pinned to the collar of the uniform was a small brass badge: a bottle with a cross struck through it. The badge of the Prohibition Bureau.

“Teacher.” Qiao Zheng came over and bent to shake his hand. His palm was dry and firm. “I know what happened at the Office. I meant to come and see you. I could never get away.”

“There was nothing to see,” said Qiusheng. “An old man retiring, that’s all.”

“You’re not old.” Qiao Zheng sat down on the square stool beside him, knees together, both hands on his knees. “Without you at the Office, the new men can’t even tell methanol from ethanol. A batch of samples came in last week. They were three days assaying it, and in the end I had to do it myself.”

“And Young Zhou?”

“Young Zhou’s still there, on the high stool beside your place. He’s the one who told me what happened at the Office,” said Qiao Zheng. “He said the day you left you wouldn’t let anyone see you off. The oversleeves were folded and put away in the drawer, and to this day no one has opened that drawer.”

“See me off for what?”

Su Qi brought out two cups of tea. She put one on the little table at Qiao Zheng’s elbow and one on the arm of Qiusheng’s rattan chair, and went back to the kitchen. Qiao Zheng lifted his and drank a mouthful, and put the cup back with its foot square on the ring it had left on the little table.

“And the director?”

“Gone at the end of the month too. The branch chief was sent down from the provincial capital. He doesn’t know spirit. He knows official letters,” said Qiao Zheng. “The new sign has been nailed up. The old one wasn’t taken down, and a strip of it shows underneath. The painter said the white paint was still good and it would be a pity to scrape it off and paint again, so the new one was nailed over the top of it. On a rainy day that strip underneath gets wet first. The doorman is the same one. When he sees me he still calls me Young Qiao.”

Qiao Zheng paused. “Teacher, what do you mean to do now? They’ve been asking at the Office.”

“We’ll see.”

“Still teaching at the night school?”

“Not any more.”

“A pity,” said Qiao Zheng. “I wanted to go and listen myself, back then. I was on night duty at the Office and couldn’t get it changed.”

“You’re an inspector now,” said Qiusheng.

“Inspector for the Fog Harbour branch.” Qiao Zheng gave a wry smile. “It sounds well. It means everything is my business. Firewater on the docks, imported liquor in Upper Town, medicinal wine at the chemist’s, and—” He stopped for a moment. “Lately a new spirit has turned up.”

In the kitchen Su Qi called to Xiaoman to fetch the bowls. Xiaoman answered and ran out of her room with the dictionary still hugged to her chest, saw Qiao Zheng, said “Godfather” again, put the dictionary on the corner of the table and ran into the kitchen.

Qiusheng watched her go. “What new spirit?”

“White Dew,” said Qiao Zheng. “That’s what they call it on the docks. Some call it Teacher’s liquor. Someone inside the union sells it by the cup, five cents a cup, and it’s sold out before ten; the firewater at the drinking den round the back is three cents a cup, and the queue there is down by half. I sent a man to buy three bottles. The seller doesn’t ask who you are. He takes the money and hands it over. Ask where it comes from and nobody says.”

The cup of tea on the arm of the rattan chair was still untouched.

“When my man came back to account for it, the three bottles were half a bottle short,” said Qiao Zheng. “He said it spilled on the way. Teacher, the strangest part is still to come.”

“What?”

“It’s clean.” Qiao Zheng’s finger pressed for a moment on the badge at his collar. “I assayed three bottles. Methanol content, nil. Fifteen years I’ve been in this line, and I have never once assayed a bottle of bootleg at nil. Firewater goes without saying. Even the imported liquor that’s smuggled in always has a little. But this White Dew is as clean as…”

“As what?”

“As a reference standard at the Office,” said Qiao Zheng. “Teacher, it was you who taught me to make the reference standards.”

For a moment the front room was quiet. From the kitchen came the clatter of bowls and plates, and Su Qi scolding Xiaoman for bringing the salt jar when she wanted the sugar.

Qiusheng lifted the tea from the arm of the chair and drank a mouthful. It was the kind Su Qi always bought, broken leaf, and it brewed harsh.

“Clean doesn’t mean lawful,” he said.

“True.” Qiao Zheng nodded. “But clean means the man who makes it knows how. There aren’t many in Fog Harbour who know this business. Teacher, you used to say that whether a spirit is any good can’t be hidden from a thermometer. I’ve been wondering whether it might be someone who used to be at the Office.”

“A great many people used to be at the Office.”

“A great many.” Qiao Zheng smiled. “But only one who could make it as well as this.”

Qiusheng put the teacup back on the arm of the chair. The foot of the cup came down without a sound.

He coughed, once. Once, no more. He did not take out his handkerchief.

Qiao Zheng’s hand started for his collar again, got halfway, and went back to his knee.

“Teacher, you’ve a cough.”

“An old complaint.”

“Young Zhou says you were already coughing at the Office. Have you seen a doctor?”

“I have.”

“What does the doctor say?”

“Fewer late nights.”

Qiao Zheng nodded.

“Dinner!” Su Qi came out carrying a plate of fish.

---

The table stood against the balcony door, and the four of them squeezed round three sides of it. Qiao Zheng had the side facing the balcony door, usually Qiusheng’s place, which Su Qi had given him, saying the guest should see the moon; Qiusheng sat with his back to the front door; Xiaoman sat close by her godfather, her stool moved over a little, their elbows almost touching; Su Qi was next to the kitchen door, where she could get up for more food without going round the table. On the table were a steamed fish, a plate of pork braised with taro, a dish of boiled water caltrops and a bowl of winter melon soup. The osmanthus lay spread on the newspaper at the corner of the table. Su Qi had not cleared it away. She pushed it a little further towards the wall. The mooncake box, its string undone, stood on the crockery cupboard.

Su Qi spoke first. “Eat.”

Qiao Zheng’s chopsticks did not move. He looked at Qiusheng. Qiusheng picked up his chopsticks and put a piece of fish in Xiaoman’s bowl. Only then did Qiao Zheng begin.

“Your fish, Ma’am,” he said. “I wait all year for this one meal.”

“From the market,” said Su Qi. “The stalls shut early today. It’s what was left.”

Xiaoman ate fast and talked as she ate. Qiao Zheng turned the fish over, took off a piece of the belly and picked the bones out of it for her one by one, and only when it was clean did he put it in her bowl. Qiusheng’s chopsticks stopped a moment over the fish plate. He took a water caltrop.

“Godfather, we’re doing a play in November,” said Xiaoman. “The whole school, in the hall. I play the one who waits for the boat. I stand at the landing, and I’ve only three lines, and all three are the same, the boat hasn’t come yet. Our teacher says this kind is the hardest to act. You stand there and don’t move, and people have to be able to see you’re waiting.”

“Waiting for what boat?”

“I don’t know. The script doesn’t say,” said Xiaoman. “She just waits, anyway.”

“Waiting for a boat I can do,” said Qiao Zheng. “Half the days at the branch office are spent on the docks waiting for a boat. Before a boat comes into harbour you can’t see a thing. You hear the whistle first. The ones who wait don’t watch the sea. Watching the sea tells you nothing.”

“Then I’ll listen.”

“Listen,” said Qiao Zheng. “When someone’s listening, the audience can see it.”

“Papa, you have to come and see it. The end of November, seven in the evening.”

“Mm.”

“What date?” Su Qi asked.

“The twenty-seventh, a Thursday,” said Xiaoman. “Our teacher says we can have the hall that day. On the Friday the head has a meeting.”

Xiaoman nudged her godfather with her elbow. “Godfather, you come too.”

“If the branch office gives me leave, I’ll come.”

Su Qi filled Qiao Zheng’s rice bowl again and moved the plate of taro over to his side. “Eat more. One look at your face and I know how the branch office feeds you.”

“Ma’am, there’s a clerk at the branch office who did two years of English at the university in the provincial capital. His family had no money, so he left and went to work,” said Qiao Zheng. “He’s free on Sundays. I’ve asked him. He’s willing to coach Xiaoman in English, once a week.”

“And his fee?”

“He says he won’t take one.”

“No fee, no coaching,” said Su Qi. “Twenty cents a week. We can pay that.”

Xiaoman looked at her mother, looked at her godfather, and made no sound. Qiao Zheng said twenty cents it was, then, and he would tell him. Su Qi nodded and turned the fish plate half round, so that the side without bones faced Xiaoman.

Qiao Zheng talked about the branch office. He talked slowly, the date first, then the people, as if he were writing a report.

“The twenty-fourth of last month, the middle of the night, No. 3 Wharf,” he said. “A freighter down from the provincial capital. A false bottom in the hold. Thirty crates of ‘medicinal wine’.”

“No. 3 Wharf?” Su Qi asked.

“Past the fish market. The one at the far end of the docks.”

“The twenty-fourth,” said Su Qi. She rested her spoon on the rim of her bowl. “The wind got up that night.”

“You remember it better than the branch office’s books, Ma’am.”

“The clothes at the stall, which day they come in, which day they go back, it all has to be remembered,” said Su Qi. “When the wind got up that night, one of the bamboo poles on the balcony came down. I got up in the middle of the night and brought it in.” She picked up her spoon. “And the ship’s master?”

“The master had an order from a chemist’s in the provincial capital. The order was real. The wine was false,” said Qiao Zheng. “The man is held at the branch office, and the order went up to the provincial capital to be checked. They’ve been checking it a month and the reply still hasn’t come.”

Qiusheng put a piece of taro in his bowl and did not eat it.

“And there was a man smuggling imported liquor,” Qiao Zheng went on, “who shipped the bottles hidden in a coffin. The undertaker’s men reported it.”

Xiaoman listened with her chopsticks stopped in mid-air.

“Was there anybody in the coffin?”

“Nobody. Twelve bottles,” said Qiao Zheng. “When the undertaker’s men lifted it they heard a clinking inside.”

“Taro,” said Su Qi. “From Saltflat. Your grandmother sent it over by the ferry. Eat.”

“The Black Flag gang’s firewater has killed seven more this month,” said Qiao Zheng. “Three at the drinking den behind the union, four in the Old Town. The branch office goes and seals the place, and the day after it’s sealed it’s open again.”

Su Qi stood up, took Qiusheng’s bowl and went into the kitchen for more rice. Qiusheng’s bowl was still half full.

“Seven,” said Xiaoman.

“Seven,” said Qiao Zheng. “Last month, nine. In the one month of June, forty-one.”

Su Qi came back with the rice bowl, set it down in front of Qiusheng, sat, picked up her spoon and ladled soup.

“Don’t talk of that at table,” she said.

“You’re right, Ma’am.” Qiao Zheng stopped there and took a piece of taro.

He picked up a water caltrop and bit it. The shell split, and he peeled out the white flesh and put it in Xiaoman’s bowl. Xiaoman said Godfather had good teeth. Su Qi bit one open too and put it in Qiusheng’s bowl. Qiusheng did not bite the shell. He picked at the flesh with the tips of his chopsticks, picked twice, broke it in pieces and put his chopsticks down. Su Qi glanced at the half caltrop and put a piece of taro in his bowl.

Xiaoman bent her head and shovelled in two mouthfuls of rice, then looked at her godfather’s collar.

“Godfather, what does the cross mean?”

“Not allowed,” said Qiao Zheng.

“What’s not allowed?”

“What’s in the bottle.”

Xiaoman looked again, bent her head and went on with her rice. Two mouthfuls, and she looked up again.

“Godfather, is the deposit for the normal school really a hundred dollars?”

“One hundred dollars,” said Qiao Zheng. He put down his chopsticks and put both hands back on his knees. “Paid the day you enter, returned the day you finish, forfeit if you leave before the end. That is the rule this year, and it won’t be less next year. The branch office took on two new clerks this year, both out of the normal school. Their families borrowed the deposit, and they are still paying it back.”

“A hundred dollars,” said Xiaoman. “That’s Mama altering five hundred uniforms.”

“Money is not your worry,” said Su Qi.

“I’ll pay,” said Qiao Zheng. “Godfather will pay.”

“No need,” said Qiusheng.

Everyone looked at him.

“I’ve found a night shift lately,” he said. “The pay is fair. Xiaoman’s school fees we’ll pay ourselves.”

Xiaoman lowered her head. Su Qi’s spoon stopped above the soup bowl. Soup dripped from the edge of the spoon back into the bowl, one drop, two, and she put the spoon down. Qiao Zheng looked at him for a while before he smiled.

“As stubborn as ever, Teacher.”

“What night shift?” Xiaoman asked.

“Checking cargo.”

“Checking cargo at night?”

“The cargo comes in at night.”

Su Qi stood up, carried the soup bowl round to Qiusheng and ladled him a bowlful, then one for Qiao Zheng, then one for Xiaoman. Her own bowl was half filled when the soup ran out. A family eats at one table, each keeping to himself one thing that cannot be said. Reunion dinners the world over are mostly like that.

The mooncakes were lotus paste, four to the box. Su Qi cut them with a knife, each into four, a little yolk in every piece. Xiaoman picked first, the piece with the most yolk. Qiao Zheng took a piece, bit into it and said the cake shop in Upper Town had put in less lotus paste this year. Qiusheng took a piece, put it beside his bowl and did not touch it.

“Papa, you’re not eating.”

“No hurry.”

Qiao Zheng took one pomelo out of the net bag, put it on the table and asked Su Qi for the loan of a knife. Su Qi passed him the knife that had cut the mooncakes, and then a damp cloth. He stood the pomelo on the table and drew the point of the knife down from the top, one cut, a turn, another cut, eight segments in all, and peeled the rind down segment by segment without breaking it at the base. Halfway through he stopped. His nostrils twitched.

“Osmanthus.”

“Picked on the back hill,” said Su Qi.

“What will you make with it, Ma’am?”

Su Qi tore off a segment of pomelo and put it in Xiaoman’s bowl. “Put it up in sugar.”

Xiaoman opened her mouth. Su Qi put another segment in her bowl. Xiaoman shut her mouth and ate pomelo.

Qiusheng picked up the piece of mooncake from beside his bowl and ate it.

---

After dinner they all went out onto the balcony to look at the moon.

Qiusheng took his coat from the back of the rattan chair and put it on. Su Qi gave it a look and went out first. She took down from the rail the bamboo pole she had brought in during the day and stood it in the corner. By the rail was a small stool, and on the stool a dish of mooncake, a dish of water caltrops, an uncut pomelo, and three sticks of incense stuck in a small bowl of rice. She lit the incense and bowed three times. Xiaoman bowed after her, and when she had done she looked round at her godfather. Qiao Zheng stood by the balcony door and said an inspector did not bow to such things.

Su Qi took no notice of him and looked round at Qiusheng. “You bow too.”

Qiusheng bowed once. He straightened up, something moved in his throat, and he swallowed the cough back down.

The balcony was narrow, a single run of rail. Qiao Zheng and Xiaoman stood at the end by the bedrooms. Xiaoman had brought the dictionary out too, and turned its pages by the light from indoors. Su Qi and Qiusheng were at the other end, Qiusheng’s hand on the rail, Su Qi on his right, still holding the damp cloth that had wiped the knife. Down the slope children were walking with lanterns, the candles swaying inside the paper, and at the foot of the slope they turned one by one into the lanes. Mrs Chen’s wireless downstairs was playing opera, and the gongs came up through the floorboards. The smoke of the three incense sticks twisted into one strand above the rail and broke whenever the wind came.

There was no fog this Mid-Autumn. The moon was big and round and hung above the cranes on the docks, as if one of them had hoisted it up there. The lamps of the docks did not show under the moonlight. There was only that one black shape, and one point of light beneath it.

The rind left from the pomelo Qiao Zheng had cut was all in one piece, eight segments joined at the base. He put it on Xiaoman’s head. Xiaoman rocked with laughter and the rind fell off. She picked it up and put it on again and wanted Qiao Zheng to wear it too. Qiao Zheng said an inspector did not wear such things.

Xiaoman turned back to the first few pages of the dictionary. “Godfather, how do you learn *vocabulary*?”

“Begin at A. When you’ve learned a page, turn down the corner.”

“Papa says get the easy ones by heart first.”

“Then get the easy ones first,” said Qiao Zheng.

“Godfather, *harbour*.”

“A port.”

“*Fog*.”

“Thick mist.”

“*Inspector*.”

“An officer who inspects,” said Qiao Zheng. “A man who looks into things.”

“That’s what Papa said too.”

Qiao Zheng looked at Qiusheng across the balcony and smiled a little.

Su Qi went indoors to pour tea, and Xiaoman followed her in to fetch a dish for the pomelo. Qiao Zheng came over to this end, laid both hands on the rail and looked at the docks.

“Teacher, those above are pressing hard about White Dew. Two official letters have come from the provincial capital, asking how Fog Harbour suddenly has a bootleg that sends nobody blind.”

“What did you answer?”

“I wrote what was true. Methanol nil, source unknown,” said Qiao Zheng. “But an official letter doesn’t want source unknown. An official letter wants an address, and a name.”

Qiusheng looked at the cranes. The incense on the stool had burned down a length.

“I answered that when I’ve found it out I shall of course write it,” said Qiao Zheng. “Teacher, when I write a report, what goes in the first line is what you taught me. First what you saw, then what you measured, and only last what you think. The part for what I think, I have left blank to this day.”

The point of light under the cranes, half a harbour away, was no bigger than the tip of an incense stick. Qiusheng’s fingers passed along the rail, from one upright to the next. The wood was dry. The day’s sun was still in it.

Qiao Zheng waited a while.

“The second letter was shorter than the first,” he said. “The short ones press hardest.”

He said no more. He took both hands off the rail, touched them to the seams of his trousers and laid them back. Down the slope one last lantern was left. It turned into a lane and was gone. The gongs downstairs quickened for a spell and stopped.

Su Qi came out with the tea, a cup each. Qiao Zheng took his, said thank you, Ma’am, and went back to Xiaoman’s end. Xiaoman held out the dish of pomelo to him. He took a segment. The skin had not come off clean, and he picked it away bit by bit with a fingernail.

“Godfather, I’ll act it for you.” Xiaoman put the dictionary on the stool and stood at the middle of the rail with her back to the docks. “You’re all the audience.”

Qiao Zheng put the pomelo back in the dish and stood up straight. Su Qi leaned on the frame of the balcony door, the damp cloth over her wrist. Qiusheng’s hand was still on the rail.

Xiaoman turned her head towards the foot of the slope and looked for a while. “The boat hasn’t come yet.”

She shifted to stand on the other foot. “The boat hasn’t come yet.”

For the third line she did not look down the slope. She looked at her own hands. “The boat hasn’t come yet.”

“The second was good,” said Qiao Zheng. “The first was too hurried, as if you were running for the boat.”

“The third sounded like scolding,” said Su Qi.

“Our teacher says I should cry on the third.”

“The one who waits for the boat doesn’t cry,” said Su Qi. “Once she cries she has stopped waiting.”

Xiaoman stood before the rail with her hands hanging at her sides and said the three lines over again, and this time she did not change feet. When she had done she went for the dictionary. Qiao Zheng clapped twice, very softly, for fear of disturbing downstairs.

Su Qi came to Qiusheng’s side and said quietly, “You’ve grown thin lately.”

“It’s the heat.”

“It’s Mid-Autumn,” said Su Qi. “The heat went long ago.”

The point of light was still there under the cranes. It did not move.

“Qiusheng,” said Su Qi, her voice lower still, “what is it you really do on the night shift?”

He turned to look at her. Su Qi was not looking at him. She was looking at the moon, and the side of her face was very calm in the moonlight, so calm it frightened him.

“Checking cargo,” he said.

“Which trading house?”

“On the docks.”

Su Qi nodded and asked no more. After a while she put out her hand and pulled a loose thread from his coat. She rolled the thread between her fingertips and did not throw it away.

“There’s a smell on your clothes,” she said. “Like fish, and like rice wine. I’ve washed them twice and it won’t come out.”

At the other end Xiaoman read out a word and Qiao Zheng could not answer. He said he would look it up when he got back. Xiaoman shut the dictionary and said there were some even Godfather did not know. The incense had burned halfway. The ash bent over and dropped onto the mooncake.

---

Qiao Zheng left at ten. He put on his shoes at the door and took his coat down from behind it. Su Qi put the two pomelos that were left back in the net bag for him to take, and he would not have them; she wrapped the two mooncakes left in the box and pushed them into his hand, and he took them. Xiaoman said Godfather must test her next time he came. Qiusheng saw him to the head of the stairs.

The stairwell had one lamp, on the half-landing below, shining on the banister. Qiao Zheng went down three steps, talking as he went.

“Go to the play, Teacher. What she said to you was ‘have to come’. What she said to me was ‘come too’.”

“Mm.”

“Teacher.” Qiao Zheng stopped on the stairs and looked back. “This matter of White Dew. I shall follow it to the end.”

“That’s your job.”

“Yes,” said Qiao Zheng. “But I want you to know that whoever it is that makes White Dew, I respect him. He has saved a lot of people. A lot fewer died on the docks this month, and I know why.”

He went down two more steps and stopped again, his back to the head of the stairs.

“But the law is the law. Teacher, you taught me that too.”

The packet of mooncakes went over to his left hand, and his right hand rested on the banister. He did not look back.

“I’ve been thinking. Whether a man is any good, I suppose, can’t be hidden either.”

The leather shoes went on down, step by step, not fast, and not slowing either. Qiusheng stood at the head of the stairs and watched his back disappear round the turn, and listened to the sound until the street door closed below. After the door had closed, far off down the slope someone let off firecrackers, one string. The stairwell lamp was still lit, shining on the empty banister.

---

He went back out onto the balcony. The moon had climbed very high, and the cranes on the docks threw long shadows in its light. Su Qi and Xiaoman were clearing the bowls, and the kitchen tap ran loud. The newspaper was still on the table with the osmanthus spread on it, the scent fainter than it had been in the afternoon.

When the bowls were done Xiaoman went back to her room hugging the dictionary, and the door shut. Su Qi wiped the table, stopped when she came to the edge of the newspaper, and went round it. She sat for a while under the lamp, picked up the sleeve of a uniform, looked at it and put it down again. She took the loose thread out of her apron pocket and laid it on the lid of the sewing box. She did not throw it away. At eleven she put out two of the lamps and said through the balcony door that she was going to bed, the chemist’s would be open tomorrow, he was to remember to buy the cough mixture. He said he was coming.

He did not go in.

The light of the one lamp indoors reached the balcony door and stopped. He looked back once: a newspaper on the table, a layer of flowers on the newspaper, a speck of white on the lid of the sewing box. His cup of tea still stood on the rail, cold. He drank a mouthful and put it on the stool, next to the dish of mooncake from the offering.

Mrs Chen’s wireless stopped. The line of light under Xiaoman’s door stayed until twelve and went out; she was learning the new dictionary, and which letter she had got to he did not know. Over at the docks the point of light under the cranes went out too, and there was only the moonlight. The incense on the stool had burned out. Three bamboo slivers stood in the rice, and ash had fallen all over the dish. He pressed his palm to the right side of his chest, through the coat.

The moon moved from above the cranes to above the breakwater, and then out over the sea. The damp came up on the rail and the wood went cold. A foghorn sounded once, very far off, from the open sea, and not a second time. There was no fog in the harbour. Out at sea there was.

He stood on the balcony until very late, so late that the moon had gone over to the west, before he remembered there was a vat on the boat to be distilled that night.

That was the first night he did not go.

---

The next morning, before she went out to the stall, Su Qi gathered the osmanthus into the white china bowl and covered the mouth of the bowl with a layer of gauze. She folded the newspaper and put it under the bowl, the situations vacant on the inside.

“They’ve had their night in the shade,” she said at the door. “No spirit, so it’s sugar.”

The door shut. Xiaoman left for school at half past seven. On her way out she pushed the dictionary into her satchel, and the satchel bulged.

“Papa, I turned down six corners yesterday,” she said at the door. “Six more today. Godfather says he’ll test me next time he comes.”

“Mm.”

“You didn’t go to the night shift last night.”

“Mm.”

“Mama says you stood there all night.” She shifted the satchel to her other shoulder. “Papa, that’s not how you wait for a boat either.”

The door shut again. On the stairs her feet took two steps at a time, like her godfather’s.

Qiusheng sat in the rattan chair with his coat still on. He had not taken it off the night before. A little after eight there were footsteps on the stairs, very light, and they stopped at the door. A piece of paper was pushed in under the door, folded in four. The footsteps went down, faster than they had come up, and the street door sounded once below.

He did not go to pick it up. He went out onto the balcony. A man was running down the slope, in a singlet, his trouser legs rolled to the calf. He did not slow until the roundabout. He wiped a hand on his trousers, turned into the road to the docks and was gone.

Qiusheng went back indoors, picked up the paper and opened it.

Ah Yue had distilled alone all night. On the paper, in a crooked hand, was written: Tide nearly went out. Made it. Ninety-one. Sorry Teacher, I did pour the heads away.
