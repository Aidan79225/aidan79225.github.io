*November · Start of Winter*

At ten o’clock there were still no customers in the main room of the Nanji. The waiter was turning the upended chairs down one by one. The cloth covers were off the birdcages, and the thrushes had not yet opened their beaks. At the table by the stairs sat Old Qi, his back to the wall, his face to the main door, his tea bowl at his left hand. In the bowl was the cheapest oolong, and the lid sat askew.

Qiusheng came in at the door with Ah Yue behind him. Ah Yue had not slept all night and had not shaved.

Old Qi did not look up.

“They’ll come again,” said Old Qi. “Next time it won’t just be the goods.”

Qiusheng stopped.

“Mr Qi, come up and sit.”

Old Qi glanced at the main door. Outside, under the arcade, the peanut seller had just set out his stall. Old Qi lifted the tea bowl, drank a mouthful and put the bowl down. “I’ll be here.”

Twelve stairs. The private room was the same one. A new layer had been pasted over the old newspapers on the board partition, covering last year’s dates. Counsel Liang was there already, in the host’s seat, his back to the window, a pot of tea and four cups before him, his fountain pen lying across a takings sheet. Qiusheng sat facing him, his back to the partition. Ah Yue did not sit and did not shut the door. He leaned on the door frame, half of him outside the room, where he could see the back of Old Qi’s head at the foot of the stairs.

Counsel Liang spoke first.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset: this has to be reported.”

“Reported?” Ah Yue came up straight off the door frame.

“Not to the Prohibition Bureau. To the detectives.” Counsel Liang drew the pink handkerchief from his pocket and wiped his forehead. “A criminal matter. Stopping a truck on the road, robbery, wounding, any one of them is enough to bring a charge. There is a chit from the bonesetter’s for Ah Shui’s arm. I know the deputy chief of the detective squad, he owes me a favour—”

“Report what?” Ah Yue roared from the door. His voice went down the stairs, and in the main room the waiter raised his head and looked up the stairs. “Tell the police someone’s robbed us of our bootleg?”

“Mr Chen, lower your voice.” Counsel Liang’s fountain pen tapped once on the table. “There are ears downstairs.”

“The detectives will come and ask: what was your truck carrying?” said Qiusheng. “Mr Liang, we can’t go to the police.”

The private room was quiet for a while. Downstairs a thrush called once.

“Then tell Madam Wen,” said Counsel Liang. “It’s her goods the Black Flag took.”

“It’s the Dockworkers’ Union’s goods they took,” said Qiusheng. “Madam Wen’s goods go by the ice truck, and the Black Flag daren’t touch that. What they took is the share we keep back for the docks.”

Counsel Liang took the handkerchief from his forehead, folded it and put it back in his pocket. He picked up the fountain pen and dotted the back of the takings sheet, one dot to a name.

“The detective squad: we cannot. Madam Wen: she will not. The Prohibition Bureau—” The third dot did not come down. “Teacher, I have counted round everyone in Fog Harbour who could settle this score for you, and I cannot get to a fourth.”

Ah Yue slapped his palm on the door frame. “I’ve got five brothers.”

“Four now,” said Counsel Liang.

Ah Yue’s hand stayed on the door frame. He did not take it away. Only when he has been robbed of his goods does a man count exactly how many friends he has.

“Teacher, then what do we do?”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He stood up and walked to the door, and Ah Yue gave way half a step. At the foot of the stairs Old Qi’s tea bowl had come up again.

“Mr Qi?”

Old Qi looked first at the main door, and only then put the bowl down. He came up the stairs, one at a time, not fast. At the door of the private room he did not come in. He stood outside the door frame with his back against the wall of the stairwell. From there he could see the main door below, and he could see the three people in the private room.

“Change the route. Change the hour,” he said.

“Which road?” Ah Yue asked.

“No more Saltflat. Too open. Go by the goods yard.”

“And the fifty bottles for Saltflat?”

“Tell Headman Ah Lu to come to the head of the sea wall for them,” said Old Qi. “By day.”

“There are trains in the goods yard at night.”

“Trains are what we want,” said Old Qi. “Go by the goods yard on the docks. When a train comes through, the barrier at the level crossing comes down. That’s the one place they can’t follow.”

Ah Yue turned his head round. “How do you know the train times?”

“I did thirty years on the railway,” said Old Qi. “I know them with my eyes shut.”

“The tide has its times too,” said Ah Yue. “Teacher copied them on the bulkhead, a line a day. The Black Flag watched the tide and waited, and that’s how they got us.”

“The tide is a clock everyone in Fog Harbour can see. The train times, I’m the only one who has them by heart.”

Counsel Liang had put down his fountain pen. “Mr Qi, are the times on paper?”

“No paper.”

Old Qi turned and went downstairs. The stairs sounded all the way to the bottom, and then came the sound of a tea bowl being lifted.

“When do we change?” Ah Yue called down the stairs.

Below there was a pause. “Tonight.”

---

At six the soy workers above knocked off and the big stove went out, and only then did the lamp go on in the cellar.

The workbench stood under the lamp. Old Qi was not sitting on his upturned vat in the corner. He sat on the long bench at one end of the workbench, his back to the brick wall, his face to the stairs; the tea bowl had been brought up off the floor and stood on the end of the bench. Qiusheng sat at the other end, the notebook open, a pencil lying across it. Ah Yue stood leaning against the pot, his arms folded on his chest. The five boys stood in a line along the row of vats, Ah Chang in the middle, the tallest; Ah Shui at the end, his right arm in a sling across his chest, his left hanging.

Old Qi felt in the pocket of his uniform and brought out a packet of cigarette papers, thin enough to see the light through. He took out a stub of pencil and wrote on the first. Small writing, line after line, till it was full. When he had done he folded the paper in half and held it out.

“Ah Chang.”

Ah Chang took it. Old Qi wrote the second.

“Ah Cai.”

One by one, a paper written and a name called. At the fifth he called no name and held it straight out to the end of the line. Ah Shui took it with his left hand.

“Learn it, then eat it,” said Old Qi.

One of the boys laughed. Old Qi did not laugh, and the boy stopped laughing.

Ah Chang unfolded the paper, held it up under the lamp and read it out. “Five-forty, down, goods. Six-twenty, down. Seven-oh-five, up. Nine-twenty, up, empties. Eleven-ten, down. One sharp, up, the provincial capital—”

“Not out loud,” said Old Qi. “Learn it.”

The cellar went quiet. Five papers in five hands, and here and there a pair of lips moving. Ah Shui laid his paper against the sling that held his arm and read down it with a finger of his left hand pressed to it.

Ah Yue came over from beside the pot. “Mr Qi, what do you mean, eat it?”

“The paper doesn’t stay.”

“And if they learn it wrong?”

“They learn it till it’s right.” Old Qi put the pencil stub back in his pocket. “Ten dollars a month a head.”

“Ten dollars?” Ah Yue wiped his hand down his trousers. “Five of them, fifty dollars, a month! Mr Qi, Old Hai’s two hundred bottles only come to three hundred—”

Old Qi did not look at him. He looked only at Qiusheng, at the other end of the workbench.

“It’s a cost of doing business.”

Qiusheng nodded. He turned to a fresh page of the notebook, borrowed the cigarette paper from Ah Chang’s hand and copied from it. When he had copied it once he copied it again. The third time he did not look at the paper. The paper had already gone back to Ah Chang. In twenty years at the Office he had copied out the boiling-point table three times, and after that he had never needed to look at the one on the wall.

Old Qi watched him copy.

“An assayer’s memory. Always good.”

From the goods yard came a whistle, a long one, muffled through the brick wall and the sauce vats. Old Qi did not raise his head.

“Nine-twenty, up, empties,” he said. “Two minutes late.”

Ah Chang looked down at his own wrist. There was nothing on it.

From the small pocket of his uniform Old Qi brought out a wristwatch. The strap was canvas, worn to a fray at the edges, and there was a crack across the glass. He laid the watch on the workbench and pushed it across towards Ah Chang.

“There has to be a watch on the truck,” he said. “The one beside the driver looks at it. The driver doesn’t.”

Ah Chang picked it up and held it to his ear. It was going. He put it on and buckled the strap at the last hole, and it was still loose.

Old Qi stood up and walked over in front of the line of boys. “Ah Chang.”

Ah Chang recited it. Five-forty down goods, six-twenty down, seven-oh-five up, all the way to the one at three in the morning, without a stop. When he had done, Old Qi said, “Eat.”

Ah Chang put the paper in his mouth. The paper was thin. Two chews and it was gone. He swallowed, and his Adam’s apple moved.

“What does it taste of?” Ah Yue asked.

“Tobacco,” said Ah Chang.

Ah Yue glanced at Old Qi. Old Qi was looking at the stairs.

“Ah Cai.”

Ah Cai got one train wrong, and Old Qi had him say it again from the top. The second time it was right. “Eat.”

Then it was Ah Shui’s turn. He did not look at the paper on the sling. He said it faster than any of them, the figures one on the heels of another. When he had done he peeled the paper off the sling with his left hand and put it in his mouth.

Old Qi gave him a look. “Not bad.”

The five boys went up the stairs. Ah Yue stood at the foot of the stairs and watched them go, then looked back at Qiusheng. Under the lamp the three copies of the timetable lay side by side in the notebook, the writing smaller each time.

“Teacher,” said Ah Yue, “they listen to Old Qi now, not to me.”

Old Qi lifted his tea bowl and drank a mouthful.

That night the truck went out at ten and came back at twelve, by the goods yard. Only when the engine died outside the iron door did Old Qi lift his tea bowl. The first thing Ah Yue said as he came down the stairs: “The eleven-ten. I watched it go by from this side of the barrier.” Old Qi did not answer.

---

Early on the morning of 14 November Ah Yue came down the stairs with a coat of ash on his shoes.

“Teacher, the Nanji’s burned.”

Qiusheng wiped the thermometer in his hand dry and put it back in the vat.

The fog had not yet lifted in the Old Town. Under the arcade at the edge of the Tin Hau Temple square a ring of people had gathered. Past them, half the front of the Nanji was black. The signboard was one long plank, hung under the eaves of the arcade. It had caught from the left, and the characters for house, for tea and for Ji had burned away one after another. At the edge of the Nan the fire had stopped. There the plank had snapped, and the half that was left hung down. One pillar of the arcade was blackened with smoke. On the ground stood an iron bucket, its sides buckled by the fire, a finger’s depth of water still in the bottom. Half the peanut seller’s stall had burned. The bamboo tray was charred and the ground was strewn with peanut shells that cracked underfoot.

The waiter squatted in the doorway, scrubbing the flagstones with a brush. The water was black.

“Two in the morning,” he said, not looking up. “The night watchman beat his gong. I came down from upstairs and the fire was already up in the eaves. Two men, faces covered, ran off towards the roundabout. The bucket they threw the stuff out of is just here.” He pointed at the iron bucket with the handle of the brush.

Qiusheng squatted down. Beside the bucket was a piece of charred wood, a fragment of the signboard. He picked it up, brought it under his nose and wafted once with his palm. Smoke. Under the smoke a thread of sweetness, sharp in the nose.

“Firewater,” he said.

Only then did the waiter look up. “How do you know, sir?”

Qiusheng put the piece of wood back on the ground.

The incense burner of the Tin Hau Temple smoked as on any other day, and the fire had not reached the couplet at the temple gate. The peanut seller stood by what was left of his stall with a handful of peanuts in his fist, shelling them one by one and dropping the shells into the charred bamboo tray.

Qiusheng crossed the main room and went out by the back door. The alley behind was narrow, wall on both sides, three stone steps, a coat of moss on the steps. Old Qi stood in the middle of the alley with his back to the back door, looking at the roundabout at the alley’s mouth. He heard the door and did not turn.

“There’s no lamp here,” he said.

Qiusheng glanced at the stone steps. On the one where Ah Lian sat every night, a patch of the moss had been worn away.

“Fire at the front door. Dark at the back,” said Old Qi. He walked two paces towards the mouth of the alley, stopped, and walked back. “The bolt is on the inside.”

“Mm.”

Old Qi shot the bolt of the back door, tried it, and drew it again. He went round to the front by the mouth of the alley.

---

At noon in the private room the window was open, and the smell of burning came in from outside and covered the smell of the tea.

Counsel Liang sat in the host’s seat, his handkerchief spread on his knee, wet. Qiusheng sat facing him. Ah Yue had sat down, on the stool by the door, both hands clamped between his knees. Downstairs at Old Qi’s place the tea bowl was still at the left hand, and the man was not there. The waiter said Mr Qi was in the alley behind.

“The insurance will pay,” said Counsel Liang.

He did not say whose insurance.

Ah Yue raised his head. “Close. Close for three days, till it blows over. There isn’t even a signboard. What is there to open?”

“We open as usual,” said Qiusheng.

“Teacher—”

“The docks mustn’t say the Teacher is scared of the Black Flag.”

Ah Yue’s hands came out from between his knees, and went back.

Counsel Liang picked up the handkerchief and wiped his forehead once. “Teacher, the Black Flag cannot find the cellar. They can find the teahouse. The teahouse’s signboard hangs at the edge of the Tin Hau Temple square, where all Fog Harbour can see it. They burned the signboard for the docks to see.”

“Mm.”

“What they burn next time, I would not care to reckon for you.”

The stairs sounded. Old Qi came up and stood outside the door frame, a film of fog on the shoulders of his railway uniform. He glanced at the main door, then at the private room.

“Who’s last out of the back door at night?”

Nobody in the private room answered. From the head of the stairs the waiter said, “Miss Ah Lian. She sings her last song and goes out by the back door.”

Old Qi nodded once and went downstairs. Ah Yue looked at the head of the stairs. His hands came out from between his knees and went to wipe down his trousers, and stopped halfway.

“Mr Chen, what time do you finish at night?” Counsel Liang asked.

“Depends on the truck.”

“Tell her to wait for you. To wait inside.”

“She waits every night,” said Ah Yue. “In the alley behind.”

Counsel Liang put the handkerchief away and did not go on.

---

At three in the afternoon the Nanji opened as usual. Three or four tables of customers in the main room, half the usual number, and every one who came to the door first looked up at the half signboard, then lowered his head and came in. The thrushes hopped in their cages, and nobody had wiped the smoke off the pillar. Old Qi was back at the table by the stairs, with fresh hot tea in the tea bowl. Qiusheng sat at the next table, his back to the board partition, a bowl of tea before him, undrunk.

On the little stage in the corner Ah Lian held her pipa, her head bent, tuning the strings. She looked up. There was light in the doorway. She began to sing.

At the third line a wind got up outside, and the smell of burning came in and went once round the main room. She stopped for a beat. The beat hung there empty, the strings of the pipa still sounding. She went on and made up the lost beat, and the tune did not falter. Nobody in the main room looked up. Only Ah Yue, at the counter, turned round.

A man in a long gown stopped in the doorway. Gold-rimmed spectacles, a book held to his chest, the corners of the book capped with brass. Madam Wen’s bookkeeper. He did not come in. He stood under the arcade and looked first at the half signboard, then all round the main room, and his eyes stopped a moment at Qiusheng’s table. The book stayed shut. He turned and went.

Old Qi’s tea bowl stopped halfway up. He watched the man’s back cross the square, and only then put the bowl down.

Qiusheng looked at the water mark on the ground by the door. The scrubbing had left a patch whiter than the flagstones beside it.

---

The South Slope market opened at six in the morning.

Su Qi’s stall was at the very back of the market, against the wall: a cutting table, a treadle sewing machine, a charcoal iron resting on an iron stand, and under the iron a pad of cloth scorched at one corner. Opposite was the fish stall. The ice was melting, and the water ran along the cracks between the flagstones as far as the front of her stall, where she kept it off with a board. On the left was Ah Gui’s greens stall; on the right they sold sundries. The market in the morning was wet. The reek of fish, the damp off the vegetable leaves, the scorch of charcoal, layer on layer.

She sat behind the sewing machine with three pins held in her mouth, points outward. The treadle went stroke after stroke, and the cloth passed under the needle. The moment it stopped the whole stall went quiet, and the ice on the fish stall could be heard dripping.

A girl from the girls’ school came first, her satchel still on her shoulder.

“Auntie He, let the skirt down an inch.”

“It can’t be done.” Su Qi turned the hem up and measured the turning with her thumb. “There’s only half an inch left in the hem.”

“Half an inch, then.”

Su Qi took a pin from her mouth and pinned it in the hem, and the tailor’s chalk drew one line on the cloth. “Wednesday. Twenty cents.”

“Xiaoman’s in Scene Three,” said the girl. “The one who stands and doesn’t move. Our teacher says she stands best of anyone.”

“Mm.”

“Are you going to see it, Auntie He?”

“I’ve the stall.” Su Qi folded the skirt and put it in the basket under the cutting table. The girl ran off.

The postman came to have a shoulder badge sewn back on. Green uniform, the badge off one shoulder and held on with a safety pin. He stood in front of the stall and would not sit, and said he had a quarter of an hour to catch his bus at the roundabout. Su Qi unpicked the badge on that side, matched it against the other, and put the needle through. Three stitches. She bit off the thread.

“A lot of letters today,” said the postman. “Down the Docks way, twice as many registered as usual. Sent by the Prohibition Bureau.”

“Ten cents.”

The postman paid, set his cap straight and went.

Next was a young man in a khaki uniform, a brass badge pinned at his collar, a bottle with a cross struck through it; under his arm, a folded pair of uniform trousers. He put the trousers on the cutting table and took off his jacket. The waistband of the pair he had on would not button, and was held in with his belt.

“Missus, let the waist out an inch. They feed us well at the Bureau.”

Su Qi stood up, took the tape measure from her neck, passed it once round his waist, pinched it at a figure, and passed it round again. She did not look at the badge.

“An inch and a half,” she said.

“An inch and a half it is.” The young man laughed. “Missus, that Teacher on the docks, have you heard of him?”

Su Qi sat down again behind the sewing machine, pulled the pair on the cutting table towards her and drew two lines of tailor’s chalk on the waistband.

“Which teacher.”

“The one who makes the liquor,” said the young man. “Our Inspector Qiao says there’s only the one man in all Fog Harbour who knows how. He has us go down to the docks once a week and buy some, and bring it back and assay it, and every time it’s nil. The Inspector says he’d like to meet this man.”

“Come for them on Thursday,” said Su Qi. “Thirty cents.”

The young man put on his jacket and went. The treadle started up again, stroke after stroke.

Ah Gui came over from the greens stall next door and leaned both elbows on the edge of Su Qi’s cutting table. She smelled of spring onions.

“Ah Qi, that man of mine hasn’t been drunk once this month.”

“Mm.”

“He’s gone over to White Dew. A bottle a week. He says it goes down and his head doesn’t ache, and next day he’s carrying the same as ever.” Ah Gui lowered her voice. “They say a schoolteacher makes it. On the docks they all call him Teacher. Nobody’s seen his face.”

Su Qi’s needle did not stop.

“How much a bottle?”

“A dollar fifty. Old Hai at the union sells it by the cup, five cents a cup.”

“Mm.”

Ah Gui waited a moment, saw she was not going to ask anything more, and went back to her own stall. Su Qi drew the cloth out from under the needle, cut the thread, pulled off the loose end and rolled it between her fingertips. Across the way the ice water from the fish stall was still running.

---

At four in the afternoon half the stalls in the market were left. Qiusheng came for the suit.

The suit hung on the wall behind the stall. The day he brought it in there had been a split in the bottom of the outside pocket. Su Qi had mended it, from the inside, so that it did not show. She did not get up. On her knee was a winter uniform from the girls’ school, the cuff unpicked. She was letting the sleeve down, a line of tailor’s chalk across the cuff and the needle following the line. She had pins in her mouth again, two, points outward.

Qiusheng stood in front of the cutting table. Under the table was a small stool. She pushed the stool out half an inch with her foot. He did not sit.

“It’s mended,” she said. The pins were still in her mouth, and the words came out between her teeth. “What did you have in the pocket. Something heavy.”

“A book.”

Qiusheng felt in his shirt pocket for twenty cents and put it on the cutting table. Su Qi glanced at it and did not take it.

“I don’t charge for mending a pocket.”

The money stayed on the cutting table. She turned back the facing of the cuff and put the needle through. The treadle was still and the stall was very quiet. The fish stall had packed up, and the water on the flagstones had dried to a white mark.

She took the two pins from her mouth and pinned them on the white line at the cuff, one beside the other.

“You smell of lees,” she said. “Nobody at the Assay Office smells of lees.”

Qiusheng’s hand rested on the edge of the cutting table. There was a white line of tailor’s chalk on the table, where the chalk had run off the skirt hem that morning.

“You paid Xiaoman’s fees. And Dr Lin’s medicine. I have altered other people’s uniforms for twenty years, twenty cents each.”

She drew the thread right through and pressed her hand once on the cloth. Qiusheng watched her hand.

“I won’t ask what you do. One question: will Qiao Zheng knock on our door one day?”

On the right, the owner of the sundries stall was putting up his shutter boards, one by one.

“If he does, take Xiaoman and go.”

Su Qi’s needle stopped. She drew the needle out of the cloth, thread and all, wound the thread round her finger, twice round, and put it in the sewing box. She pulled the two pins from the cuff and stuck them back in the pincushion. She shut the lid of the box.

“He Qiusheng, when did you learn to say things like that.”

She took the suit down from the wall, folded it, laid it on the cutting table and pushed it across. Qiusheng picked it up and hung it over his arm. He pressed his palm once to the right side of his chest. He did not cough.

A woman came over from the market entrance with a pair of trousers in her hand and asked how much to take up the legs. Su Qi said twenty cents, tomorrow. The woman put twenty cents on the cutting table, next to the twenty cents already there. Su Qi picked up the second twenty cents and pulled open the drawer under the table. A biscuit tin. She lifted the lid and put the money in. Pressed flat in the bottom of the tin were a few banknotes, a string of copper coins, and an old prescription folded in four, its corners gone soft, no stamp on the top line.

She pressed the lid on. The first twenty cents was still on the cutting table, next to the white line of tailor’s chalk.

On Sunday afternoon a black motor car stopped at the mouth of the Futai siding, a foreign crest on its door.

Out of it got a red-faced old foreigner, white beard trimmed neat, a three-piece suit, a watch chain across the waistcoat. He would not let the driver come with him. He carried a wicker picnic basket himself and walked along the siding into the yard. On Sundays the sauce was not turned. The bamboo lids were on the vats and there was no one among them; the watchdog lay in the doorway of the works building, raised its head once, and did not bark. The bookkeeper had sent word the day before: Mr Smith of the Consulate, on Sunday. The iron door of the cellar had been left for him, unlocked.

He took the stairs slowly, his leather shoes pausing on each one. At the bottom he stood on the last stair and looked first at the row of vats, then at the pot, then at Qiusheng sitting at the workbench.

“Mr Ho,” he said.

Qiusheng stood up.

Smith set the picnic basket on the workbench and lifted the lid. Inside were a glass jar, black, with cloth tied over its mouth; a bottle with no label, the spirit in it dark brown; two small glasses wrapped in a napkin. He took the things out and set them in a row, and only then sat down.

“This, molasses.” He pointed at the glass jar. His Chinese was slow, one word at a time. “Madam Wen’s ship, last month, it brought back. One drum, I take a little.”

“Mm.”

“This,” and he picked up the bottle of dark brown spirit, “I make. Twenty-eight years ago, on the ship.”

He poured two glasses, pushed one to Qiusheng and held the other. He did not drink. He smelled first, his nose resting a moment over the rim.

Qiusheng did not drink either. He held the glass up under the lamp. The spirit clung to the glass, slowly. He smelled. Burnt sugar on the outside, wood beneath it, and beneath the wood another layer, sweet, the sweetness of ripe fruit left out overnight.

“Banana,” he said.

Smith’s red face went redder. “Yes. Banana. This word, in Chinese, I do not know.”

He put down the glass, took a small book from the basket and opened it. It was in English, the writing very small.

“Molasses, with water. One part molasses, three parts water, not more. Add a handful of—” He stopped, looking for the word. “Of the vat before. At the bottom. The leavings.”

“Dregs.”

“Dregs.” He nodded, and wrote two square characters in the book, very slowly. “Add dregs. It ferments fast, it ferments wild. The vat will make a noise, night after night, like a drunk man singing.”

“How many days does it ferment?”

“Seven day,” said Smith. He thought. “Seven days. One day more, it is sour. On the ship it is hot, five days.”

“At how many degrees do you stop?”

Smith turned a page of the book. On it was a drawing of a pot, with figures written beside it, in English. He looked at it for a while.

“On the ship there is not this.” He pointed at the thermometer Qiusheng had stuck in at the side of a vat. “On the ship, the nose. The nose says good, it is good.”

Qiusheng listened. He put the glass back on the workbench.

“You do not drink, Mr Ho?”

“An assayer does not drink.”

Smith nodded, as if he had understood, and as if he had not. His own glass stood beside the book, and he had not touched it either.

“Mr Ho, your White Dew, I have drunk it,” said Smith. “At the Consulate there is someone who buys. Clean. I was ten years on the ship, and I never drank a spirit so clean.” He thought, and said something in English, quickly: “*The rum never lies. The label does.*”

Qiusheng understood half of it. The word *rum* he had seen at the Office, on the labels of imported liquor they had seized.

Smith looked at his face and said it again in Chinese, slowly.

“The spirit, it does not lie. The one that lies is the label. This is what we say, on the ship.”

Qiusheng nodded. Against the wall at the far end of the workbench stood a crate, unloaded from the ice truck the week before, with square bottles in it, not yet opened; beside the crate three tin drums, sealed, unloaded the same day, a ring of sugar crusted round each. He did not look at the crate.

“Mr Smith,” he said, “why did you leave the ship?”

Smith lifted the glass, and this time he drank, a small mouthful.

“When the spirit on the ship is all drunk, the man is old.”

He put the glass down, drew his watch from his waistcoat pocket, glanced at it and put it back.

“Next Sunday, I come to see the vat.”

He left the molasses, and the bottle too, and went off carrying the basket. His shoes sounded on the stairs one by one, quicker than when he came down.

At the very back of the cellar Old Lu was squatting by the three bricks, patting a brick with the flat of his hand. From first to last he had not made a sound. Only when the shoes had reached the top did he stand up.

“Foreigners make liquor too?”

“They do.”

“A sight too sweet,” said Old Lu. He walked towards the stairs, and halfway there he turned his head. “Those three crocks of ours. Don’t you let him smell them.”

That vat was filled the same night. The seal of one tin drum was prised open and the molasses poured into the vat, black, thick, clinging to the wall of the vat and not running down, with a burnt sweetness. Water was sluiced in and stirred, and a handful of dregs from the last vat scattered over. By the second night the vat was making a noise. Not the small sound of a rice vat. A string of gurgles, a stop, another string, the froth rushing to the mouth of the vat and lifting the cloth cover. Every night Qiusheng raised a corner and smelled. On the third night he wrote in the notebook: *Noise, all night.*

On Sunday Smith came again. This time the jacket of the suit stayed in the car and his shirtsleeves were rolled to the elbow. On his forearm was a faded blue tattoo, done on the ship. He did not sit. He stood by the vat, laid his ear against its wall and listened for a while, and straightened up, and the red of his face was deeper.

The glass that came off the pot he held up under the lamp. He looked at it, smelled it, drank it. Caramel, banana, no wood. He put the glass down and looked at Qiusheng, very seriously.

“Can it be a little darker?”

Qiusheng glanced at the crate of square bottles.

“We’ll see.”

---

On the Tuesday between the two Sundays he went to the surgery on South Slope.

There was only one old man on the waiting bench. Dr Lin listened to Qiusheng’s chest first. The stethoscope stayed a long time on the right side, and stopped a moment on the left too. He hung the stethoscope back round his neck. He did not take out the film.

“Mr He, last time I said two weeks.”

“Mm.”

“It has been four months.”

Dr Lin pulled open the drawer and took out the ledger. He opened it, shut it, and opened it again. He turned the ledger round and pushed it in front of Qiusheng. In the ledger, under the name He Qiusheng, were two lines: medicine, October; medicine, November. After each was written cash, and the payer’s column was the same both times: Nanji Teahouse.

“Mr He, if the Prohibition Bureau comes asking, I won’t lie,” said Dr Lin.

Qiusheng looked at the two lines.

“Tell them the truth.”

Dr Lin took the ledger back, shut it and put it in the drawer. He pulled the prescription pad over and wrote the names of medicines, two lines.

“I’ll give you the same medicine for another month,” he said. “A new injection has reached the provincial capital. It works on the cavities. A course of twelve. It is not cheap.”

“Mm.”

“Every two weeks you come in to have air pumped. Without it, the lung on that side gets no rest.” The butt of his pencil tapped once on the desk. “Mr He, next Tuesday.”

“We’ll see.”

Dr Lin gave him a look, tore off the prescription and held it out.

The next Tuesday he was in the cellar.

---

20 November, a Thursday. It got dark early. At half past five Qiusheng went back to South Slope to change his clothes. He had to leave at six.

Xiaoman stood at the window of the front room with her back to the room, a sheet of paper in her hand. The paper hung by her leg, unread. Outside the window were the lamps at the foot of the slope, coming on one by one. She was saying, “The boat hasn’t come yet.” A pause. “The boat hasn’t come yet.” The third line she did not say aloud. Her lips moved.

Qiusheng was changing his shoes beside the rattan chair.

“Papa, are you home tonight?” She did not turn round. She might have been asking whether there would be fog tomorrow.

“I have to go in tonight.”

“Next Thursday,” said Xiaoman, still looking out of the window. “The twenty-seventh. The hall, seven o’clock. Scene Three is me.”

“I’ll come.”

“Godfather can’t get leave. He sent word by the clerk.” She folded the paper and put it in her skirt pocket. “So there’s only you.”

“I’ll come.”

Only then did she turn her head and look at him, once, and turn back. Su Qi was in the kitchen. The tap was running, and she had not turned it down.

---

On the twenty-seventh Qiusheng was at Futai by the afternoon.

The two hundred bottles were packed, twenty crates, stacked beside the goods track. Qiusheng was at the workbench, the notebook open. Old Qi sat on the upturned vat in the corner, his back to the wall, his face to the stairs, his tea bowl on the floor at his left hand. Ah Chang stood at the foot of the stairs. Ah Yue was taking the truck out at five. One run to the union, he said, one to the Paramount, back before seven; Teacher would leave at half past six and be in time. He tore the day’s page off the tear-off calendar and slapped it on the lid of the pot: the twenty-seventh.

“Teacher, you go on ahead.”

“I’ll wait till you’re back.”

“Wait for me? What for?”

“I’ll wait.”

Ah Chang raised his wrist, glanced at the watch and lowered it. Old Qi said one thing: “Seven-oh-five, up.”

“Back before seven,” said Ah Yue.

Old Qi did not answer.

The truck drove off from outside the iron door. The gravel sounded for a while and died away. Old Qi had not got in. Qiusheng’s notebook lay open, not a word written in it.

Six o’clock. Above, they knocked off, and the big stove went out. Six-twenty, a whistle from the goods yard.

“Six-twenty, down,” said Old Qi. He did not raise his head. “They’ll have done unloading at the union.”

Half past six. Qiusheng shut the notebook, put on his coat and walked to the foot of the stairs. He stood there. Old Qi did not move.

The calendar page lay on the lid of the pot, the twenty-seventh, with the day’s tides printed at the bottom. Up in the sauce-vat yard the dog barked once and stopped. A wind came in through the vent windows, smelling of sauce, lifted a corner of the page and let it drop.

Six-forty. Six-fifty.

“There’s a checkpoint at the roundabout,” said Old Qi.

Qiusheng did up the buttons of his coat, and undid one.

Seven o’clock. Qiusheng pressed his palm to the right side of his chest. The iron door at the head of the stairs was shut. Under the door a line, white. The fog had come up outside.

Five past seven, a whistle. A long one, coming from the goods-yard end, nearer than usual.

Old Qi’s tea bowl went down.

“Seven-oh-five, up,” he said. He glanced at the stairs. “The barrier’s down.”

---

The truck was on the lane between the warehouses. The fog was thick. The headlamps reached ten feet, and beyond that it was white. Ah Yue held the wheel, both hands. Ah Chang sat beside him, his left wrist resting on his knee.

At the corner of Warehouse No. 7 there was a blur of light in the mirror, two lamps, yellow. The light came round the corner after them, and came closer. Behind the lamps was a black saloon, its window wound down, a man leaning half out.

Ah Chang raised his wrist and glanced at the watch.

“Ah Yue!”

Ah Yue did not lift his foot. Ahead nothing could be seen, only a point of red, flashing. Once. Again. Under the red point a level bar of shadow was sinking. The whistle sounded on their right, a long one, coming along the ground. The train was still in the fog. The sound came first.

The red lamp flashing. The barrier coming down. The whistle.

In the instant the truck tore across the level crossing, the arm of the barrier scraped the cab roof with a shriek that set the teeth on edge. The next second the engine thundered past behind them, one wagon, two, three, the wagons joined into a wall of iron.

At the top of South Slope the lights of the girls’ school hall went out at that moment, and the curtain opened.

The black saloon stopped on the far side of the barrier, cut off by the train.

Ah Yue pulled the truck in behind a warehouse, slumped over the wheel and laughed, laughed till he could not breathe.

---

It was seven-twenty when the truck got back outside the iron door. Old Qi’s tea bowl came up, and went down again.

Ah Yue came jumping down the stairs two at a time, and at the bottom he was still laughing. He told Qiusheng the whole thing without drawing breath, and when he came to the arm of the barrier his hand cut across above his head. Ah Chang came behind, one hand pressed over the watch on his other wrist, saying nothing. Qiusheng heard him out. He did not laugh.

“Don’t do that again,” he said.

“Why not? We shook them off!”

“If the barrier had been a little lower,” said Qiusheng. “If the train had been a second sooner.”

Slowly Ah Yue’s smile went.

“Teacher,” he said, “you’re sounding more like my mother every day.”

That was their last good night.

---

It was early the next morning when he got back to South Slope.

One lamp in the front room, on all night, a film of dust on the bulb. On the table a ticket, red paper, printed with the name of the girls’ school and the words “The Landing”. It had not been torn. The stub was still joined to the ticket. Su Qi sat under the lamp sewing on a button. It was on the uniform Xiaoman had taken off, and the thread went three times round under the button. She did not look up.

“You promised her.”

Qiusheng stood in the doorway. He had not taken off his shoes.

The door of Xiaoman’s room opened. She was dressed in her uniform, her satchel on her shoulder. She walked to the table, picked up the ticket, looked at the stub end, walked to the chest of drawers, pulled open the top drawer, put it in and pushed the drawer shut. She did not look at him. The door opened and closed, and her steps on the stairs went two at a time.

Xiaoman put the play’s ticket stub in a drawer and never took it out again.

Su Qi bit off the thread, folded the uniform and put it in the cloth bundle.

---

The last night of November. The second crate, unloaded from the ice truck on Thursday, stood at the foot of the stairs. In it were square bottles, mouths up, forty-eight to the crate. There was a small wooden box besides, with a sheaf of labels in it, the stag printed at the top and English underneath; a paper packet of caramel colouring; a bag of wood chips. The crate by the wall, the one that had come first, Ah Yue walked past every day, and it had never been opened.

Qiusheng cleared the workbench. The empty square bottles stood in a row at one end, a little cup of caramel colouring dissolved in water at the other, and between them an earthenware basin of water steeped in wood chips, brown. The base spirit was from the pot he had run that afternoon, and he had not begun to collect until the mercury passed seventy-eight. Before he filled a bottle he poured a little of its spirit into a glass, held it up to the lamp, wafted once with his palm, and smelled. Then he mixed. Two drops of caramel colouring, a spoonful of the wood-chip water, a stir. When the colour was right he filled the bottle and pushed in the cork.

He had cut the heads from every bottle and assayed every one, and they were far cleaner than anything from Madam Wen’s factory in the South Seas. But each time he sealed a bottle, he did not look at the stag.

It was Ah Yue who stuck on the labels. He came back from the truck in the middle of the night, saw the row of square bottles on the workbench, and stopped.

“Teacher, what’s this?”

“Madam Wen’s goods.”

Ah Yue picked up a bottle and looked at it against the lamp. Caramel-coloured. He swirled it. It clung to the glass. He dipped a label in the paste, stuck it on and smoothed it flat with his palm, the stag facing him.

“Imported liquor?”

“Mm.”

“How much a bottle?”

“She sells it at twelve dollars.”

Ah Yue’s hand stopped a moment, then smoothed the second one flat. Ninety-six bottles, ninety-six labels. By the last one the paste had dried on his fingers. He packed the square bottles back into the crates row by row, stags upward, and shut the lids. He asked nothing more. It was the first lie Qiusheng told him.

After Ah Yue had gone there was one lamp left in the cellar. Qiusheng set three small glasses in a row under the lamp: rice wine, sorghum, rum. He smelled them one by one. The rice wine was clean, the sorghum had backbone, the rum had a deep sweetness.

He always felt that something was missing, and always felt that nothing was missing. It was only that they had not yet been put together.
