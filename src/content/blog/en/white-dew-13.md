*March · Awakening of Insects*

In spring Headman Ah Lu sent a boatload of fish and a message.

The Saturday after Rain Water, before it was light, the truck came back from the head of the sea wall. The sound of the chain came first, then the smell of fish, pouring in from the iron door at the goods-yard end all the way to the foot of the stairs. Ah Yue came down, one step heavy and one light. His right foot had not mended yet. He carried a bamboo basket in his hand, its mouth covered with seaweed.

“Teacher. From Uncle Ah Lu.” He set the basket down by the workbench. “The north wind’s dropped, the boats have been out, first net. A boatload. Eight baskets on the truck. His men helped carry them over the flats.”

Qiusheng lifted the seaweed. The fish were silver, their gills lifting and closing, still alive.

“What did he say?”

Ah Yue put on Headman Ah Lu’s way of speaking, slow, stopping twice in a sentence.

“From now on, Teacher’s trucks go where they like, on the Saltflat mudflats.”

Qiusheng looked at the fish in the basket.

“There was one thing more,” said Ah Yue. “He asked whether your cough was better. I said it was.”

“Teacher, eight baskets,” he said. “Futai can’t eat that many.”

“The union,” said Qiusheng. “Keep one basket back for the Lu crew.”

Ah Yue nodded and went up the stairs with the basket. Old Qi sat on the upturned vat in the corner, his tea bowl on the floor at his left hand. He glanced at the wet mark the basket had left and said nothing.

At six that day the fire was lit and the truck had not yet gone. Old Qi lifted his tea bowl halfway and looked first at the iron door at the head of the stairs.

“That inspector. He was at the Nanji the day before yesterday. Takes his tea without sugar, same as you.”

Qiusheng drew the thermometer out of the vat and wiped it dry.

“Mm.”

“Sat out one pot. Up the stairs, down again,” said Old Qi. “Ten cents for the tea. Paid it himself.”

At the stove door Ah Yue turned his head. “Teacher, Qiao Zheng—”

“Take the truck out,” said Qiusheng.

Old Qi put the tea bowl back at his left hand, stood up and went up the stairs first.

Wednesday afternoon, the private room at the Nanji. Counsel Liang drew the pastry box in front of him and did not lift the lid yet.

“Inspector Qiao left a message as he went, for me to bring to you, Teacher,” he said. “‘His student called.’”

Qiusheng lifted his teacup.

“I said Mr He has a great many students, thirty to a class at the night school.” Counsel Liang’s fountain pen tapped the table once. “He said you would know which one.”

“We’ll see.”

Counsel Liang looked at him a moment, lifted the lid of the box and counted the money.

---

After Rain Water Ye Mingde did not come down the stairs at Futai again. His six lines still lay spread at the far end of the workbench, the corners of the paper curled. Qiusheng had not put them away.

The day of Awakening of Insects was a Thursday. The ice truck came as usual and backed in through the side gate, its tail to the iron door at the head of the stairs. This time the door on the other side of the cab opened first, and the man who got down was the bookkeeper.

Gold-rimmed spectacles, a long gown, the book held to his chest, the brass on its corners wet with fog. He did not go down into the cellar. He stood on the flagstones outside the iron door and waited for Qiusheng to come up the stairs.

“Mr He, Madam Wen invites you to see the cold store,” he said. “Four o’clock this afternoon. A car will come for you.”

“The cold store?” Ah Yue asked from the tail of the truck.

The bookkeeper did not look at him. “The far eastern end of the docks. The Wen cold store.” He changed the book to his other arm. “You are to bring the notebook, Mr He. Madam Wen said so.”

Old Qi leaned by the side gate, his eyes on the railway at the far end of the siding. The bookkeeper walked past him. Old Qi did not move. The truck drove out by the side gate. Water dripped from its tail onto the flagstones, and dripped all the way to the docks.

Ah Yue glanced at the line of water outside the side gate. “Teacher, under the cold store. They say on the docks there’s a floor under the Wen cold store. The ice trucks go in at night and don’t come out till daylight.”

Qiusheng picked the notebook up off the workbench and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

“Teacher—”

“Four o’clock.”

At five to four the Wen & Co. motor car stopped outside the side gate. Black, the same one that had come to Futai in October. The doorman opened the car door. Qiusheng got in. Old Qi did not go with him. He stood by the side gate and watched the car back out along the siding, over the level crossing and away east.

The Wen cold store stood at the far eastern end of the docks. Past Warehouse No. 9, past the Futai siding, and past the open space where the Lu crew had their iron pot. There the railway of the goods yard came to its end, and a single line ran on east and broke off only at the gate of the cold store. Behind it was the sea. A grey concrete building, three storeys high, with no windows. On the open ground at the gate stood a row of trucks painted Wen Cold Storage, their back doors wide open, water running to the ground in streams. The main gate was wire netting, in two leaves, and beside it stood a doorman in a long gown. When the car stopped he drew one leaf open and bowed.

Madam Wen was already inside the gate. A dark green qipao, a short black wool coat over it, the jade at her wrist showing half its circle beyond the cuff. The bookkeeper stood a step behind her and to one side, the book held to his chest. She did not come forward.

“Mr He,” she said. “Come and walk through with me.”

The doorman drew open the inner door. The door was thick, wood, sheathed in iron on the outside. As it opened a rush of cold came out and struck Qiusheng in the face, and the sweat on his face went cold at once.

Inside it was below freezing. The lamps were yellow, hung from the roof, and under the lamps row on row of iron hooks, and on the hooks fish, whole, frozen hard. Frost lay on the fish, white, a layer of it; a layer on the iron hooks too, and a man’s breath went white as it left his mouth. The smell of fish was a frozen smell of fish, not strong, but it was everywhere. Qiusheng went a dozen paces and coughed once, his handkerchief pressed to his mouth. The cold went into his lungs, and the right side hurt first.

Madam Wen walked ahead, her heels knocking on the cement floor, not fast. The bookkeeper followed her. The doorman came last.

At the far end of the freezing room, an iron railing, behind the railing an opening, in the opening a spiral stair, iron, painted rust red, going down turn after turn.

“Mr He, hold the rail,” said Madam Wen. She kept one hand on the rail herself and went down step by step, the jade knocking on the rail, once, and again.

The first turn, and the cold was gone. Another smell came up, a choking one, driving straight up the nose, and the eyes stung with it. Ammonia. Beside the stair was the engine room, and in it a compressor, black, cased in iron, taller than a vat, pipes running out from its body, the pipes coated in white frost, the frost dripping. It was running, a hum, and the whole stair shook with it. A workman squatting beside it wiping a valve looked up at Madam Wen and lowered his head again.

Qiusheng began to cough. This time it would not stop. His hand on the rail, he bent double. Madam Wen stopped one turn below and did not look back. She waited. The bookkeeper waited. When the coughing was done Qiusheng folded his handkerchief, pressed his palm to the right side of his chest and went on down.

The second turn, and the heat came up. First on the face, then on the hands. The smell of coal, the smell of fire. The boiler. An iron door stood open, and inside it was a boiler, its mouth red, a stoker stripped to the waist shovelling coal, and at each shovelful the firelight flashed out. Pipes ran from the top of the boiler, down, through the floor. There was no frost here. The iron of the stair was dry, and warm to the touch.

Further down, an iron door, shut. The doorman went past Madam Wen, drew the bolt and pushed the door open.

The smell of spirit.

This, at last, was the smell of spirit. The sweetness of the rice, a clean thread of cool under it, pouring out of the door and covering the coal and the ammonia both.

Madam Wen went in first. The bookkeeper followed.

Twenty stainless-steel fermenting tanks, four distilling columns two storeys high, a whole wall of thermometers and pressure gauges, extractor fans set in the ceiling, a terrazzo floor without a grain of sand on it. The lamps were white, shining down from above onto the stainless steel, which gave the light back in sheets. The fermenting tanks stood ten to a row, two rows, each with a round lid on top, a round pane of glass let into the lid, a thermometer beside it. The distilling columns stood against the innermost wall, one against the next, each built up section on section from the floor to the ceiling, a pressure gauge to every section. The pipe at the head of the column bent down and ran into a copper condenser, and under the condenser was a stainless-steel collecting tank, empty, dry. Along the wall a long workbench with a white porcelain top, and on it a spirit lamp, a row of measuring cylinders, and a hydrometer lying on velvet.

Qiusheng stood in the iron doorway. The extractor fans hummed, and he could hear his own breathing louder than the fans.

Ye Mingde stood beside the first distilling column, his overcoat off, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, both hands gripping a spanner. He was not using it. He saw Qiusheng, changed the spanner to his left hand and with his right pushed up his spectacles. There was a mist on the lenses. He pushed, and the mist was still there. He did not come over.

“Imported from Germany.” Madam Wen stood at the near end of the first row of fermenting tanks, her hand on the lid of a tank. “The columns, to Mr Ye’s drawings. No waiting for one vat after another.”

Ye Mingde’s spanner went back to his right hand.

Qiusheng walked in from the iron doorway. A terrazzo floor, and his soles made no sound. He went to the first fermenting tank and laid his palm against it. The stainless steel was cool, cooler than the earthenware vats at Futai, drier than the bulkhead. He took his hand away. There was nothing on his palm. He went to a column and looked up. Pressure gauges one above another up its length, every needle resting on nought. He went to the workbench and looked at the hydrometer. A glass tube, velvet. No chamois. He did not touch it.

All his life he had worked in one small assay office, with one hydrometer, one measuring cylinder, one thermometer, assaying spirit drop by drop.

“This place can make ten thousand bottles a month,” said Madam Wen. “Mr He, I should like you to take charge of it.”

Qiusheng looked at the wall of needles. The bookkeeper turned a page of his book. Ye Mingde’s spanner touched the side of the column, very lightly.

“Why?” Qiusheng asked at last.

“Because the Temperance Act is about to be repealed,” said Madam Wen.

Qiusheng turned his head and looked at her.

“Parliament votes in May. The votes have been counted.” Madam Wen’s voice was very even. “Bootleg will be just liquor. Whoever has the best spirit and sells the most of it is the liquor king of Fog Harbour.”

She walked up to one of the distilling columns and tapped its stainless-steel casing lightly with a finger. One note, hollow.

“This cold store will be the biggest lawful distillery in Fog Harbour. Mr He, I want your craft, and I want your name.”

The doorman came in from outside with a vacuum flask in a bamboo case and a tray with two cups, and set them at one end of the workbench, a little way from the measuring cylinders. Madam Wen walked over, poured a cup and pushed it across to Qiusheng’s side. She did not wait for him to speak.

“Mr Ye will keep you company tonight while you try one tank,” she said. “Tomorrow morning I want a bottle.”

She put the cap back on the flask. The jade at her wrist turned half a turn. The bookkeeper closed his book and followed her towards the iron door. The doorman went ahead to draw the door open. In the doorway she stopped and did not look back.

“Mr He, it isn’t cold down here.”

The iron door shut. On the spiral stair the heels went up, the jade knocking on the rail, once, and again, to the top, and then nothing.

Two men were left in the basement. The extractor fans hummed. Ye Mingde laid the spanner down by the column and took up his notebook.

The cup of tea on the workbench Qiusheng did not pick up.

---

The principle of a continuous distilling column Qiusheng understood. The valves and the pressure gauges had to be learned afresh, one by one.

“The steam comes down from the boiler. This valve, open it a third.” Ye Mingde’s finger was on a brass valve. “The mash goes in here, into the middle of the column. Inside the column there are plates, one above another, thirty-six of them. The steam goes up, the spirit goes up, the water falls. Seventy-eight degrees at the head, a hundred and two at the foot, and between them a few tenths of a degree from one plate to the next. Mr He, it doesn’t have to wait. As much as goes in comes out. It doesn’t stop.”

“Doesn’t stop.”

“Continuous,” said Ye Mingde. “The copper one up there is the condenser. The heart comes out there and runs into the tank.”

Qiusheng wrote. He wrote the positions of the valves, wrote thirty-six, wrote seventy-eight and a hundred and two. When he had written he looked up at the head of the column. Before the pipe at the head bent into the condenser a thin one branched off it, went away to the side and joined a small valve, and from under the valve a thin tube ran down to the drain in the floor. He looked at the thin tube.

“That?”

“The side draw,” said Ye Mingde. “For regulating the pressure. That’s how the book shows it.”

Qiusheng did not take it up, and drew the thin tube into his notebook as well.

At ten at night they began the feed. The mash was a tank Ye Mingde had filled after Rain Water, the one in the first row; under the round glass in its lid the froth had gone flat. The valve opened, there was a sound in the pipes, and the column warmed from the bottom section by section. Under the palm each section was cooler than the one below it. The needles of the pressure gauges moved. One, two, and then the whole wall of needles was moving.

Qiusheng stood at the foot of the column with his head tilted back.

Twenty years at the Assay Office, half a year at Futai: the mercury in the thermometer climbed to sixty-four and a half degrees, stopped, and climbed again. That stop was what he watched for. Here there was no stop. The needle on the gauge at the head of the column went to seventy-eight and did not stop. It trembled a hair above seventy-eight, went on trembling, and did not come down. The needle on the gauge at the foot went to a hundred and two and trembled too. Thirty-odd gauges between, and every one of them trembling. The needles on the wall kept moving, and not one of them would stop.

He watched a long time before he understood what it was this column lacked.

At the condenser end the first drop had not yet come. Ye Mingde was beside the innermost column with his back to him, standing on an iron stool copying down the pressure gauges, his pencil moving over his notebook.

Qiusheng went to the iron ladder on the side of the column and climbed it, holding the handrail. The ladder was narrow, the width of one man. On the platform at the second level the pipe at the head of the column was before his eyes, and the thin tube that branched from it, and the small valve. The valve was brass, with a wheel. He closed his hand on it. Cool. He turned it half a turn.

There was a sound in the thin tube. A thread fell from under the valve, down the tube, into the drain, and was gone. He brought his nose over the mouth of the tube and wafted once with his palm. Sweet. A sharp sweetness, the kind at sixty-four and a half degrees. His hand stayed closed on the valve wheel. In the basement there were only the extractor fans and the sound of that thread falling in the thin tube. After a while he wafted again. The sweetness was fainter. After another while the sweetness was gone and only the rice was left. He turned the valve wheel back. The thread broke. The tube was empty.

He came down, his hand on the handrail. Ye Mingde was still copying at the far end. The iron stool had not moved and the pencil had not stopped.

It was two in the morning when a sound came in the collecting tank. A drop, then a thread, three times as thick as the one at the end of the copper pipe at Futai. Ye Mingde got down from the iron stool, went to the collecting tank and squatted to look, and his spectacles misted over again. He stood up and wrote the time in his notebook.

“Mr He, it’s coming through.”

“No hurry.”

The spirit ran all through the small hours. The stokers changed shift, and the sound of the coal shovel came down through the floor above, stroke after stroke. The spirit in the collecting tank rose to the second mark. Qiusheng sat on the stool by the workbench and had two spells of coughing, his handkerchief pressed to his mouth, the palm of his other hand pressed to the right side of his chest. Each time, Ye Mingde turned his head away to look at the pressure gauges.

Towards dawn Qiusheng took a small glass, caught it half full and held it up under the lamp.

Clear as nothing at all. The beads on the inside of the glass were as fine as frost.

He smelled it. He wafted once with his palm. The sweetness of the rice, the thread of cool under it, and under the cool there was nothing. He took a small sip and shut his eyes.

“Ninety-nine,” he said.

Ye Mingde jumped up, threw his arms round him, let go again in a hurry, and went red right to his ears.

“First Dew! Mr He, First Dew!”

Qiusheng did not smile. He put what was left of the half glass down on the workbench and looked at it. The frost on the inside of the glass turned to water bead by bead.

Ye Mingde opened his notebook and his pencil ran across it, one line, then another. “6 March, first tank, ninety-nine. Mr He, ninety-nine! In ten years the department has never made a ninety-five!”

Qiusheng’s notebook lay open beside him, full of valves and figures. That line he did not write.

“Mingde,” he said, “where will this tank be sold?”

“The Consulate, the hotels in Upper Town, the provincial capital—”

“And the docks?”

Ye Mingde’s pencil stopped on the notebook.

“Madam Wen will sell this tank at ten dollars a bottle,” said Qiusheng. “A dockworker earns eighty cents a day. Old Hai can’t afford it. Headman Ah Lu can’t afford it. Ah Gui, who sells greens at the market, her husband can’t afford it.”

Ye Mingde’s pencil lifted from the paper and went back to it, and did not write.

Qiusheng closed the notebook.

“What Madam Wen wants is to be the liquor king of Fog Harbour,” he said. “What I want is for the people on the docks not to go blind.”

The extractor fans hummed. The needle at the head of the column was still trembling.

Day came. Ye Mingde took two bottles from the cupboard, narrow-necked, glass, with no labels. He filled one, put in the stopper and set it on the workbench. “The one Madam Wen wants.” The second he filled halfway, stopped, and looked at Qiusheng.

“This one I’ll take with me,” said Qiusheng.

“Mr He, the bookkeeper says every bottle that goes out has to be entered—”

“Enter it,” said Qiusheng. “For assay.”

Ye Mingde filled the bottle to the top and stoppered it. He wrote a line in his notebook, and wrote it slowly. Qiusheng put the bottle in his coat pocket. The pocket hung heavy. The tea at the far end of the workbench had stood cold all night. He had not touched it.

He did not take Madam Wen’s car. From the cold store back along the railway to the mouth of the Futai siding, a quarter of an hour, the sky only just turned pale, the fog lying close along the rails. Ah Yue was waiting at the iron door, unshaven.

“Teacher, what’s it like down there?”

“Clean.”

“Cleaner than here?”

“Mm.”

Ah Yue looked back at the six vats, one after another, covered with boards, a brick weighting each board. He asked nothing more.

That night the fire at Futai was lit as usual. Qiusheng wrote in the notebook: *6 March, sixty-sixth vat, rice wine, ninety-three.* When he had written it he closed the notebook and put it under the thermometer case.

---

He did not say yes to Madam Wen, and he did not say no.

Madam Wen did not press him. The two hundred bottles for the docks he kept back as before.

On Sunday afternoon Smith came.

He came down the stairs, his leather shoes pausing on each one. His wool overcoat hung open, the watch chain lay across his waistcoat, and he carried the wicker picnic basket in his hand. At the bottom he stood on the last stair and looked first at the row of vats, then at Old Lu squatting at the very back, then at Qiusheng by the workbench.

“Mr Ho.”

Qiusheng stood up.

Old Lu did not stand. He squatted by the four bricks, his palm flat on a brick, his face towards the fire. Ah Yue sat on an empty crate with his right foot propped on another crate, the shoe off, the ankle bound with cloth. Ah Shui squatted at the stone trough washing bottles, the brush in his left hand. He heard the leather shoes and did not look round.

Smith set the basket on the workbench and lifted the lid. This time there was only a bottle in the basket, and two small glasses. The bottle was square, with the stag label on it, half full, the spirit in it caramel-coloured.

He stood the bottle on the workbench.

“This, I take from the liquor cabinet at the Consulate,” he said, his Chinese slow, one word at a time. “Madam Wen’s ship, it brings. One crate, twelve bottles. Last year, September, one crate, the guests drink, the head hurts three day—three days.” He pulled the stopper. “These months, it does not hurt. The Consul says, Madam Wen’s liquor is good now.”

He poured two glasses and pushed one across. Qiusheng did not pick it up. He lowered his head, brought his nose to the rim and wafted once with his palm. Caramel, water steeped in wood chips, and under them it was clean, a thread of cool. His own base spirit, with the heads cut.

“Mr Ho, you smell it,” said Smith. “This bottle and the bottle of last year, it is not the same man who made them.”

Qiusheng pushed the glass back.

Smith did not drink. He turned the square bottle half round, so that the stag faced Qiusheng, the English under it.

“Mr Ho, why do you not write your own name on the label?”

In the cellar there was only the sound of water at the stone trough. Ah Shui’s brush turned in a bottle. At the very back Old Lu was on his feet now, and gave a brick a pat. Qiusheng picked up the stopper, put it back in the mouth of the bottle and pressed it home.

Smith waited a while. He took the small book from his overcoat pocket and opened it.

“The Consul, in April, gives a reception,” he said. “A big one. People come from the provincial capital, people of the shipping companies. The Consul says he wants the best Fog Harbour has. Price is no object.”

“There’s no spirit in April,” said Qiusheng.

Smith closed the book. “April, none?”

“None.”

“March, there is.”

“March I’m keeping.”

Smith glanced at the row of vats, then at Qiusheng. He pulled the stopper again, poured the two glasses back into the square bottle one at a time, stoppered it and put it back in the basket. He wrapped the two glasses in the napkin.

“Mr Ho, in April, I come again to ask.”

The basket was lifted. The leather shoes went up the stairs, pausing on each one.

Old Lu came over and glanced at the two rings the glasses had left on the workbench.

“The foreigner asks about a name,” he said. “Those three crocks of ours. No name.”

He went up the stairs and away. Ah Yue took his foot down off the crate, tried his weight on it, frowned and propped it up again.

“Teacher, at the Consulate it’s twelve dollars a bottle,” he said. “He says it doesn’t hurt.”

“Mm.”

“The dollar-fifty one on the docks doesn’t hurt either.”

Qiusheng wiped the two rings away with a cloth.

---

The next Thursday, when the ice truck came, the bookkeeper got down from it again.

Still he did not go down into the cellar. He stood on the flagstones outside the iron door, drew a bill out of the book and passed it in at the iron door. Ah Yue was lifting crates at the tail of the truck, and Ah Shui steadied a corner of each crate with his left hand. Old Qi leaned by the side gate.

The bill was in the bookkeeper’s hand, thin, no stroke joined to the next. The upper half: Cold store, 6 March, first tank, assay reading ninety-nine, two hundred and forty-two bottles. The Consulate, sixty, at ten dollars a bottle; the hotels in Upper Town, a hundred and twenty; the provincial capital, sixty. The Wen residence, one bottle. Taken by Mr He, one bottle. The lower half: Futai, this week, Wen & Co., one thousand; the Dockworkers’ Union, two hundred, charged at the cold-store price.

“Charged at the cold-store price?” Qiusheng asked.

The bookkeeper pushed up his spectacles. “Madam Wen says every bottle distilled in the cold store bears the name Wen.”

“These two hundred bottles are Futai’s.”

“You have gone down into the cold store, Mr He. Futai is a branch of the cold store,” said the bookkeeper, one word at a time, with no rise or fall. “A cold-store bottle is ten dollars. For the Dockworkers’ Union’s two hundred bottles Madam Wen does not ask ten dollars. She asks three. Six hundred a week.”

Ah Yue came over from the tail of the truck, wiping his hand down his trousers. “Teacher never said yes to her!”

The bookkeeper did not look at him.

Qiusheng turned the bill over. The back was blank. He drew his own fountain pen from his shirt pocket, the one with the barrel worn pale, and wrote on the back. He wrote small, stroke by stroke.

First Dew, one bottle, ten dollars. The docks, two hundred bottles, three dollars a bottle, six hundred. 12 March, He Qiusheng.

When he had written it he went down the stairs, took the pastry box from under the workbench, lifted the lid, counted out six hundred and ten dollars, wrapped it in a sheet of newspaper, came up again and passed it out at the iron door together with the bill.

“Paid,” he said. “The two hundred bottles go to the docks as before.”

The bookkeeper took it and did not count it. He put the newspaper parcel under the book, slipped the bill inside the book and got into the truck. The truck drove out by the side gate. Water dripped onto the flagstones, and dripped all the way to the docks.

Old Qi watched the truck over the level crossing, and watched it go east.

“Wednesday’s six hundred. Gone,” he said.

Qiusheng went down the stairs, put the lid on the pastry box and pushed it back under the workbench.

Old Qi said no more. He went back to the upturned vat in the corner.

The Thursday after that, when the ice truck had gone, Old Qi got down from Ah Yue’s truck and came down into the cellar. He looked first at the iron door at the head of the stairs, and only then spoke.

“There are two more doormen at the gate of the cold store.”

Qiusheng was at the stone trough washing the measuring glass.

“Long gowns,” said Old Qi. “They don’t watch the gate. They watch people.”

He walked to the upturned vat in the corner and did not sit. The tea bowl was on the floor to the left of it. He looked at the bowl.

“Ten years I’ve collected her debts. Not once has she asked me whether the liquor was any good.”

Qiusheng poured the water from the measuring glass into the stone trough.

“She’s counting your days,” said Old Qi.

He sat down. Ah Yue stood at the foot of the stairs. His hand stopped on his trousers and did not wipe.

---

Old Hai came the day after, from the track end.

Past six, the charcoal in the stove only just red. Ah Yue sat on a crate before the stove door, the door open a crack. Qiusheng sat at the workbench, the notebook open. Old Hai ducked in under the chain, bald, in his padded waistcoat, the cigarette tin gripped in his hand. He walked to the workbench and did not sit. His one eye went over the cellar from end to end, the vats, the pots, the shelves, the chalk on the wall, and when it came to the four bricks at the very back it stopped a moment.

“Teacher,” he said. “On the docks they say you are Madam Wen’s man now.”

Qiusheng looked at him.

“That floor under the cold store, the docks know about it,” said Old Hai, a sentence at a time. “The ice trucks go in and come out at daylight. On the day of Awakening of Insects, you rode into the cold store in her car, Teacher. There are more than a thousand mouths on the docks. One man sees, and a thousand mouths know.”

Ah Yue stood up from the stove door. “Old Hai, Teacher—”

“Ah Yue, I’m talking to Teacher.”

Ah Yue sat down again.

Qiusheng stood up and went to the shelves. On the top shelf, at the back, a narrow-necked glass bottle with no label, the one he had brought back from the cold store. He took the bottle down, set it on the workbench, pulled the stopper, poured half a glass and pushed it across.

Old Hai looked at the half glass. He picked the glass up, held it under the lamp, turned his head to one side and looked with his one eye at the light in the glass, looked a while, lowered it, raised it again. He drank.

His brow creased.

He did not say it was good. He put the glass back on the workbench and looked at the bottle.

“Teacher, this. How much a bottle?”

“Ten dollars.”

Old Hai put the cigarette tin in his trouser pocket, picked up the bottle and pressed the stopper home.

“I’ll take it back with me.”

“What for?”

“To show them,” said Old Hai. “The ten-dollar one. This is what it’s like.”

He tucked the bottle under his arm, walked to the iron door and looked back.

“Thursday, two hundred bottles. As before?”

“As before.”

Old Hai ducked out under the chain. Ah Yue looked at the iron door.

“Teacher, he’s taken it.”

“Mm.”

“Ninety-nine.”

Qiusheng rinsed the empty glass and stood it upside down on the board.

---

Wednesday, three in the afternoon, the private room at the Nanji.

Counsel Liang sat in the host’s seat, his back to the window, his fountain pen lying across the takings sheet. His handkerchief was in the pocket of his suit, one pink corner showing. Qiusheng sat facing him, his back to the board partition. On the table a pot of tea, two cups. The pastry box stood at the corner of the table, lighter than usual, and Counsel Liang did not reach to draw it over. Downstairs at the table by the stairs Old Qi’s tea bowl was at his left hand. Qiusheng had seen it as he came up.

Counsel Liang spoke first. His hand went towards his pocket, got as far as the corner of the handkerchief, stopped, came away and went back to the table.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset,” he said. “If I tell you, I may lose my licence.”

“What is it?”

“Madam Wen’s friends in the provincial capital are not only members of Parliament.” Counsel Liang lowered his voice. The fountain pen did not tap the table. “There is also the Director-General of the Prohibition Bureau. Before the Temperance Act is repealed the Bureau will make one last great sweep and take all Fog Harbour’s bootleg stills in a single net. Only one will not be swept.”

“The cold store.”

“The cold store.” Counsel Liang nodded. “And then Madam Wen will be the only person in Fog Harbour with a still, with outlets, and with the recipe. Teacher, she does not need you to say yes. She needs only your recipe, and your absence.”

Qiusheng lifted his tea and did not drink.

“What day?”

“The day I do not know,” said Counsel Liang. “The people who know the day do not take their tea at the Nanji.”

“How do you know?”

“A lawyer in the provincial capital, who took his licence the same year I did. A paper of the Director-General’s passed through his hands,” said Counsel Liang. “He telephoned and said one thing only: Henian, let your clients in Fog Harbour take a rest before the end of March. Teacher, I have fought cases for twenty years. What is written on a paper I read. What is not written on it I listen for.”

Qiusheng put the cup down.

“Ye Mingde.”

“He is a good boy,” said Counsel Liang. “But he is Madam Wen’s man.”

He drew the pastry box in front of him, lifted the lid, glanced in and put the lid back. This time he did not count.

“Teacher, this goes through as before,” he said. “Do not put the matter of the cold store down to my account.”

Qiusheng stood up. Counsel Liang’s hand went towards his pocket again. The handkerchief stayed in the pocket.

“Teacher,” he said. “The end of March.”

“Mm.”

Qiusheng went downstairs. At the table by the stairs Old Qi had his tea bowl lifted halfway, his eyes on the door. Qiusheng went past the table and out by the back door. Three stone steps, and the patch where the moss was worn away was still there.

---

That night Qiusheng went to the cold store.

Past ten, the fog coming in off the sea, the end of the railway white. The row of trucks at the gate of the cold store stood dark, and by the wire-netting gate stood two men in long gowns, one on the left and one on the right. Seeing him, the one on the left drew the gate open a crack, bowed, and asked nothing. The one on the right watched him walk to the iron-sheathed door inside, and watched the doorman draw the door open for him.

Only half the lamps in the freezing room were lit. The fish hung, white with frost. He walked through, coughed once, and held back the second cough. The spiral stair. The first turn, ammonia, the compressor silent, the frost on the pipes dripping, a drop, then a drop. The second turn, the smell of coal, no stoker, the fire banked, a line of red showing at the crack of the fire door. Further down, the iron door stood open a crack.

The smell of spirit.

Ye Mingde was alone in the basement, standing on the iron ladder of the second column, facing a valve, a spanner in his hand. The columns were not running, and the needles of the pressure gauges had all fallen to nought. He heard the iron door, turned his head, and nearly dropped the spanner.

“Mr He, so late—”

“Mingde,” said Qiusheng, “your notebook. Let me see it.”

Ye Mingde came down the iron ladder and laid the spanner by the column. He pushed up his spectacles, took the notebook from his overcoat pocket and held it out. Halfway across, his hand stopped a moment, and only then let go.

Qiusheng sat on the stool by the workbench and turned the pages one by one. The lamp shone down from above, white. Fermentation curves, six of them, the six vats at Futai; yields, a column each for red yeast and white; temperatures for cutting the heads, sixty-four and a half degrees, seventy-eight, and beside them a line of small writing: Mr He says it must be slow. The proportions of yeast, so many catties of rice to a cake of yeast. Further on was the cold store: thirty-six plates, nought point two, seventy-eight, a hundred and two, 6 March, ninety-nine.

On the last page Ye Mingde had written in a neat hand: White Dew process, complete. Under it a line was ruled, and under the line was written a date: 10 April.

“What day is this?”

Ye Mingde’s face went white.

“Madam Wen said…” He swallowed. “Madam Wen said that after the tenth of April the four columns in the cold store are to run day and night. She said you would come. She said… she said if you don’t come, I am to make it myself.”

Qiusheng looked at him. A mist had come over the young man’s spectacles. The basement was not cold. It was hot.

“The tenth of April,” said Qiusheng. “That is the day of the Bureau’s great sweep, isn’t it.”

Ye Mingde did not answer. He picked the spanner up from beside the column and put it down again.

Qiusheng gave him back the notebook.

“Your notes are very good,” he said. “Better than mine. But you have left one thing out.”

“What?”

Qiusheng stood up, walked to the distilling column, looked up and pointed at the head of it. The condenser, the pipe bending down, the thin one beside it.

“The heads,” he said. “Your columns distil continuously. The heads and the heart come out together. Nowhere in your notes does it say how the heads are to be cut.”

Ye Mingde opened the notebook, turned to the page on distilling, read it through, and read it through again. A column was drawn on the page, a thin line branching off at its head, and beside the line two words were written: pressure regulation.

“There’s a side draw at the top of the column…” he murmured. “I thought it was for regulating the pressure…”

“It is for drawing off the heads,” said Qiusheng. “If you don’t know that, the first batch of spirit you make will be the same as the Black Flag’s firewater.”

Ye Mingde raised his head. The notebook closed in his hands.

Qiusheng turned and walked towards the iron door.

Ye Mingde did not come after him. He turned the notebook back to the page on distilling, and beside the side draw his pencil made an arrow, and under the arrow wrote seventy-eight. The pencil stopped on the figure, and he did not put it down.

Qiusheng pushed open the iron door and went up the spiral stair. The heat of the boiler fell away behind him, the ammonia came up, the compressor started just then and the whole stair gave a shake. The freezing room, frost, the smell of fish, the doorman drawing the door open for him. The two doormen by the wire-netting gate still stood where they had been, and the one on the left drew the gate open a crack.

At the end of the railway a man stood in the fog.

Old Qi. The shoulders of his railway uniform were covered in beads of water, the peak of his cap dripped, and in both hands he held a little book, cloth-covered, the size of a palm. He was not looking at the gate of the cold store. His eyes were on the far end of the railway. Hearing the footsteps, he turned round.

“Teacher, there’s something I have to tell you.”
