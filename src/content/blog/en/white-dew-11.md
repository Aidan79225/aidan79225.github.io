*January · Lesser Cold*

On the evening of Lesser Cold, Qiusheng went back to South Slope.

The flagstones of the slope were dry. The January wind came in off the sea, and by the top of the slope only a little chill was left of it. He opened the second-floor door with his own key.

Nothing in the room had been moved. On the table a uniform, half altered, the cuff unpicked, the needle stuck in the lining. Beside the uniform a key, the one Su Qi had left, still where it was; beyond it the dictionary in its hard covers, a size bigger than his hand. In the corner the cloth from the tailor’s stall lay in a heap, a thin layer of dust on it. He did not put the lamp on.

In the wardrobe his few shirts hung on the left. The right side was half empty, the hangers one against another, and they rattled at a touch. He took down two shirts and a singlet, folded them and put them on the arm of the rattan chair.

The flat was so quiet he could hear the bamboo poles downstairs as the washing was taken in.

He sat down in the rattan chair. The bamboo poles stopped, and the door downstairs shut. Down the slope the lamps came on one by one. They did not reach the second floor. He sat, one hand on the pile of clothes on the arm of the chair.

When he woke the room was quite dark. Mrs Chen’s wireless had stopped long before, and there was no sound from down the slope. He did not know what time it was.

He stood up, went to the wardrobe and stepped up on the square stool. At the back of the top shelf, three tin boxes, all of a size, one against another. In October he had cleared this shelf out once; the Nanji could swallow only six hundred a week, and in November and again in December he had bought another box at the ironmonger’s in the Old Town.

He lifted the three boxes down one by one and set them in a row on the table, beside the uniform. The boxes were cold, the lids pressed down tight. He did not open them. He sat down on the square stool and looked at the three boxes, and at the uniform beside the boxes. The needle was stuck in the cuff, its eye towards him.

Outside the window it turned grey. Down the slope someone was taking down shutter boards. He lifted the three boxes back one by one, put them at the back of the top shelf, pushed the hangers across to hide them and shut the wardrobe door. The pile of clothes under his arm, his shoes put on at the door, the door locked, the key back in his pocket. The one on the table he did not take.

At the foot of the stairs Mrs Chen’s door opened a crack. She had a broom in her hand.

“Those night shifts of yours are hard work, Mr He. My nephew is a caretaker at the Prohibition Bureau.”

Qiusheng nodded once and went down the slope.

---

Ye Mingde was sent by Madam Wen.

8 January, a Thursday. The ice truck came as usual. This time the driver did not back straight in through the side gate. He stopped first on the siding, the door on the other side of the cab opened, and a man got down.

Qiusheng stood inside the iron door at the head of the stairs. Ah Yue was at the tail of the truck waiting to unload the crates, his hand on the side of the bed. Old Qi leaned by the side gate with his eyes on the railway at the far end of the siding. The driver handed a folded paper out of the cab window. Ah Yue took it and brought it round the front of the truck to the iron door. The bookkeeper’s hand, thin, no stroke joined to the next: Ye Mingde, teaching assistant in the university’s chemistry department. Madam Wen’s orders: he is to learn the craft from Mr He.

The man came towards the iron door. Thirty or so, tall, a grey wool overcoat buttoned to the top, round spectacles, the lenses misted over in the cold wind. A leather case in his left hand, an oiled-paper parcel in his right. Outside the iron door he stopped, changed the case to his right hand and the parcel to his left, and changed them back.

“Mr He,” he said. Half the mist had cleared from his lenses. “I’ve read your report. In the Assay Office’s annual report, the one you wrote, ‘The Behaviour of Methanol in Fractional Distillation’. Our department uses it for teaching.”

Qiusheng glanced at him. He had written that report twelve years ago. He had supposed no one but the director had ever read it.

“Did you understand it?”

“I understood half of it,” said Ye Mingde. “The curve you drew, between sixty-four and a half degrees and seventy-eight, we can’t get in the laboratory. The temperature always drifts.”

“Because you use electric burners,” said Qiusheng. “Electric burners are too quick.”

Ye Mingde set the case on the ground and tucked the oiled-paper parcel under his arm. He pushed his spectacles up, took a notebook from his overcoat pocket and began to write, there at the iron door. He wrote a line and looked up.

“Mr He, what do you use, then?”

“Charcoal.”

He wrote again. At the tail of the truck Ah Yue lifted a crate up and glanced back towards the iron door. Old Qi did not look. He was listening to the siding.

At the bottom of the fourteen stone steps Ye Mingde looked first for the thermometer that stood in a vat, went close and read the scale, and only then turned to the lines of chalk on the wall by the stairs. On the nail beside the pot hung a new tear-off calendar, one Ah Yue had begged at the end of the year from the rice merchant on the docks. It was printed with the solar terms and the tides like the old one, and was turned to the page for the eighth. Ye Mingde put the case down at his feet and undid the oiled paper. Inside was a book, its blue cloth cover faded to grey, the spine resewn with thread. He turned to a page. The edge of the paper was worn to a fuzz, the page was underlined in red pencil, and the margins were full of small writing. The curve was printed in the bottom corner of the page.

Qiusheng looked at the page. His own drawing, smaller in print than he had drawn it, two figures on the horizontal axis, one sixty-four and a half degrees, one seventy-eight. He had not seen it in twelve years.

“This page,” said Ye Mingde. “Three people in our department have copied it out.”

“Mm.”

Ye Mingde shut the book and went and stood at the foot of the wall. The chalk ran from 12 October to today, a line a day: the date, the temperature, a figure. His finger stopped in the air and did not touch it.

“Mr He, these. May I copy them?”

“Copy them.”

“The temperature of every vat, every day?”

“Every day.”

“And the smell?” He turned the pages of his notebook. “The height of the foam?”

Qiusheng opened the notebook on the workbench, turned to the pages for October and pushed it across. Ye Mingde bent over it, his pencil following the words on the page. After three pages he looked up.

“Mr He, the books say methanol and ethanol come over together. The boiling points are thirteen and a half degrees apart, but they won’t part company. You can’t cut clean.”

“So you go slowly.”

“Slowly, and they still come over together.”

“So you cut more.”

“Cut more and some still gets through.”

“So you assay every vat.”

Ye Mingde wrote the three sentences down, looked at them when he had done, and wrote a figure beside them.

Ah Yue came down the stairs carrying a bag of ice the driver had left. He put the ice in the stone trough and dusted his hands.

“Mr Ye, what’s Madam Wen paying you?”

“Three times what the department pays.”

“What does the department pay?”

“Thirty.”

Ah Yue worked it out and asked no more.

“The professors in the department are all writing reports for members of Parliament this month,” said Ye Mingde. “There’s only me left in fermentation.”

He said no more, bent his head and went on copying.

That day Ye Mingde copied until five, and did not take off his overcoat. Only when Old Qi, at the stairs, glanced up at the iron door did Ye Mingde shut his notebook, wrap the annual report back in its oiled paper and go up the stairs with his case. On the seventh step he looked back.

“Mr He, what time tomorrow?”

“There’s no fire in the cellar by day.”

“I can copy.”

Qiusheng nodded once.

Ye Mingde went up. Old Qi went back to his corner and sat down. Ah Yue opened the stove door and looked at the charcoal.

“Teacher, we’re down to four,” he said. “Ah Shui hasn’t come since New Year’s Day. He says with one hand he’d be taking the brothers’ money for nothing.”

Qiusheng looked at the lines on the wall.

“And his mother?”

“I don’t know,” said Ah Yue. “He didn’t say.”

---

Ye Mingde came every day.

He copied everything on the wall into his notebook, then copied what was in the notebook onto sheet after sheet of paper, and on the paper he drew lines, the days across, the temperature up, a line to a vat. Six lines lay in a row on the workbench, and he went through them for Qiusheng with his pencil, one by one. The lines for the two vats by the stairs were the flattest, and the innermost vat’s rose and fell the most; the vats with red yeast gave more spirit, the white ten per cent less. Qiusheng looked, and nodded. These things he knew. He had never drawn them.

Of the notebook, Ye Mingde saw only the pages Qiusheng turned to for him. October, November, the middle of December. Before the last page came, Qiusheng shut the notebook. Ye Mingde did not ask.

On 15 January he came down the stairs carrying something in his arms.

It was a glass tube, two feet long, joined from three lengths, the joints bound with rubber rings. He held it to his chest with both hands under it, like a man carrying a baby, down one step, both feet together, then down the next. At the bottom he laid it on the workbench, with a cloth spread under it.

“Mr He, it’s glass. Thrown out by the department laboratory. I joined three lengths,” he said. “Copper reacts with things in the spirit. Glass doesn’t.”

Qiusheng looked at the copper pipe on the pot. Picked out of the scrap-iron heap on the docks, bent in August round a wooden post, an inch at a time, into three bends, run out through the porthole of the Fair Wind to lie two months in the sea. Hauled up, it had left a streak of green on the mudflats.

“This one came off the Fair Wind,” he said.

“I know. Mr Chen told me,” said Ye Mingde. “Mr He, try one vat. Just one.”

“We’ll see.”

Ye Mingde left the glass tube on the workbench and went.

That night Ah Yue took the truck out at ten, and Old Qi rode with it. Qiusheng was left alone in the cellar. He took the copper pipe off the pot, unwinding the wet strips of cloth from the joint turn by turn. He fitted the glass tube, bound the joint with cloth and poured water on the cloth. He lit the fire and squatted by the pot with his eyes on the thermometer.

The mercury climbed. Sixty-four and a half degrees. It stopped. His palm was pressed to the right side of his chest. The mouth of the tube dripped. The tube was glass, and he could see where the drop came from and where it went. The little cup stood under the mouth of the tube. The mercury passed seventy-eight.

“Cut.”

He said it himself. There was no one else in the cellar. He put the jar in the cup’s place.

The heart ran all night. Before dawn he caught half a glass and held it up to the lamp. Clear. He took a small sip and shut his eyes. The sweetness of the rice, a thread of cool under it, a shade cleaner than usual. He opened the notebook and wrote: *16 January, fifty-first vat, rice wine, ninety-five.*

Ye Mingde came down the stairs next morning and looked first at the wall, then at the notebook. He read that line twice.

“Ninety-five!” Ye Mingde pushed his spectacles up, and pushed them up again. “Mr He, any higher and it’s First Dew!”

Qiusheng was at the stone trough washing the measuring glass.

“First Dew is ninety-nine.”

“Four! Only four short!” Ye Mingde’s pencil scored across his notebook. “Mr He, keep the glass tube, I’ll go and join another, both pots—”

“One,” said Qiusheng. “The other pot stays as it is.”

Ye Mingde stopped a moment, wrote that down and drew a line under it.

The copper pipe leaned against the wall for three days. On the third day Qiusheng fetched it and with a hacksaw cut off the length with the bends in it. He wiped the cut with a cloth and wiped the three bends dry one by one. The straight length he leaned back against the wall. The tin box on the shelf, the one with Assay printed on it, he took down and opened. A stack of yellowed reports, and under them a few old forms never used. He laid the length he had sawn off on top of the reports. Bent as it was, it just went in. He pressed the lid on. He put the box back on the shelf.

Ye Mingde was copying figures at the far end of the workbench and did not look up.

That week Qiusheng coughed badly. Once, squatting by a vat, he coughed until he was bent double, his hand on the rim of the vat, cough after cough. Ye Mingde stood by, picked up the cup of water and put it down again. Qiusheng straightened, pressed his palm to the right side of his chest and held it there, as if pressing something back in. He put his handkerchief away in his pocket, walked back to the workbench and picked up his pen.

“Mr He,” said Ye Mingde, looking at the thermometer, “eighteen and a half degrees.”

“Mm.”

Ah Yue came back from the goods yard in the middle of that night. He came down to the foot of the steps, felt in the pocket of his jacket, took out a paper folded in four and held it out.

“Ah Lian wrote it. At her mother’s cousin’s. She said to show it to you, Teacher.”

The paper was an old Assay Office form, folded with the ruled side in. On the back a line of writing, in pencil: White Dew, White Dew, White Dew. Ten times. In the first the two characters were the two birds, leaning apart, one each way; in the tenth they stood straight, the rain radical on Dew neither too big nor too small. Qiusheng read it through once, folded the paper along its creases and put it in his shirt pocket.

“Will you still teach her the characters?” Ah Yue asked.

“When she comes back.”

---

On the afternoon of Wednesday 21 January he went up to South Slope first, to fetch clothes, and then on to the Nanji.

Something had been pushed under the second-floor door. He bent and picked it up. An envelope, postmarked Saltflat Village, his name on it in Xiaoman’s writing, the three characters separate, none joined.

He sat down on the square stool and opened it.

Papa, the school here has only one teacher, and he says my English is better than his. He teaches the third year to the sixth, one classroom. I sit in the back row and help him mark the third year’s arithmetic. He asked me how far I had got with my *vocabulary*. I said H. He said he was at G. There is no cat at Grandmother’s. There is a goose. It gets up on the table at meals, and it only gets down when Grandmother raps it on the head with her chopsticks. In English it is *goose*. The schoolmaster said so. Uncle Ah Lu comes up from the ferry landing every morning and asks Mama if Mr He’s cough is better. Mama tells him to ask you. Papa, you must go to Dr Lin. I have put in a strand of seaweed. I picked it up at low tide and dried it three days. I have no school on Saturdays. The ferry makes three crossings a day. Xiaoman.

Tucked in the envelope was a strand of dried seaweed. Green, gone darker in the drying, a white mark of salt on one side. A pinch would crumble it. He did not pinch it.

He folded the letter back along its creases and put it in his shirt pocket, where it lay against Ah Lian’s paper. The seaweed he put in the notebook. The notebook was in the inside pocket of his coat. He turned to that page: two words at the top, a name beside them, three lines underneath. He laid the seaweed beside the name and shut the notebook.

The clothes he did not take.

Three o’clock, the private room at the Nanji. Counsel Liang sat in the host’s seat, his fountain pen lying across the takings sheet, his handkerchief on his knee. Qiusheng put the pastry box on the table, and put an envelope down beside the box.

“Teacher, there’s something extra today,” said Counsel Liang.

“Every month. Saltflat. In the teahouse’s name.”

Counsel Liang weighed the envelope in his hand and did not open it.

“To whom?”

“Mrs He.”

The handkerchief went from his knee to his forehead and wiped once.

“Teacher, a teahouse sends money to a tailor, and the people at the Saltflat post office will wonder.” He drew the takings sheet out from under the fountain pen and turned it over. “We put it down as a dividend. Mrs He is a minor shareholder in the Nanji, paid once a month.”

“Mm.”

“How much?”

“School fees thirty-two, a term. The deposit a hundred, in September,” said Qiusheng. “Send the rest once a month.”

Counsel Liang wrote three figures on the back of the takings sheet. When he had written them the fountain pen tapped the table once.

“The ferry makes three crossings a day,” he said. “You won’t go yourself, Teacher?”

Qiusheng lifted his teacup, drank, and put it down.

“No.”

Counsel Liang looked at him a moment and put the envelope in the inside pocket of his suit.

The ferry landing was beside the fish-market gate, a quarter of an hour on foot from the side gate at Futai. Three crossings on a Saturday. He did not go.

---

In the last week of January Old Hai came in from the track end.

Past six, the fire just lit. Old Qi was in his corner, his back to the wall. Ah Yue was at the stove door and had just put on charcoal. Ye Mingde had gone at five, and his six lines were left on the workbench. Old Hai ducked in under the chain with a bottle gripped in his hand, walked to the workbench and put the bottle down.

A soda bottle. The cork was new. On the shoulder of the bottle a paper label, and on the label two words, written in ink, square and upright, squarer than Ah Yue’s hand: White Dew.

Old Hai pulled the cork and pushed the bottle across.

“Teacher, today’s spirit tastes of disinfectant.”

Qiusheng picked up the bottle, brought its mouth under his nose and wafted once with his palm. Sweet. A sharp sweetness, the kind at sixty-four and a half degrees. He put the bottle back on the workbench, a little further from him.

“It isn’t mine.”

“The label says White Dew,” said Old Hai.

“Where is it from?”

“The Old Town. By the roundabout, at the door of that padlocked Black Flag drinking den.” Old Hai said it a sentence at a time. “Eighty cents a bottle. Sold only after dark. Yesterday the union carried two men out. They can’t see.” He took the cigarette tin from his trouser pocket and put it on the workbench. “The men who bought it say it’s Teacher’s liquor.”

Ah Yue stood up from the stove door. “Our liquor has never gone into the Old Town!” He brought his palm down on the lid of the pot.

Old Hai did not look at him. His one eye was on the bottle.

“Teacher, the men on the docks don’t go by the liquor,” he said. “They go by the words.”

In the cellar there was only the sound of the fire. From his corner Old Qi glanced at the iron door.

“Tomorrow,” said Qiusheng. “At the union.”

“To do what?”

“Assay it.”

Old Hai put the cigarette tin away, left the bottle on the workbench and ducked out under the chain. Ah Yue lifted the lid of the pot and put it back.

---

The union’s warehouse was in the red-brick building behind Warehouse No. 3. The front room was the office; the back was stacked with sacks, stacked to the roof, with one lane down the middle.

At four the next afternoon Old Hai had men shift sacks to clear a space and set a long table in it. On the table a leather case stood open: a spirit lamp, a small distilling flask, a measuring cylinder, a thermometer, a glass. The distilling flask was one Ye Mingde had brought from the department; Qiusheng had borrowed it for the day. The eighty-cent bottle stood beside the measuring cylinder, its label facing out.

Old Hai sat on a crate at one end of the table, the cigarette tin on his knee, his one eye on the measuring cylinder. The workers stood in a half circle, twenty-odd of them, padded waistcoats over their singlets, their hands tucked in their armpits. On a sack at the very front sat a young man with a wet cloth bound over his eyes, water dripping from the corner of the cloth, and a woman with her hand on his shoulder. Ah Yue stood leaning by the door. The door was open a crack, and the alley showed through it. Old Qi had not come in. He was at the mouth of the alley. Qiusheng stood behind the long table, his coat off and hung over the back of a chair, his sleeves rolled to the elbow.

Old Hai spoke first.

“Teacher. Assay it.”

Qiusheng lit the spirit lamp. The wick caught, and the flame went from yellow to blue. He poured half the bottle into the distilling flask, put in the stopper, set the thermometer through the hole in the stopper and turned the mouth of the tube to the measuring cylinder. He moved the flame under the flask.

The warehouse went quiet. The mould smell of the sacks, the smell of paraffin, and under them a layer of fish. What was in the flask did not move at first. Then small bubbles rose on the wall of the flask, one by one. The mercury climbed. Fifty, fifty-five, sixty. Old Hai’s one eye followed the mercury. The half circle shifted half a step forward and stopped.

Sixty-four and a half degrees.

The mercury stopped.

The mouth of the tube dripped. One drop, two, into the bottom of the measuring cylinder.

An oily sheen rose. Thin, plain to see when the lamp caught it, trembling on the surface.

The worker at the front with the cloth over his eyes asked, “Has it come through?”

No one answered him. The woman pressed her hand on his shoulder.

The sheen floated in the measuring cylinder. Beside the measuring cylinder the bottle, its label facing out, two words.

Qiusheng moved the flame away. He picked up the measuring cylinder, held it to the lamp and put it down again.

“Industrial spirit. Thinned with water.”

The workers looked at the layer of oil. No one spoke. Old Hai picked the cigarette tin up off his knee and put it down again.

“Who made it?” he asked.

Qiusheng looked at Ah Yue. Ah Yue was looking at the crack of the door.

“Everyone I know on the docks who makes this went inside in December,” said Ah Yue. “This isn’t the Black Flag’s hand. The Black Flag don’t write.”

Qiusheng capped the flame of the spirit lamp. “Mr Qi.”

Old Qi came in from outside the door, looked first at the back door of the warehouse and only then at the table. He looked at the measuring cylinder, then at the paper label on the bottle.

“Three days,” he said.

He went out. The workers broke up and went out, one by one, by the lane between the sacks. The young man with the cloth over his eyes stood up, the woman holding him, and at the door he turned back, his face towards the long table, the corner of the cloth still dripping. Old Hai put the cigarette tin in his trouser pocket and stayed sitting on the crate until Qiusheng had packed the case, and only then stood up.

“Teacher, those two. One’s from No. 3 Wharf, one’s from the Lu crew,” he said. “The one from the Lu crew, Old Lu told me not to tell you.”

Qiusheng fastened the catches of the case.

---

Old Qi came back three days later and said only, “Found him. He’s a kid.”

That was nine at night, the fire at its height, Qiusheng keeping watch by the pot. Old Qi came down from the iron door at the head of the stairs, came down as far as the tenth step, stopped and looked back up. There was someone else at the top of the stairs, standing in the doorway, not coming down. Ah Yue looked up from the stove door.

“Ah Shui.”

He came down. His right arm hung at his side. The sling was off, and the elbow still would not straighten. His left hand was on the wall, a step at a time. At the bottom he stood on the last step with his head down, looking at his shoes.

Ah Yue walked over. He walked fast.

“Chen Yue,” said Qiusheng.

Ah Yue stopped. He wiped his hand once down his trousers.

“Where did the spirit come from?” Qiusheng asked.

“Drums the Black Flag left. Behind the warehouse. Two dollars a drum,” said Ah Shui.

“The water?”

“Tap water.”

“Who wrote the labels?”

“I did.”

“Who taught you the characters?”

Ah Shui paused. “Big brother Ah Yue. Last year, when he was writing the labels, I stood by and watched.”

Ah Yue’s hand stopped on his trousers and did not wipe. He turned round, walked back to the stove door and squatted, his back to the stairs.

“How many bottles did you sell?”

“Forty.”

“Two men have gone blind,” said Qiusheng.

Ah Shui’s right hand shrank back behind him. “I didn’t know it would blind them. The Black Flag thin theirs the same way.” After a while, his head down: “My mother’s coughing. Like you, Teacher.”

The fire sounded in the stove. Old Qi on the tenth step did not move. Ah Yue had his back to the stairs, the stove door open a crack, the firelight on his face.

Qiusheng looked at the right hand hanging there.

“Come tomorrow at six.”

Ah Shui raised his head.

“Teacher?”

“To wash bottles.”

Ah Yue turned his head from the stove door. “Teacher, he—”

“To wash bottles,” said Qiusheng. “No hurry.”

Ah Shui stood on the last step, his left hand still on the wall. He nodded once, nodded again, and turned and went up. His soles on the stone steps, one step, and another, slowly. Old Qi let him pass, watched him out of the iron door, and only then came down, walked to the upturned vat in the corner and sat. His tea bowl was on the floor at his left hand. He did not lift it.

At six the next day Ah Shui came.

By the stone trough stood a tub of empty bottles, soda bottles with the trade marks scraped off. He squatted, gripped a bottle between his knees, took the brush in his left hand, pushed it in at the mouth, turned it, drew it out, poured in water, swilled it and stood the bottle upside down on the board. Each bottle took him twice as long as it took Ah Yue. The water was cold, and by the tenth bottle his left hand was red. He did not stop.

Qiusheng was by the pot, Old Qi in his corner, Ah Yue at the stove door. The fire was lit. The mouth of the pipe dripped, and Qiusheng called.

“Ah Shui. Come here.”

Ah Shui wiped his hand on his knee and came over. Qiusheng held out a little cup to him, and he took it with his left hand.

“The heads. Pour them away.”

“Teacher?”

“The first cupful, until the thermometer passes seventy-eight degrees. Pour all of it away.”

Ah Shui held the cup under the mouth of the pipe. The thread of spirit fell into the cup. He looked at the cup, and at Qiusheng. The cup filled. He carried it to the stone trough, turned his wrist and poured it into the trough. The water ran away through the bottom of the trough without a sound.

“Whether I’m here or not, however short of money you are, that cupful gets poured away.”

Ah Shui stood by the stone trough with the cup still in his left hand.

“Teacher, I’ll remember,” he said.

The mercury passed seventy-eight. Qiusheng said one word. Ah Shui pushed the jar under the mouth of the pipe, squatted down again by the stone trough and went on washing. Qiusheng bent to look at the thread of spirit at the mouth of the jar.

Old Qi looked at him a long time. “That will get every one of us killed.”

“His mother’s coughing,” said Qiusheng.

Old Qi said no more. He lifted his tea bowl, drank a mouthful and put it back at his left hand. From that night on, every time the truck went out, Old Qi was on it.

Ah Yue squatted at the stove door and watched Ah Shui wash bottles. One, and another, upside down on the board, in a row. He watched a while, stood up, walked over, moved the tub of empty bottles half a foot nearer Ah Shui, and went back and squatted.

“The left hand’s slow,” he said.

“It’ll get faster,” said Ah Shui.

Ah Yue did not take it up. He opened the stove door a crack and looked at the charcoal inside.

---

At the beginning of February Old Hai came with the union ledger.

Blue cloth covers, the corners worn white, three times as thick as Qiusheng’s notebook. He put the ledger on the workbench, opened it without looking, and pressed his finger on the first line of the first page.

“Ah Gui of No. 3 Wharf. June,” he said. “A wife, two children.”

The finger moved down a line.

“Ah Wang. June. Blind, the left eye. An old mother.”

Down another line. He could not read, and not once did his finger press in the wrong place. A page turned, and another. June had the most names, a page and a half. July, August, September, shorter page by page. The page for October was longer again. December, two lines. January, two lines, and the ink of those last two was still fresh.

“Seventy-three widows. Twenty-six blind.” Old Hai shut the ledger and pressed his palm on the cover. “The union puts in a share, and every man on the docks puts in a cent. I want to start a mutual aid society.”

Qiusheng looked at the ledger.

“Teacher, your spirit has saved the living. Someone has to look after the families of the dead.”

“Wednesday,” said Qiusheng.

Old Hai put the ledger under his arm and ducked out under the chain.

Wednesday, 4 February, Start of Spring. The pastry box Qiusheng carried to the Nanji was heavier than usual. He had been up to South Slope the evening before. Of the three boxes on the top shelf of the wardrobe he had opened one, wrapped the money in newspaper and packed it in the pastry box. The tin box was empty, and he had put it back in its place.

Counsel Liang drew the box towards him, lifted the lid, glanced in and put the lid back.

“Teacher, this is not six hundred.”

“It doesn’t go through the books,” said Qiusheng. “A donation.”

“To whom?”

“The Dockworkers’ Union. The mutual aid society.”

Counsel Liang pulled over a sheet of receipt paper, the Nanji’s, and uncapped his fountain pen.

“Donor?”

“The Nanji.”

The pen stopped a moment, then wrote: Nanji Teahouse. When he had written it he blew on the ink and turned the receipt round for Qiusheng to see.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset: a teahouse gives money to the union, and people in Upper Town will ask where the Nanji gets its money.”

“The tea sells well.”

Counsel Liang’s gold tooth flashed. He folded the receipt and put it with the box.

“On Thursday have Mr Chen take it to the union. The money and the paper together,” he said. He did not say that was the going rate.

On Thursday night Old Hai came in from the track end with the receipt gripped in his hand. He walked to the workbench and looked at Ah Yue. Ah Yue took from behind his ear the stub of pencil he did his sums with and held it out. Old Hai turned the receipt over, held the stub in three fingers and drew in the very middle of the paper: a circle, drawn to a point at the bottom, with a dot on top. When he had drawn it he looked at it a while and gave the pencil back to Ah Yue.

“Teacher, I can’t write,” he said.

On the society’s first receipt, the donor read: the Nanji Teahouse. On the back Old Hai drew a single drop of dew.

He folded the receipt, put it inside his waistcoat and went.

---

On the morning of 6 February Ah Yue came back from the goods yard and jumped down from the running board. His right foot came down on the gravel beside the track and turned. He stood a while holding the cab door, then walked, one step heavy and one light. Ah Chang said go to the bonesetter’s. He said there was no need.

That afternoon Qiusheng was in the main room of the Nanji.

A Friday. He did not ordinarily come. Counsel Liang had sent word by the waiter that there was a man who wanted to see Teacher; Teacher need only come and listen, and need not come upstairs. Qiusheng sat at the table by the board partition, his back to the stairs, a bowl of tea before him. The table by the stairs was empty; Old Qi was riding with the truck. Five or six tables of customers in the main room, the wireless playing Cantonese music, the little stage in the corner empty, the pipa in its cloth cover leaning against the leg of the stool.

At the next table sat three men, off the docks, in padded waistcoats, the calluses on their hands plain to see from a table away. They were talking about White Dew.

“A dollar fifty on the docks, fifty cents a glass in Upper Town.”

“Who makes it?”

“Nobody’s seen him,” said one. “On the docks they just call him Teacher. Everyone says he brought down those four Black Flag drinking dens on his own. But which man is Teacher, nobody can say.”

“And the eighty-cent stuff in the Old Town?”

“Fake,” said the man. “Old Hai’s assayed it. Oil floating on the top. The one who faked it, they say on the docks, is only a kid.”

A man nobody had seen the face of, who had shut down the Black Flag single-handed. The speaker had no idea Qiusheng was at the next table.

Qiusheng lifted his tea bowl and drank a mouthful. Tea sweepings, harsh.

A young man came in at the door. Twenty-five or six, a padded gown, glasses, a pad in his hand and a sheet of paper tucked in the pad, one corner showing. He stood a moment in the doorway, looked round and walked to the counter.

“Is Mr Liang in?”

“Upstairs,” said the waiter. “Your name, sir?”

“From the paper,” said the young man. “It wasn’t me who came last time.”

The waiter gave him a look and went upstairs. The young man sat down at the empty table beside the three men, across an aisle from Qiusheng’s table. He opened the pad, drew out the sheet and smoothed it flat. Qiusheng could see half of it: union paper, line after line of names, and after each name a month.

“Your tea, sir.” The waiter was back, and set down a pot of tea. “Mr Liang says, would you sit a while, sir.” He looked down at the paper. “What list is that?”

“Old Hai at the union let me copy it,” said the young man. “He told me to count.”

“Count what?”

“Count how many fewer.” The young man pressed the paper flat. “June, forty-one. October, eleven. December, two. January, two, and those lost their sight. Not dead.” He turned the pages of his pad. “I mean to write a piece, ‘The Teacher of Fog Harbour’.”

“The one last time wanted to write the secret,” said the waiter.

“I don’t want the secret,” said the young man. “No names. Just how many fewer men are dying on the docks.”

The waiter made no answer and went back to the counter. The three men at the next table had stopped talking, and one turned and glanced at the list. The young man folded the list and tucked it back in the pad.

The stairs sounded. Counsel Liang came down, his handkerchief in his hand. He glanced at the young man, glanced at the pad, and did not look at Qiusheng’s table.

“Mr Reporter. Upstairs.”

The two of them went upstairs. Qiusheng drank what was left in his tea bowl and went out by the back door. Three stone steps in the alley behind, and the patch where the moss was worn away was still there.

On Sunday Old Lu came as always, down to the cellar after work, squatted a quarter of an hour by the four bricks at the very back, patted the bricks, and went up and out. Not one question.

The next Wednesday Counsel Liang said the paper’s editor was very fond of tea too. Nobody ever saw the piece again.

“Mm,” said Qiusheng.

---

In February the cellar was chilly. The big stove in the sauce-vat yard above did not burn its hardest until midday, and the steam that rolled in through the vent windows had only a little warmth left by the time it reached the bottom.

His cough was getting worse. Sometimes in the cellar he coughed until he was bent double, and Ye Mingde, standing by, turned his head away to look at the thermometer.

“Mr He, you ought to see a doctor,” Ye Mingde said one day.

“I have.”

“And what did the doctor say?”

Qiusheng put the handkerchief away in his pocket. There was another spot of red on it.

“The doctor says I should do what I love.”

Ye Mingde pushed his spectacles up. His pencil rested on his notebook and did not write.

It was he who brought word of the lecture. A card, printed by the branch office, one to every bar owner in Upper Town; Madam Wen’s bookkeeper had asked for one more and given it to him. On the card was one line: Inspector Qiao Zheng of the Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau, will lecture on Thursday 12 February at seven in the evening in the lecture hall of the town hall; the liquor trade and the press are welcome.

“Madam Wen told me to go and listen,” said Ye Mingde. “Mr He, what he’s lecturing on is your report.”

Qiusheng glanced at the card and put it on the workbench.

Old Qi spoke from his corner. “I’ll be by the door.”

---

Qiao Zheng gave a lecture at the town hall: ‘How to Recognise Poisoned Bootleg’. The hall was full of bar owners and reporters, and his teaching material was a report Qiusheng had written twelve years ago.

The town hall stood at the corner of the ring road in Upper Town, three storeys, grey stone. The lecture hall was on the first floor: twelve rows of long wooden benches, an aisle down the middle. On the platform a blackboard and a table, and on the table a spirit lamp, two distilling flasks, two measuring cylinders, a thermometer, two bottles. At ten to seven more than a hundred people were in their seats.

The first two rows were bar owners, long gowns of silk, fur-lined overcoats, gold watch chains across their bellies, sitting one against another, talking low. Boss Ding was in the second row by the aisle, the collar of his camel-hair overcoat turned up, his eyes shut, his head nodding. In the third row three reporters, pads open on their knees, pencils between their teeth. In the fifth row by the aisle was Counsel Liang, in a pale grey suit; he took out his handkerchief and wiped his forehead once, and wiped it again. In the seventh row sat Ye Mingde, his overcoat off and laid on his knees, his notebook open, his pencil sharpened to a point. Old Qi stood by the back door, his back against the door frame, his railway uniform buttoned to the top, looking not at the platform but at the aisle. Qiusheng sat in the back row, in the seat by the wall, his coat still on, both hands on his knees.

Qiao Zheng came in by the side door at seven exactly. The khaki uniform, the badge at his collar, an annual report with its blue cover faded to grey, a sheaf of papers. He put the annual report on the table, and his finger pressed for a moment on the badge at his collar.

“Gentlemen of the trade, gentlemen of the press,” he said. His voice was not loud, and it carried through the lecture hall. “The rule of the Prohibition Bureau is that it does not teach people to drink. But in three years of the Temperance Act, more men have died on the docks each year than before the Act. So today I shall teach you one thing: how to tell whether a bottle of spirit will blind a man.”

He turned and wrote two figures on the blackboard. Sixty-four and a half degrees. Seventy-eight.

“Methanol comes over at sixty-four and a half degrees. Ethanol comes over at seventy-eight degrees. Whatever a glass of spirit hides, it cannot hide it over a flame.”

Qiusheng looked at the blackboard. The chalk figures were upright, each stroke separate, none joined.

Qiao Zheng lit the spirit lamp. The first bottle went into a distilling flask, the flame was moved under it, the mercury climbed. The lecture hall went quiet. Someone in the front row leaned forward, and the man beside him tugged his sleeve. Sixty-four and a half degrees. It stopped. The mouth of the tube dripped, and an oily sheen rose in the measuring cylinder. Qiao Zheng lifted the measuring cylinder, held it to the lamp and walked to the edge of the platform to let the first two rows see.

“This bottle was sold last month by the Old Town roundabout. Eighty cents,” he said. “Two men went blind.”

He poured the second bottle into the other distilling flask. This time too the mercury stopped at sixty-four and a half degrees, and went on up. Nothing rose in the measuring cylinder. He lifted the measuring cylinder, and the men in the first two rows craned their necks.

“This bottle was sold on the docks. A dollar fifty,” said Qiao Zheng. “The man who made this bottle poured the heads away. The first cupful, up to seventy-eight degrees, all of it poured away.”

Someone in the front row laughed. “Inspector Qiao, everything he pours away is money.”

Boss Ding woke, opened his eyes and shut them again.

“The cupful poured away, sir, comes to less than a tael in a bottle,” said Qiao Zheng.

The man stopped laughing. The reporters’ pencils moved on their pads.

Qiao Zheng opened the annual report. He turned to a page, its edge worn to a fuzz, and held the page up to show the hall the curve in its corner.

“This curve is from the Assay Office’s annual report of twelve years ago, ‘The Behaviour of Methanol in Fractional Distillation’. The man who wrote it—”

He raised his head. His eyes went over twelve rows of benches and came to rest on the back row, on the seat by the wall. He stopped for the space of half a sentence.

“—set the temperature for cutting the heads at seventy-eight degrees. He said: collect only once it is past seventy-eight. Cut one degree more, and one man fewer goes blind.”

He looked down at the annual report and went on. In the seventh row Ye Mingde’s pencil stopped a moment and moved again. Counsel Liang’s handkerchief stopped on his forehead. By the back door Old Qi’s eyes moved from the aisle to the platform and back to the aisle.

He spoke for an hour. At the end he set the two measuring cylinders side by side on the table, one with the sheen, one without.

“On the day you gentlemen open your doors for business,” he said, “before you take in a bottle, light a lamp.”

It was over. The front rows stood up first and crowded into the aisle. Boss Ding was helped towards the door by the floor manager. The reporters gathered at the edge of the platform. Ye Mingde shut his notebook, pushed forward through the crowd, stopped, and looked back once towards the back row. Counsel Liang put his handkerchief back in his pocket and stood in the aisle at the fifth row, going neither forward nor back.

Qiusheng stood up. He was in the back row, three paces from the back door. Old Qi did not look at him. He looked at the aisle. Qiusheng passed Old Qi, went out by the back door and down the stairs. Twenty-four stone steps.

At the door of the town hall the lamps of the ring road were lit, and far off a tram bell rang once. The fog had climbed up from the docks. It had climbed as far as Upper Town, and every street lamp was a blur. Out by the breakwater the foghorn sounded once, low, half the town away.

He walked along the road, down the hill. A dozen paces on, in the first-floor windows of the town hall behind him, the lights of the lecture hall went out row by row.
