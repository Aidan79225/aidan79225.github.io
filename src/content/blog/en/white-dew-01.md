*July · After Lesser Heat*

The fog in Fog Harbour rolled in from the sea, layer upon layer. First it swallowed the lamps on the breakwater, then the cranes on the docks, and at last even the white-painted sign at the door of the Customs Assay Office could not be read. Only one stroke of the character for assay still floated in the fog, like a splinter that had not been pulled clean out.

He Qiusheng had stood beneath that sign for twenty years.

Twenty years, half past seven. The doorman did not have to look up to know him by his step. The July fog was warm and clung to the skin and would not let go. By the time he reached the door his shirt was stuck to his back. The newsboy was squatting under the sign with a bundle of the Fog Harbour Daily hugged to his chest to keep it from the damp. When he saw Qiusheng he stood up. Qiusheng found a cent and took a paper for it, folded the paper twice and pushed it into his coat pocket. He did not look at it.

The coat went on the third peg. The pegs to left and right of it were empty. One had been Old Zhang’s, who had gone last year, and one Old Wu’s, who had gone the year before. Under Old Zhang’s a black cloth umbrella still leaned against the foot of the wall. In a year no one had come for it. The two men’s benches were still there too, piled with the new forms the Prohibition Bureau had sent, bundle on bundle, tied with hemp string. The assay room faced the sea. Three benches in a row, and pinned to the wall a boiling-point table, the paper gone yellow and curling at the corners. The smell of spirit from the spirit lamps had not cleared in twenty years. The drawer slid open, the velvet-lined case opened. The hydrometer had been in use eleven years. Once over with the chamois, and the glass tube held up to the window. A foggy day gave no light. He turned slightly to one side and borrowed a little of the grey-white to see whether there were bubbles in the tube. There were none. He gave it a nod, as he would an old colleague.

“Morning, Master He.”

Young Zhou was already settled on the high stool beside him, oversleeves pulled up to the elbow, the forms spread on his knees. He was a little over twenty and had been at the Office a year and a half. He wrote more than he spoke. Qiusheng nodded and pulled on his own oversleeves.

Young Zhou handed him one of the forms from his knees. A new one. The paper was whiter than the old, and in the top right corner was printed the Bureau’s emblem, a bottle with a cross struck through it. The headings were the same as the old ones: sample number, place of seizure, specific gravity, boiling range, methanol content. The last column had been a blank line. Now two boxes were printed there, one Pass, one Fail, waiting for a tick.

“The old ones ran out yesterday,” said Young Zhou.

Qiusheng glanced at the two boxes and handed the form back.

The first sample of the day was a crate of “medicinal wine” seized on the docks. On the wooden crate was chalked “15 July · No. 3 Wharf”, and the customs seal was torn half across. The bottles were printed in red with the name of some medicine hall or other, and at the bottom of each sat a layer of sediment no one could have put a name to. He tilted a bottle and the sediment slid slowly across. He drew the stopper.

He did not drink it. An assayer does not drink. That was the first thing he had learned in the trade.

He brought the mouth of the bottle up under his nose and wafted lightly with his palm. The sweetness came up at him, a sharp sweetness, the kind he had watched for twenty years at sixty-four and a half degrees.

“Methanol,” he said.

Beside him Young Zhou bent his head and wrote. The nib paused. “How much?”

“Over four per cent.” Qiusheng put the bottle back in the crate. “This crate could blind a whole ship’s crew.”

Young Zhou wrote again. At the verdict column his pen stopped. “The director says on the new form we’re to put Pass or Fail.”

“Write Poisonous.”

“…Poisonous.” Young Zhou wrote the one word between the two boxes, the strokes lying over the lines. “The director will send it back.”

“Then write it again. Pass or fail is for those above us.” Qiusheng was already lighting the spirit lamp. The flame jumped and went blue. He did not go on.

He took the specific gravity first. He poured the measuring cylinder half full and lowered the hydrometer in. The glass tube bobbed twice on the surface and held. He bent to read the scale and called out a figure, and Young Zhou wrote. Then, by the rules, he drew a sample, heated it, condensed it, caught the distillate drop by drop in a measuring cylinder and watched the mercury in the thermometer climb to sixty-four and a half degrees, stop, and climb again. He liked this part. Whatever a glass of spirit hid, it could not hide it over a flame. What was meant to come over at a given degree came over at that degree, not one degree out.

Young Zhou leaned over to look. “Master He, it’s at sixty-four. Do I put it down?”

“No hurry. Put it down when it stops.”

The mercury stopped. On the surface in the measuring cylinder an oily sheen had risen, very thin, plain to see when the lamp caught it. Young Zhou looked once at the oil and bent his head to write. He wrote slowly, stroke by stroke, pressing harder than usual.

Qiusheng coughed once.

Once, no more. He fished a handkerchief out of his trouser pocket and pressed it to his mouth. His other hand still steadied the measuring cylinder and did not let go. Young Zhou looked up. Qiusheng explained nothing. He put the handkerchief away and watched the mercury. When the mercury passed seventy-eight he moved the spirit lamp away.

“Change bottles.”

Twelve bottles to the crate, every bottle the same. After nine a customs officer came pushing a handcart and unloaded two more crates, the same chalk on them, No. 3 Wharf. “Master He, these are off the same ship,” said the officer. “Give us a stamp.”

“Which ship?”

“A freighter up from the south. A false bottom in the hold. The ship’s master says it’s medicine.”

Qiusheng stamped the receipt. The officer pushed the handcart off, its wheels rattling down the corridor all the way to the door.

Young Zhou looked at the two crates, then at the stack of forms on his knees, not yet filled in.

“Master He, the same ship, the same stuff—”

“Same or not, it gets assayed.” Qiusheng drew the stopper of the seventh bottle. “The reports get written one by one.”

He held the seventh bottle out. “You smell it.”

Young Zhou took it and put his nose to the mouth of the bottle.

“Waft with your hand. Keep your nose back.”

Young Zhou held the bottle a little further off and wafted once over the mouth with his palm, then once more. “Sweet.”

“Remember that sweetness.” Qiusheng took the bottle back. “Smell it once and you won’t mistake it a second time.”

Young Zhou took a fresh form. By the ninth bottle he had stopped asking. He finished a sheet, tore it off, laid it on the pile at his left hand and waited for Qiusheng to call the next figure. Outside the window the crane chains rattled for a while and stopped. The doorman was mopping the corridor, the mop knocking against the foot of the wall, stroke after stroke. At five to ten the doorman put his head in. “Master He.” He pointed the mop handle down the corridor.

Qiusheng stoppered the bottle in his hand and put it back in the crate, took off his oversleeves and hung them over the back of the chair. The hydrometer was still on the bench, lying across the chamois. He did not put it away.

---

At ten o’clock the director asked him into his office.

The office was at the end of the corridor, behind a glass door with frosted paper pasted over the glass. The director sat behind his desk. On the desk were a teapot, two cups and an official letter. The chief accountant stood by the filing cabinet. He did not sit. He held a book in both hands with a pencil shut in it. Qiusheng sat down. The chair was the one kept for outsiders, a few inches lower than the rest, and the desk top came level with his chest.

The director spoke first. “Old He, sit down.” Qiusheng was already sitting. The director said it all the same. Then he lifted the teapot and poured Qiusheng a cup. It was the teapot the Office had used for ten years and more. A piece was gone from the spout, and it had to be held crooked to pour.

The director was a good man, and when a good man does a bad thing he always pours a cup of tea first. Qiusheng watched the tea leaves slowly open at the bottom of the cup and listened as the director went from the Temperance Act to Parliament, to the budget, to the Prohibition Bureau taking over every assay office in the country, to “the new establishment doesn’t need so many of the old hands”.

The official letter lay under the director’s right hand the whole time. The letterhead was printed in red. His fingers rested on the red, and with each stretch of talk they moved an inch. Part-way through he looked down and read one sentence off it: “Technical staff of the former Customs Assay Office are to be retained on merit.” When he had read it his finger moved to the words “on merit” and stopped there. When he came to “take over” he turned the letter face down.

The chief accountant turned a page of his book. “Old He, the severance pay goes by years of service. It’s not a small sum.” He said that much, shut the book, and did not open his mouth again.

The tea leaves were half open. A few still floated on the water and would not go down.

“They’re keeping the building,” said the director. “The sign is to change as well. Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau. The white-painted one they say they won’t take down. They’ll nail a new one over it.”

Qiusheng looked at the cup.

“I’ve seen the takeover list,” said the director. “On the Bureau’s side, Qiao Zheng is among the inspectors. The one you trained. Promoted only last month, the fastest of the young men.” He paused and waited for Qiusheng to speak. Qiusheng lifted the cup and set it down again. The director had to go on by himself. “With him here, the ways of the Office won’t be lost.”

“Mm.”

“Old He, you’re the best in the Office,” said the director. “That’s why I’m telling you myself.”

Qiusheng nodded. He did not know what face a man was supposed to wear when he was told he was the best and then made redundant. He thought only that the hydrometer was still lying on the bench. It had not been put back in its case.

“And Young Zhou?” he asked.

“The young ones stay,” said the director. “The list is theirs, not mine.” He pressed flat a corner of the letter that had curled up. “What will you do afterwards?”

Qiusheng looked at the tea leaves in the cup. The director waited a while. Nothing came, and he lifted his own cup and drank a mouthful.

“The end of the month,” said the director. “You work to the end of this month. The severance pay comes all together at the end of the month, and the Office will stand you a dinner.”

“No need.”

The chief accountant drew a slip out of his book and put it in front of Qiusheng with the pencil laid on it. Qiusheng picked up the pencil and signed his name. The chief accountant put the slip back in the book, pulled open the filing cabinet, put the book in and pushed it shut. The steel door clanged once. The director turned the letter face up again and pushed it to the corner of the desk, and his hand left it. The tea had gone half cold. Qiusheng had not drunk it. He stood up. The director stood up too and held out his hand across the desk. Qiusheng shook it. The hand was dry.

The corridor was long. When he got back to the bench Young Zhou stood up, then sat down again. The hydrometer lay on the chamois with its glass tube towards the window. No one had touched it. Qiusheng looked at it. Outside the window the fog was a layer thinner. The outlines of the cranes showed, their chains hanging still.

“Master He, the director sent for you—”

“Next bottle.”

He pulled his oversleeves back on, picked up the hydrometer, looked into the tube first, and then lowered it into the measuring cylinder.

The crate took until eleven. Twelve reports lay stacked on the left. The last one he wrote himself. He did not look at the two boxes in the verdict column. He wrote one word between them, then his signature, then the date. Young Zhou stamped the date on the sheets, one by one, and when he had done he carried the reports to the director’s office. At half past eleven the doorman brought the stack back. Twelve sheets, the director’s stamp on every one, the verdict column untouched. Qiusheng took off his oversleeves, folded them and put them in the drawer. He wiped the hydrometer and put it away in the velvet-lined case. He took his coat down from the third peg and laid it over his arm. The day was too hot to wear it.

“Master He, those two crates—”

“Tomorrow.”

In twenty years he had never once left early. Young Zhou opened his mouth and did not ask. The doorman watched him walk out and did not ask either. At the door stood a painter with a tape measure in his hand, head back, measuring the white-painted sign. When he had measured he wrote a figure on his palm.

“How old is this one, Master?”

“Twenty years.”

“The paint’s still good,” said the painter. “Pity it’s to be covered over.”

Qiusheng walked past him. The sign was in the fog. The one stroke still floated.

He walked along the railway line by the docks. It was noon and the fog had not lifted. Under the cranes a row of workers sat eating, lunch tins on their knees. One man brought a flat bottle out from inside his shirt, took a mouthful and passed it to the man beside him; that man drank and passed it on. In the few steps it took Qiusheng to go by, the wind blew over from their side. Sweet. He did not stop. The pot at the noodle stall was steaming under its canvas. The owner stirred with long chopsticks and gave him a look. He walked to the corner of Warehouse No. 7 and got on the bus for the South Slope roundabout. The fare was ten cents.

The bus was empty. The conductor dozed against the door. Qiusheng sat by the window with his coat on his knees. The bus passed the Customs wall and the union’s red-brick gate, and went over the level crossing at the goods yard—the barrier was up, no train had come. On the climb the bus shook. He pressed his hand to his coat pocket. The newspaper was still there.

---

He did not go home that afternoon. He went first to Dr Lin’s surgery on South Slope, to collect the X-ray taken the week before.

The bus stopped at the South Slope roundabout. He got down and walked up the slope. Shops lined both sides: a rice shop, the chemist’s, a stationer’s that sold school things, with the badge of the girls’ school hanging at its door. Further up was the wall of the municipal primary school. Lessons were on inside, and the sound of children reciting came over the wall in waves. The girls’ school was at the top of the slope, out of sight. The July sun was behind the fog. The light was white, the flagstones were wet, and ten steps brought out the sweat.

The surgery was halfway up the slope of South Slope, on the first floor. At the turn of the stairs hung a wooden board: Physician · Diseases of the Chest. Three people sat on the waiting bench. An electric fan turned overhead and blew the smell of medicine out to the head of the stairs. Qiusheng took the seat nearest the wall. Next to him was a woman holding a child, and the child was asleep; beyond her an old man who coughed every so often and after each cough bent to look into the spittoon. Qiusheng drew the newspaper out of his pocket and spread it on his knees.

The front page was the provincial capital’s news. At the foot of page two was one column under the headline FORTY-ONE DEAD OF POISONING ON THE DOCKS LAST MONTH: BAD LIQUOR BLAMED, and below it a short paragraph saying the Prohibition Bureau had sent men to investigate, and that most of the dead were dock coolies. He read the column twice and folded the paper back. When his name was called he put the paper back in his pocket.

In the consulting room were a desk, a light box and a medicine cabinet along one wall. Dr Lin listened to the chest first. The stethoscope stayed a long time on the right side, moved down, stayed again; the left it only touched. “Breathe in. Again. Cough for me.” Qiusheng coughed for him. Dr Lin hung the stethoscope back round his neck, slid the film onto the light box and with the end of his pencil tapped a blurred patch of white at the top of the right lung. “Infiltration,” he said. “There’s a cavity now. Mr He, how long have you been coughing?”

“Since the summer came in.”

“You should have come sooner.” Dr Lin took off his glasses. “You need hospital, you need injections, you need medicine, you need the sanatorium in the hills. The whole of it comes to—”

He put his glasses back on, pulled open a drawer, took out a ledger, opened it, and shut it again.

“Three hundred dollars. The sanatorium is extra, fifty a month. Every two weeks you come in to have air pumped. It presses the lung on that side down and lets it rest.”

In his head Qiusheng divided the figure by his monthly salary, then by the monthly salary he no longer had. It would not divide.

“Does the pumping hurt?”

“More than not pumping,” said Dr Lin. “The needle goes in between the ribs. Each time, you lie down for half a day. The first few times the chest feels tight.”

“How long in hospital?”

“A month to begin with. The hills depend on how it goes. Six months at the least,” said Dr Lin. “The air is dry in the hills. What that lung of yours wants is dry and quiet.”

“No hospital. Only the medicine?”

“Medicine alone won’t hold it down.” Dr Lin tapped the patch of white again with the end of his pencil. “Mr He, this thing waits for no one. It grows by itself.”

Qiusheng looked at the light box. Under the lamp the patch of white had no edge. It lay sunk in the top corner of the lobe.

“We’ll see,” he said.

Dr Lin gave him a look and did not press him. “No tobacco and no drink.”

“I don’t smoke. I don’t drink.”

“Then it’s overwork. Fewer late nights,” said Dr Lin. “How many at home?”

“Three. There’s a child.”

“Keep your bowl and chopsticks separate. Scald your handkerchiefs in boiling water every day.”

Qiusheng nodded.

“Do they know at home?”

“No.”

“You must tell them,” said Dr Lin. “This illness is not one person’s affair.”

Qiusheng bent his head and did his shirt buttons up again, all the way to the collar. Dr Lin waited a moment, then reached for the prescription pad. He wrote the name of a medicine, paused at the bottom, and added another line. When he had done he put the pencil back in his pocket, tore the sheet off and held it out.

“The top line is for the lung. Have it made up at the chemist’s. The bottom line is for the cough. It’s cheap. If the coughing keeps you awake at night, take one.” He opened the ledger again, made an entry, and shut it. “The fee is as before. Nothing for the cough line.”

Qiusheng folded the prescription in four and put it in the pocket that held the newspaper. Dr Lin did not give him the film. He took it down, slid it back into its paper envelope and put it away in the drawer.

“Two weeks,” said Dr Lin. “Come back in two weeks and give me an answer.”

---

When he came out of the surgery the fog had lifted a little. The sun pressed down through the gaps and the flagstones steamed. Schoolgirls let out for the day were coming down the slope of South Slope in twos and threes, their blue uniform skirts turning over in the wind. He looked for Xiaoman among them and could not find her, and only then remembered that the girls’ school had extra lessons until five today.

Xiaoman meant to sit for the normal school. Next term’s fees were one sum; the deposit for the normal school was another. At the tailor’s stall Su Qi could alter ten uniforms a day, twenty cents each.

The schoolgirls went by. At the roadside only a carrying-pole stand was left, two big copper kettles with cloth wrapped round their spouts. Qiusheng stood halfway up the slope for a long time, so long that an old man selling herbal tea asked him whether he wanted a bowl.

Only then did he feel that sweat had soaked the whole back of his shirt. It stuck to his back and went cold whenever he moved. He nodded. The old man poured a bowl, dark brown, with a little bitterness rising off it. He drank it in one go and handed the bowl back. The old man wiped it with a cloth and set it upside down on the edge of the stand.

“You’re early today, sir,” said the old man. “Most days it’s past six before you come up the slope. Your colour’s poor. Herbal tea takes the heat down. Another bowl?”

Qiusheng shook his head, found some copper coins and put them on the stand, and walked on down the slope.

He came down as far as the wall of the municipal primary school. Below the wall was the South Slope market, and the sounds of packing up carried to him, boards being stacked one on another. Su Qi’s stall was at the very back of the market. He did not cut through the market. He went round by the outside road.

The chemist’s was at the foot of the slope, a few steps from the roundabout, with a small sign at the door: Liquor Sold on Prescription. Behind the counter stood a row of brown bottles with prescription tags hung round their necks. The chemist took the prescription and looked at the top line, then at the bottom line.

“One of Dr Lin’s,” said the chemist. “This top line is imported. It’s dear. Do you want both made up?”

“The bottom line.”

The chemist read the name of the medicine aloud, counted tablets out of a drawer, wrapped them in paper, put a small stamp at the bottom of the prescription and handed it back. The top line had no stamp. Qiusheng paid. The packet went into his pocket, squeezed in with the newspaper and the prescription.

Outside the chemist’s he looked back up the slope. No one was coming down from the direction of the girls’ school. He pressed his palm to the right side of his chest, held it there a moment, let go and walked on.

---

He was home a little after four. The flat was on the second floor. Mrs Chen’s wireless downstairs sounded through the floorboards, playing opera from the provincial capital. There was no one in. The windows were all open, and the washing on the balcony had dried and hung stiff. He hung up his coat and left the newspaper in the pocket with the prescription. He sat down in the rattan chair and did not turn on the lamp.

At half past five Xiaoman came in and slung her satchel onto a chair. She saw him and stopped.

“Papa, you’re early today.”

“Mm.”

“Extra lessons went on till five, and then our teacher kept us another quarter of an hour.” She went into the kitchen to wash her hands and put her head out again. “Where’s Mama?”

“Not back yet.”

The wireless downstairs gave six o’clock and went on to the news. When it came to the word “docks” Mrs Chen turned the sound down, and the rest could not be made out.

Xiaoman pushed the balcony door open a little and said it was stuffy indoors. She wiped the table and laid three sets of bowls and chopsticks. At her own place her hand stopped. She drew a sheet of paper out of her satchel, looked at it, folded it, tucked it under her bowl, and set the bowl straight.

At six Su Qi came home, a vegetable basket in one hand, the cloth bundle from the tailor’s stall under the other arm. She put the bundle on the chair by the door and carried the basket into the kitchen. Passing the rattan chair she gave Qiusheng a look.

“You’re back.”

“Mm.”

The tap began to run. Xiaoman followed her into the kitchen saying she would help, was sent out again, and sat back down at the table with both hands pressed on her bowl.

---

At supper he said nothing.

The table stood against the balcony door, three people on three sides. Xiaoman had her back to the front door and a bowl of rice in front of her; Qiusheng sat on the side that faced the balcony door, which was open, and over towards the docks all was grey-white; Su Qi sat by the kitchen door, where she could get up for more food without going round the table. On the table were a steamed fish, a plate of fried water spinach and a bowl of loofah soup. Su Qi had a palm-leaf fan in one hand, and fanned as she turned the fish and picked out its bones.

Xiaoman spoke first. She began the moment she sat down, before she had her chopsticks properly in hand.

“Papa, today our Chinese teacher’s glasses fell in the goldfish bowl. He was halfway through reading out the lesson and he looked down at the book and they just slid off. Plop. All the goldfish shot to the bottom. Nobody in the class dared laugh, and then he laughed first himself, and he put his hand in to fish them out and got his sleeve wet to the elbow.”

Su Qi’s fan stopped a moment and went on. “Eat.”

“I am eating.” Xiaoman shovelled in a mouthful of rice. “Our mathematics teacher says I can sit for the normal school in Upper Town, if my English gets a bit stronger. The examination’s next June. Chinese, arithmetic, English and general knowledge, and she says I don’t need coaching in arithmetic. She says my *grammar* will do, it’s only my *vocabulary* that’s short. Three girls in my class go to Upper Town for English coaching, twice a week.”

Su Qi turned the fish over. “The girls’ school is changing to summer skirts. I let down eleven skirts today.”

“Mama, mine’s short too.”

“I’ll let it down when you’ve finished growing.”

Xiaoman lifted her rice bowl and shifted it towards the edge of the table. A corner of the paper underneath showed, printed with the name of the girls’ school. She did not look at the paper. She went on about which street the coaching class was in and which of the teachers came from the provincial capital.

Su Qi finished boning the fish, laid the fan in her lap, turned her chopsticks round and with the blunt ends drew the paper out from under the bowl. Printed on it was “Tuition and sundry fees, next term: thirty-two dollars”, with a red stamp below. She looked at it once, folded it in half, folded it again and put it in her apron pocket. She said nothing. She turned the chopsticks back and put a piece of the fish’s belly into Xiaoman’s bowl.

Xiaoman talked faster. “Papa, were any of your night-school students cleverer than me?”

Qiusheng thought of someone. The English and arithmetic class at the night school four years ago, the back row by the window. Always late, and the first thing he did inside the door was wipe his hands twice on his trousers. The algebra he handed in was the best in the class. Qiusheng could not think where that boy was now.

“There was one,” he said. “Better than you at algebra.”

“Algebra doesn’t count,” said Xiaoman. “I asked about clever.”

“Algebra is clever.”

Xiaoman humphed and bent to her rice. Su Qi picked up the fan, fanned twice towards Xiaoman, then towards Qiusheng.

“I altered two hundred uniforms last month,” she said. “Twenty cents each. If you want the coaching, go and have it.”

Xiaoman raised her head and lowered it again. “What about the deposit for the normal school?”

“That’s next year,” said Su Qi. “Eat.”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He pushed the bowl of loofah soup a little way towards Xiaoman.

Halfway through the soup Su Qi looked at him.

“The cough today?”

“Less.”

“I heard all of it last night,” said Xiaoman. “Through the wall.”

“Eat,” said Su Qi. From over at the docks came one long whistle, far off.

“The Office?”

Qiusheng put a piece of fish into Xiaoman’s bowl. “Busy.”

---

After supper Xiaoman learned her English words at the table, the dictionary open, her finger pressed under each word as she read down the page. When she did not know one she stopped and asked. Qiusheng sat in the rattan chair with the Fog Harbour Daily turned to page two and spread on his knees. He never turned the page. Su Qi was mending the hem of a uniform under the lamp, three pins held in her mouth, taking them out one at a time and pinning them into the cloth.

“Papa, *temperance*.”

“Moderation.”

“As in the Temperance Act?”

“Mm.”

“And *inspector*?”

“An officer who inspects,” said Qiusheng. “A man who looks into things.”

Xiaoman wrote two characters in the margin of the dictionary and turned the page. “Papa, test me. You read the English and I’ll say the Chinese.”

He closed the newspaper and read from her dictionary. “*Harbour*.”

“A port.”

“*Fog*.”

“Thick mist.”

“*Clean*.”

“Not dirty,” said Xiaoman. “That one’s too easy.”

“Get the easy ones by heart first,” he said.

Su Qi put in the last pin, bit off the thread and smoothed the cloth flat with her fingers. She took the school bill out of her apron pocket, still folded in its small square, and tucked it under the sewing box.

“Bed,” she said. “You have extra lessons tomorrow.”

Xiaoman went off to her room hugging the dictionary. Two lamps went out. The one by the balcony door was left. Su Qi folded the uniform and put it in the cloth bundle, and put the bundle on the chair by the door to take to the stall tomorrow. The sewing box she did not put away. It stayed on the corner of the table.

Qiusheng got up and went into the kitchen for a drink of water. On the rack the three sets of bowls and chopsticks were drying together. He looked at them and did not touch them. Back in the bedroom he coughed twice on the edge of the bed.

“The chemist’s has a cough mixture,” Su Qi said in the dark. “Buy a bottle tomorrow.”

“Mm.”

The coat hung behind the door. The packet of tablets in the pocket he did not go and fetch.

---

That night the coughing woke him. Afraid of disturbing Su Qi, he went out onto the balcony. He passed Xiaoman’s door. It stood open a crack, and the dictionary still lay open on her desk.

The July night was not cold, and he wore only a singlet. The lamps of South Slope had gone out one by one. Only over at the docks were they still lit, and the fog blurred the lamplight into dim yellow balls of fluff. Cicadas shrilled in the trees below the slope; further off the crane chains sounded in the night, slack, then taut. Someone was unloading a night ship.

The foghorn on the breakwater gave its low roar once a minute. He counted the blasts and waited for the coughing to stop. One, two, and the cough bent him double with a hand on the rail; four, five, and the thing in his throat loosened a little; at the seventh the coughing stopped. He straightened up and pressed his palm to the right side of his chest. Through the singlet that side was hotter than the other. Indoors Su Qi turned over. The bed boards creaked once and were quiet.

He thought of the crate of medicinal wine that morning, of the oily sheen in the measuring cylinder, of the Fog Harbour Daily saying that forty-one workers had died on the docks last month, all from drinking firewater. Forty-one people. At the Assay Office he had found this stuff in hundreds of crates and written hundreds of reports. The reports went up, the crates were sealed, and the next day men on the docks drank it all the same.

He had assayed spirit for twenty years and knew how a good glass of spirit ought to be made. How the rice should be soaked, which yeast to use, where the temperature should hold in the distilling, that the heads must be cut and the tails cut clean. Methanol came over at sixty-four and a half degrees. So long as someone was willing to wait those few minutes, willing to cut the first cupful, a glass of spirit would not send a man blind. He knew this better than anyone in Fog Harbour.

There was a film of damp on the rail. Qiusheng laid his hand on it and coughed again for a while, and in the phlegm he brought up there was a little red. He looked at it for a time, folded it into his handkerchief and put it in his pocket.

In the pocket of the coat behind the door the prescription was still there, folded in four, its corners gone soft with sweat, no stamp on the top line. He did not go in for it.

Tomorrow he would still walk in at half past seven, and he would stay at the Assay Office to the end of the month. After that he would not be an assayer.

Then what was he?

The fog was rolling in from the sea again.
