*October · Cold Dew*

On the afternoon of Cold Dew, Counsel Liang asked Qiusheng to the Nanji again and said there was someone he should meet.

This time too they did not go upstairs.

The main room of the Nanji had few customers in the afternoon. Of its seven or eight tables half stood empty, and the wireless on the counter was still playing the same piece of Cantonese music. The little stage in the corner was empty, the lamp on the stool unlit, the pipa in its cloth cover leaning against the leg of the stool. At the table by the stairs sat an old man. The table stood against the wall. He had his back to the wall, his face to the main door, and whoever came in at the door, he saw first. Still the same railway uniform, washed nearly white. Seen close, the brass buttons at the collar were down to three. The tea bowl was at his left hand, not his right. The tea in it was pale, the cheapest oolong on the price board, ten cents a pot. He drank it a mouthful at a time. The bowl went up, the bowl went down, exactly onto the ring it had left on the table.

Counsel Liang sat opposite him with his back to the door and could not sit easy. Every little while he looked round. Qiusheng sat down at the side, facing the stairs. The waiter brought a pot of the same oolong and two bowls.

“Qi Zhenbang. Thirty years with the railway police,” Counsel Liang said under his breath. “Since he retired he has collected debts for Madam Wen and smoothed things over for me. The Mr Qi Madam Wen spoke of to you at Futai. This is the man.”

Qiusheng nodded.

“Teacher, if you’re going to do business with Madam Wen, you need a man like this beside you.”

“I don’t need a bodyguard.”

“He isn’t a bodyguard,” said Counsel Liang. “He is a man who knows which road has police on it, which buyer is heading for trouble, when to move and when to stop. With him there, your business will have a great deal less trouble.”

The old man did not put in a word. From the docks came a whistle, a long way off, held for two beats. He did not look up.

“The three-fifteen goods,” he said. “Late.”

Counsel Liang gave him a look, and looked round again at the door.

Old Qi raised his head. His clouded eyes rested a moment on Qiusheng’s face and dropped back into the tea bowl.

“So you’re the Teacher,” he said. His voice was hoarse.

“I am.”

“I’ve drunk your liquor,” said Old Qi. “Forty years I’ve been drinking. The best cup I ever had was thirty years ago, at an old still in the provincial capital. Your White Dew is better than that cup.”

He finished his tea and stood up.

“Three hundred dollars a month, and I don’t haggle,” he said. “And another thing. I don’t kill people.”

Qiusheng looked at him. “I don’t need you to kill anyone.”

Old Qi nodded, as if he had expected the answer.

“Then we’ll get on fine.”

He walked to the door, looked first to the left, then to the right, before he stepped out from under the arcade. Only then did Counsel Liang take out his handkerchief and wipe his forehead.

“Teacher, three hundred dollars. A month,” he said. “The manager of a foreign firm in Upper Town doesn’t draw that figure. But let me say this at the outset: he has collected Madam Wen’s debts for ten years and never been a cent out. The police on the roads see him and call him Captain Qi. What the three hundred buys is the road, not the man.”

“And a truck?”

“The bookkeeper has bought one,” said Counsel Liang. “A trading house’s cast-off. It will be at the goods yard tomorrow.”

Qiusheng glanced at Old Qi’s tea bowl. A ring of leaf in the bottom, and not a drop of water on the rim. He drank his own.

The waiter came to clear the bowls and asked whether Mr Qi would be coming again tomorrow.

“He comes every day,” said Counsel Liang. “Always this table.”

The last run on the Fair Wind was on the night of 11 October.

High tide was at ten. Once the fire was lit the hold was as hot as in September, and sweat dripped from his eyebrows onto the thermometer. The difference was above the hatch: five dock boys squatting on the deck, not allowed down, a steamed bun each. Ah Yue called their names down the hatch for Qiusheng to hear: Ah Chang, the one who rode beside the driver; Ah Shui, the youngest, seventeen; and three more whose names Qiusheng forgot as soon as he had heard them.

When the heart had been collected they drew the fire, a quarter of an hour before the ebb. The charcoal was doused, white smoke went up out of the hatch, and the boys above moved aside. There was a sound from under the boat, and the boards began to tilt. Ah Yue lifted off the pot lid. Qiusheng drew out the thermometer, wiped it dry and put it in its case.

Stripping the boat took most of the night. The vats were carried out of the forward hold and hoisted up through the hatch one at a time, and the five boys took them. The copper pot was roped with hemp, and four of them carried it. The condenser came out last, drawn an inch at a time out of the mud under the porthole. The pipe had lain two months in the mud, and when it was out it left a track on the flats, green, running all the way to the water’s edge. Ah Shui squatted and put his hand to the green, and a film of it came off on his fingers. Before daylight the tide would cover it.

Qiusheng did not rub out the times of high tide chalked on the bulkhead. The tear-off calendar he took down and folded into his pocket.

The truck stood at the head of the sea wall. It was the trading house’s cast-off. The boards of the bed had been replaced and the paint on the cab had not. The trading house’s lettering was still there, half of it painted out in black. The bookkeeper had put up the money and said it was for carrying Futai’s sauce vats. Ah Yue had driven the trading house’s little delivery truck. A real truck was another matter. He had been three days learning and had knocked down two posts in the goods yard. He was hitting the second when Old Qi happened to walk past, and Old Qi got in and said only one thing: “Lighter on the clutch.”

Old Qi was there tonight too. He sat in the cab, in the seat beside the driver’s, the door open, one foot on the running board, listening to the far end of the sea wall. The vats were lifted onto the bed, padded with sacks and roped down. On the last trip Qiusheng came down from the sea wall, and Old Qi gave up the seat, climbed onto the bed himself and sat among the vats.

“Mr Qi—”

“On the truck I ride in the back,” said Old Qi. “I can see behind.”

The truck ran along the sea wall of the salt pans, past the Saltflat stop, up onto the metalled road and round the Old Town roundabout. The door on the edge of the roundabout stood open, and the oil lamp inside was still burning. Ah Yue did not look at it. When the truck reached the east end of the docks it was two hours to daylight.

The Futai Soy Works stood on a piece of ground at the end of the siding: a brick wall, and inside the wall row on row of sauce vats, their mouths covered with bamboo lids. It was still dark. On Sundays the sauce was not turned. No one walked among the vats, and dew lay on the bamboo lids. The main gate was not opened. The truck went in by the side gate. Inside the side gate ran the goods track, two rails, from the foot of the wall to the goods yard on the docks behind the works. The key the bookkeeper had given them opened the iron door. Beside the door was a wooden sign, nailed up less than a week, the ink still black: PICKLING · NO ADMITTANCE. The bookkeeper’s hand, thin, no stroke joined to the next.

Fourteen steps. The vats went down backwards, two men bearing the weight in front and two hauling back behind, Ah Yue in the middle calling the work chant, one step down to every call. Ah Shui was the smallest and went in front.

At a press of the switch at the head of the steps, two of the three lamps came on. Ah Yue put a new bulb in the third the next day. A water pipe ran along the wall. When the tap was turned the water was cold and smelled of chlorine, the same as on the sixth. It would do for washing vats. The water for filling them would be brought in separately, from the spring as before, eight barrels a trip on Ah Yue’s truck. From the square vent in the ceiling an iron pipe ran all the way to the foot of the wall of the sauce-vat yard; along the top of the wall there was also a row of vent windows, which Qiusheng had not seen last time. Outside them was the sauce-vat yard, and the wind came in that way, carrying the smell of sauce. The floor was brick, and dry. Qiusheng put out a hand and felt the wall. The wall was dry. He felt a second wall. Dry.

He took the chalk from his pocket and on the wall by the stairs wrote the first line: 12 October, eighteen degrees. He stepped back half a pace, then went up to it again and traced the writing over.

The vats were set in a row, the pot at the end by the stairs. The six sauce vats the old owner had left had stood three years with water rotting in their bottoms. Qiusheng smelled them one by one and said rice could not go into them. All six were pushed into the corner and turned upside down. Ah Yue asked for the tear-off calendar out of Qiusheng’s pocket and nailed it to the wall beside the pot. The nail went into a seam between the bricks, one blow. The calendar was open at the page for 12 October.

Before daylight Qiusheng made a trip up to South Slope. Two tin boxes on the top shelf of the wardrobe. The one with no lettering was already empty. He put the lid on it and put it back.

He came back carrying the other, the one with Assay printed on it. The money from both boxes had gone to Counsel Liang the day before and into the Nanji’s books. The receipt was written out, three thousand dollars exactly, received by Liang Henian—Counsel Liang said an investment had to be written as a round sum; he would advance the difference himself and take it back out of the six hundred each week. The receipt was in Qiusheng’s shirt pocket. In the box he had brought down there remained the stack of copies of his reports, twenty years, yellowed paper, and under them a few old forms that had never been used. He put it on the shelf above the workbench and pressed the lid down tight. There was not a dollar left in the wardrobe. He thought that from now on his home would be clean.

---

The day at Futai began before light.

At four the men in the sauce-vat yard lifted the bamboo lids and turned the sauce, the bamboo rakes scraping the sides of the vats, stroke after stroke. At seven the big stove was lit to boil the beans. At midday the big stove burned its hardest, steam rolled in through the vent windows and no sound from under the floor could be heard, and that was the hour Ah Yue’s boys used for shifting bottles, scrubbing vats and stacking empty crates. The foreman did not come down. He chalked the day’s account on the door of the works building, so many vats, so many loads, so many catties sold, and in the evening the bookkeeper came and copied it, and wiped it off when it was copied. The men knocked off at six. They went out by the side gate and the main gate was locked. Only after six was the fire lit in the cellar.

Once the fire was lit there was a layer of spirit under the smell of sauce. It went out through the vent windows and mixed with the smell of the sauce-vat yard. Qiusheng had stood under the vent windows and smelled. The sauce was on top, the spirit underneath. With a wall between, it could not be smelled.

On the third day Ah Yue hauled three more vats from the Old Town. Six vats in a row, two pots, five hundred bottles a week.

The ice truck came on the first Thursday.

Painted on its side was Wen Cold Storage · Fresh Fish Direct. It backed in through the side gate, its tail to the iron door at the head of the stairs. In the bed was a layer of sawdust, and packed in the sawdust, crushed ice, and only under the ice the empty crates. The driver did not get down. He spoke through the window.

“Madam Wen’s orders: every bottle is assayed.”

“They’ve been assayed,” said Ah Yue.

“Madam Wen’s orders.”

Qiusheng stood on the stairs inside the iron door, opened the notebook and read out the figures for the batch. The driver heard him out, nodded once and said to load up. The crates were lifted onto the bed and buried in the sawdust and ice. The ice melted fast. Water dripped from the tail onto the flagstones in a line; the truck drove out by the side gate and the line of water went out with it, dripping all the way to the docks.

Old Qi stood by the side gate and looked at the line of water.

“Rain is best,” he said. “It won’t show.”

---

Supper on South Slope was at seven. This week Qiusheng came home for it every day.

After supper the table was wiped. Su Qi sat under the lamp mending a winter uniform from the girls’ school, not Xiaoman’s, the cuff unpicked, three pins held in her mouth. Xiaoman had her English textbook open on the table and was reading a lesson. Qiusheng sat opposite her, the newspaper on his knees unturned.

“*The teacher is in the classroom*,” Xiaoman read.

“Read it again.”

“*The teacher*—”

“Not ‘de’,” said Qiusheng. “The tip of the tongue between the upper and lower teeth. The breath comes out through the gap.”

Xiaoman read it three times. At the third Su Qi’s needle stopped a moment, and went through again.

“The clerk says the same,” said Xiaoman. “He says my *th* is a South Slope *th*.”

“He’s right.”

Qiusheng took the pencil and in the margin beside that line wrote *teacher*, and under it the word in Chinese, two characters. When he had written it he put the pencil back by her hand.

“Papa, is this the lesson you taught at the night school too?”

“More or less.”

“The one who was better than me at algebra. Was his *th* right?”

“He didn’t say *th*,” said Qiusheng. “He did sums.”

Xiaoman turned the page of the textbook and turned it back, her finger pressed on the line with *teacher*.

“Papa, will the deposit really be there?”

Su Qi’s needle did not stop.

“It will be there.”

“The clerk says the two at the branch office who came out of the normal school, their families both borrowed it, and they’re still paying it back.”

“It will be there,” said Qiusheng. “Get on with your lesson.”

Xiaoman went on with it until half past nine. Su Qi finished sewing the cuff of the uniform, bit off the thread, looked at it, and unpicked the other cuff. Xiaoman put away her textbook and went into her room hugging the dictionary. The door closed. The line of light under it stayed a while and went out.

Su Qi was still under the lamp. Qiusheng went into the bedroom and opened the wardrobe. At the very back hung Xiaoman’s winter uniform from last year, dark blue, the sleeves a length too short. Su Qi had said she would let them down when the weather cooled, and had not touched it. He took the receipt from his shirt pocket, three thousand dollars exactly, folded it in four, then in eight, folded it very small and ran a fingernail over the folds. In the lining of the cuff there was a seam where the thread had worked loose. He pushed the little square in through the seam, pushed it right down, and felt for it with his fingers through the cloth. It could not be felt. He hung the uniform at the very back again and put Xiaoman’s summer skirts in front of it.

When he came out Su Qi was standing by the rattan chair with his coat in her hands. She had not hung it up. She held it by the collar, her nose close to the shoulder.

“Soy sauce.”

Qiusheng stood still.

Su Qi did not ask. She laid the coat over her arm and went into the kitchen. The tap began to run.

---

Old Hai came in from the track end.

It was just after six that day. The workers had just gone and the fire in the cellar was not yet lit. Ah Yue brought him in by the iron door at the goods-yard end. The iron door was where the goods track went out, and was ordinarily kept locked with a chain. Once in the cellar Old Hai would not sit. He stood at the foot of the stairs, bald, in his singlet, the cigarette tin held in the fingers of one hand and in the other a copy of the Fog Harbour Daily, rolled up. Qiusheng stood by the workbench with the notebook open. Old Qi sat in the corner by the wall, on an upturned vat, his tea bowl on the floor at his left hand. The bowl had come with him from the Nanji. Ah Yue stood on the third step of the stairs with one foot on the step above.

With his one eye Old Hai looked the cellar over from end to end: the vats, the pots, the shelves, the chalk on the wall. When he had looked he walked to the workbench and slapped the newspaper down on it.

The newspaper lay open at page two. He could not read, but his finger came down in the right place, on the headline of the column: ELEVEN MORE POISONED ON THE DOCKS THIS MONTH: BAD LIQUOR BLAMED.

“Eleven,” said Old Hai. “The den behind the union has come down from three cents to two. Teacher, there isn’t enough of your spirit.”

Qiusheng looked at the column.

“Six vats, five hundred a week,” he said. “Three hundred to Madam Wen, two hundred to the union. Any more has to wait for the vats.”

“Wait for the vats,” said Old Hai. He picked the newspaper up and rolled it again, very tight. “There are more than a thousand mouths on the docks. Two hundred bottles. That’s less than a mouthful a man a week. The nine hundred mouths left over still turn down the alley behind the union when they knock off. Two cents a cup. They drink it. Some go blind. Some are gone.”

“A vat takes twelve days.”

“Twelve days.” Old Hai nodded. He struck the rolled newspaper once on his palm. “Teacher, the docks can wait. The dead can’t.”

He left the newspaper on the bench, put the cigarette tin in his trouser pocket and turned towards the track end. At the iron door he looked back once at the lines of chalk on the wall, and went out. Ah Yue went out after him to lock the chain.

Two men were left in the cellar. Old Qi lifted the tea bowl from the floor, drank a mouthful and put it back at his left hand.

“It’s the same with the thousand bottles,” he said.

Qiusheng did not take it up. He folded the newspaper and put it under the notebook. With the chalk in his hand he went to the wall and started a new line under the row of temperatures. First he wrote the character for Wen, and under it, a thousand; then “union”, and under it, two hundred. When he had written them he looked at the two figures. Under the two hundred he added one more word: Owed.

Ah Yue came back and saw the word. He opened the stove door and lit the fire.

The Lu crew lived at the far east end of the goods yard.

It was where the railway ended, an open space between two rows of warehouses, and in the open space a big iron pot set on three stones over a fire of cinders. There were forty or fifty porters who had fled from the north, a gang to themselves. The docks called them the Lu crew, and the goods at the east end of the yard were theirs to carry. That evening Old Hai took four crates of White Dew over. He said the Lu crew did not drink the union’s liquor and thought southern liquor was like water; but the Lu crew and the union shared one railway, with goods at both ends of it, and had to keep calling on each other. Ah Yue drove him, with Qiusheng in the seat beside the driver’s. Old Qi got down at the gate of the goods yard, leaned against the gatepost and went no further in.

When the truck arrived a goods train was being unloaded. The Lu crew stood in a line, and the sacks came down from the wagon door one at a time, hand to hand, each onto the shoulder of the last man, who carried it off. The work chant was not the one from this end of the docks: one man led, drawing it out long, and when he had drawn it to the end all of them answered together, one sound, short, heavy, and the sack came down on that sound. When the last wagon was empty the line broke up and the men walked over to the iron pot.

In the pot a porridge of mixed grains was boiling, thick, a bowl a man. On the railway side sat the Lu crew, on sleepers and on sacks, row behind row; on the warehouse side were the few union men Old Hai had brought, sitting on crates. The fire was between them. Old Hai sat down on the union side and put his cigarette tin on his knee. Ah Yue carried the four crates of liquor to the fire and set them at Old Hai’s feet, then squatted down inside the circle himself, close to the Lu crew’s side. Qiusheng stood outside the circle, behind Old Hai, both hands in his coat pockets. He did not sit.

Old Lu was the last to come over. Fifty-odd, a head shorter than the rest and a good deal broader in the shoulder, stripped to the waist, a towel round his neck. He ladled a bowl of porridge from the pot and held it, not eating yet, and looked across the fire at the crates.

“Old Hai. What have you brought us today?”

“Teacher’s liquor,” said Old Hai. “On the docks they call it White Dew.”

“White Dew.” Old Lu put the porridge down by his foot. Ah Yue drew a bottle from a crate and passed it across the fire. Old Lu pulled the cork and held out a hand towards the sleepers, and someone passed him an empty earthenware bowl. He poured it full, carried it round the fire to the crate side and, standing, took a mouthful.

No one on the Lu crew’s side made a sound. A cinder cracked in the fire.

Old Lu frowned.

“Good stuff. Just too dainty. The stills back home don’t use vats.”

“What do they use, if not vats?” Ah Yue asked.

“Not your sort of vat,” said Old Lu. He bent down and tapped a finger on the earth. “The ground.”

“How does it ferment in the ground?”

“Why wouldn’t it?” said Old Lu. “Every crop there is grows in the ground.”

He called across the fire to the sleepers and another earthenware bowl was passed over. He poured a bowl, this time for Old Hai. Old Hai raised the bowl, looked at it against the firelight with his one eye, and drank.

“Which one’s the Teacher?” Old Lu asked.

Old Hai did not look up. He tipped his chin back over his shoulder. Outside the circle Qiusheng nodded once.

Old Lu turned round, bowl in hand. His eyes rested on Qiusheng’s coat, then dropped to the two hands in its pockets.

“Teacher not sitting?”

“I’m all right standing.”

“Teacher’s a liquor maker?”

“An assayer.”

Old Lu laughed, showing a mouthful of yellow teeth. “An assayer making liquor,” he said. “No wonder it’s dainty. Back home the liquor makers have hands that are all callus, every man of them.”

“An assayer’s hands have it too,” said Qiusheng.

Old Lu looked at his hands. Qiusheng’s hands were still in his pockets.

“It’s a sight too hot down here in your south,” said Old Lu. “Leave grain soaking in a vat and of course it sours.”

The second bowl Old Lu drank slowly. He went back round to the other side of the fire and sat down on a sleeper. On the Lu crew’s side some of them began to talk in the speech of home. Qiusheng could not follow it. He could only tell that they were saying the names of places, one after another. The men on the union side watched them, said nothing, and got on with their own drinking. Ah Yue squatted in the middle and listened to both sides.

The third bowl Old Lu filled and did not drink. He held the bowl up over the fire, looked once at the firelight in it, and began to sing.

“A field of sorghum gone red in the frost,”

The men on the sleepers stopped talking.

“light the still and it’s sweet for ten li round.”

Someone beat on a bowl with his chopsticks.

“Under the snow our crocks lie buried:”

He stopped for a beat. The whole row came in together, short, heavy, the same as the sound at the unloading:

“dig them up come spring, first cup to Father and Mother.”

When it was sung Old Lu tipped the bowl of liquor into the fire. The fire leapt up as high as a man, blue, and at once sank back. The men on the union side drew back. Old Hai did not move.

“A waste,” said Ah Yue.

“An offering,” said Old Lu. He set the empty bowl down by his foot and looked across the fire at Qiusheng. “Teacher, tomorrow I’ll carry something over to you.”

“What?”

“You’ll know when I’ve carried it.”

On the way back Old Hai rode in the bed and Qiusheng in the cab. Old Qi got on at the gate of the goods yard. Before he climbed onto the bed he looked in at the cab door, at Ah Yue’s feet.

“Lighter.”

Ah Yue put his foot on the clutch. The truck went over the level crossing. The barrier was up.

“Mr Qi,” Ah Yue said over his shoulder, “that Old Lu of the Lu crew. What did he do up north, before?”

“Worked the land.” Old Qi’s voice came from the bed of the truck. “Grew sorghum.”

---

The next day he carried in two sacks of sorghum and a lump of daqu the size of a brick. The first two pits soured; the third came off the still and the whole cellar smelled of grain.

Eighteen days lay in between.

Old Lu came in by the iron door at the goods-yard end with the two sacks of sorghum, one on each shoulder, the brick of daqu starter clamped under his arm, wrapped in a piece of oiled paper. Old Qi gave him a look from the corner and did not get up. Ah Yue had already said this man was with him. Qiusheng stood up in the middle of the row of vats, the thermometer still in his hand, not yet wiped. Old Lu dropped the sacks on the floor, straightened up, looked up at the vent windows, then down at the bricks under his feet.

“Not half bad, this place,” he said.

He untied a sack, took up a handful of sorghum and opened his palm to show Qiusheng. Red, round, hard.

“You don’t soak sorghum. Steam it through, mix in the daqu, bury it in a pit and shut it in, and when you distil you load the steamer a layer at a time. Not like you southerners, with it sitting in water.”

“A pit?”

“A vat sunk in the ground, the mouth level with the floor.” Old Lu stamped his foot in one corner of the cellar. Under the brick was earth. “It’ll do here.”

“How long?”

“Depends on the weather,” said Old Lu. “Back home we bury it when the snow comes and dig it out come spring. You get no snow here. Shut it in a few days less.”

“How many days?”

“Teacher, you don’t count a crop in days,” said Old Lu. “When it’s ready you’ll smell it.”

Qiusheng opened the notebook and wrote down the date. Old Lu saw the notebook and laughed.

“Making liquor with a ledger,” he said. “Never saw that back home.”

“The sour vat. It was some use there.”

“Sour is sour,” said Old Lu. “It’s the heat.”

In one corner of the cellar a few bricks were prised up. Underneath was yellow earth. Ah Yue and two of the boys dug for an afternoon and dug three holes, and in the holes sat three vats, their mouths level with the floor. The sorghum was steamed through in the copper pot and spread on the brick floor to cool. Old Lu ground the daqu on a brick, ground it to powder, scattered it, turned the grain, scattered again. He pushed his hand into the sorghum, drew it out and said it would do, they could fill. The three vats were filled the same night, a cloth stretched over each and a brick laid on the cloth. Old Lu patted the three bricks one after another, very lightly, his palm resting on each brick a moment before he took it away.

“That’s it,” he said. “Don’t uncover them.”

Every morning when Qiusheng went down to the cellar the first thing he did was walk to that corner, squat, lift one corner of the cloth and put his nose to the mouth of the vat. The first day it was the smell of raw grain. The third day there was a little sourness, a cool one. The fifth day there was sweetness under the sour. He wrote down in the notebook what he smelled each day, then laid his palm on the earth at the mouth of the vat. The earth was warm, a few degrees above the brick floor. Ah Yue said he looked as if he were feeling a man’s forehead.

On the morning of the twelfth day the sourness of the first pit had changed. Not the cool sour. The vinegar kind, coming straight up from under the cloth. He lifted the cloth. The froth was a blackish yellow and had climbed to the mouth. He put his hand in and took a handful, opened it and looked, then smelled it. He took a shovel and shovelled the mash out, a shovelful at a time, into a sack, and tied the neck of the sack tight. Ah Yue stood by and watched, from the first shovelful to the last, and said nothing. When it was done Ah Yue picked up the sack, wheeled it out along the track and tipped it into the sea at the end of the goods yard.

On the fifteenth day the second went sour too. This time Ah Yue took the other shovel.

The third pit Old Lu would not let Qiusheng uncover. “This one’s furthest in, a long way from the big stove. It’s cool,” Old Lu said when he came to look. “It’s slow. A slow one you don’t hurry.” Qiusheng lifted only a corner, smelled once, covered it again and set the brick straight.

Three lines were added to the chalk on the wall. Two of them ended with the word “sour”. The end of the third was left blank.

---

Every Wednesday Qiusheng went to the Nanji.

The money went in a pastry box, six hundred dollars, and was handed to Counsel Liang. In the books it was written as two hundred pots of tea and three hundred steamers of dim sum. When their business was done Counsel Liang saw him to the head of the stairs, not to the door. Qiusheng left by the back door. The back door gave on a small alley, the alley ran through to the roundabout, and it was nearer than the front.

One Wednesday late in October the accounts took a long time, and when he came downstairs the lamps were already lit in the main room. He pushed open the back door. Someone was sitting on the stone step.

It was the singer. She had changed out of the qipao she wore on the stage into a cotton blouse, her hair was down, and a cloth bundle lay on her knees. There was no lamp in the alley, only a strip of light leaking through the crack of the back door. She was singing, very low, not the kind of singing meant for anyone to hear: “Fog comes in on the harbour, white on white, a lamp at the landing waits for my love tonight.” There she stopped and did not go on.

She looked round, saw him and stood up.

“Teacher He.”

Qiusheng stood still.

“Ah Yue has talked about you.” She spoke with a little halt, resting once in the middle of a sentence. “Every day. How Teacher distils, how Teacher reads the temperature, how Teacher says the first cupful—” She stopped. “He says Teacher taught him English at the night school, and arithmetic.”

“Mm.”

“I’m waiting for him to finish work,” she said. “He said it’s the Saltflat run today and he’ll be late.”

At the mouth of the alley a car went round the roundabout. Its lights swept the alley, and it was dark again. She sat back down on the step with the bundle held in her arms and looked up at him.

“Teacher He, I’ve been singing five years,” she said. “Five years, and every word of every song I learned by ear. Teacher He, could you teach me to read?”

Qiusheng looked at her. In the light from the crack of the door there was only half of her face.

“After closing,” he said.

---

He taught her twice.

The first time was in the private room. Downstairs the waiter had turned the chairs up onto the tables and switched off the wireless, and the only lamp left in the main room was the one on the counter. In the private room, one table, one lamp. Ah Lian sat at the table with both hands on her knees. Ah Yue sat on the chair by the door holding a Fog Harbour Daily up in front of his face. The paper was yesterday’s. He read it from the front page to the end and turned back to the front page. Qiusheng sat opposite Ah Lian.

He had brought the writing paper himself. Old Assay Office forms, blank, a few sheets drawn from the bottom of the tin box. The front was printed with ruled boxes. He turned a sheet over. The back was white.

“Your name first.”

“Lian,” she said. “Lotus. The Lian in Ah Lian.”

He wrote 蓮, the character for Lian, large, in the very middle of the sheet, each stroke separate, a pause after every stroke.

“On top is the grass radical,” he said. “Underneath is the character for joining. The grass radical. Everything that grows in water has it.”

She picked up the pencil. She gripped it too tight and the first stroke went crooked. She put the pencil down, shook out her hand and picked it up again. The grass radical came out as two small crosses, and the part underneath leaned off to the right. When she had written it she looked at the character, and then at the one he had written.

“It doesn’t look like yours.”

“It does,” said Qiusheng. “Write it again.”

She wrote a line of them. By the fifth the grass radical stood up.

“This is my name,” she said. She picked up the sheet and held it to the lamp and looked at it for a while, then folded it, folded it in four, and put it in the innermost layer of the cloth bundle.

By the door the newspaper turned a page.

The second time, as soon as she came in she put the bundle down, took out the sheet and smoothed it flat, and wrote the character for Lian once for him to see. The grass radical was straight.

“What shall we write today?”

“White Dew,” she said. “Two characters. I sing them every day and I can’t write them.”

Qiusheng picked up the pencil. The point hung over the paper a moment before it came down. The character for white, 白, was easy, five strokes. The one for dew, 露, he wrote slowly: rain on top, road underneath. When he had written them he turned the sheet round to her.

She wrote. White came right the first time, square and upright. Dew she wrote three times. The rain radical was too big and the road underneath was squeezed into a lump, and when the third one came out the two characters stood one high and one low, each leaning its own way.

She looked at them and laughed.

“Like two birds,” she said. “A big one and a little one.”

By the door the newspaper did not turn. Ah Yue’s face was behind the newspaper. A pair of ears showed, red.

“At home in East Ridge,” said Ah Lian, and the pencil turned half a circle in her hand, “we sing picking tea on the hill too. A verse for every row you pick, and by the time you’ve sung to the top the basket is full. My mother sang and I sang after her, and there wasn’t one word of it I could read.”

“Where is East Ridge?”

“In the hills to the east,” she said. “You take the bus to the foot of the hills and then walk half a day.”

She folded the sheet with White Dew on it too and put it in the bundle, together with the one with her name.

“Teacher He, will you teach me again next week?”

“Mm.”

That night when he got back to Futai the fire in the cellar was still burning, and Ah Chang was minding the stove door. He wrote the night’s temperature on the wall, and in the notebook he wrote: *29 October, nineteenth vat, rice wine, ninety-two.* He turned back a few pages. From September to October, in every line of the notebook that recorded a vat, the same two words stood before the reading. He closed the notebook and put it under the thermometer case.

---

The third pit was opened on 2 November.

It was a Sunday. There was no one in the sauce-vat yard, the big stove was cold, and the cellar smelled only of sauce and earth. Old Lu came straight from work and brought a steamer, a wooden one, three tiers, which he carried down the stairs himself. Ah Yue and the five boys were all there. Old Qi sat on the upturned vat in the corner. Qiusheng squatted by the third pit, the cloth drawn back, the brick set to one side.

Old Lu pushed his hand into the vat, to the wrist, to the elbow, and drew it out. The mash on his hand was dark brown, loose, not sticky. He brought the hand up under his nose, shut his eyes, and opened them.

“It’ll do.”

The mash was loaded into the steamer a layer at a time, Old Lu spreading, Qiusheng watching the thermometer. The steamer sat on the copper pot. In the pot was water. The fire came from below and the steam went up through the mash. Old Lu said that was all there was to the stills back home: the fire did not touch the grain, the steam did.

First there was vapour in the cellar, white, coming from round the edge of the steamer lid. Then there was a smell.

Not the smell of rice. Grain, roasted, coming in wave after wave from under the steamer lid, first to the pot, then to the stairs, then to the innermost corner. Old Lu had stood up. At the stairs Ah Shui sniffed. The tea bowl Old Qi was lifting stopped halfway.

The mouth of the pipe dripped.

One drop.

Two drops.

They joined into a thread.

Qiusheng watched the thermometer and said one word. Ah Yue put the jar in place.

The whole cellar smelled of grain.

When the heart had filled a little under half the jar Qiusheng caught a small glassful and held it out. The glass came into Old Lu’s hand. He did not look at it. He did not smell it.

Old Lu drank it and his eyes went red; for a long time he said nothing. He turned his head towards the fire in the stove and squatted down. In the cellar the fire could be heard, and the spirit falling into the jar. He put the empty glass on his knee and covered it with his palm.

“Back home, the best crocks from the still are buried in the ground. A winter underground, and then it’s liquor.”

“How long underground?” Qiusheng asked.

“Until you’ve forgotten it,” said Old Lu. “That’s when it’s liquor.”

Ah Yue asked where to bury them. Old Lu stood up and walked to the very back of the cellar, to the foot of the last wall, and stamped his foot. “Here. Furthest from the fire.” Three of the boys took picks, prised up the bricks and dug a hole. When the hole was dug the heart was divided among three crocks, the mouths sealed with cloth, the cloth sealed with clay, a brick laid on the clay. The three crocks were set down in the hole, the earth was filled back and trodden flat. Last of all Old Lu squatted and patted the three bricks one after another.

“I’ll come on Sundays and look,” he said. “One look and I’m gone.”

Every Sunday after that he came down to the cellar straight from work, squatted a quarter of an hour, patted the bricks, drank nothing, said little, went up the stairs and was gone.

That night after Old Lu had gone Ah Yue squatted at the edge of the newly filled earth, pressed his palm on the fresh earth, and looked up.

“Teacher, now when people on the docks see my truck, they wave to me.”

When he had said it he was the first to laugh out loud. Qiusheng laughed too.

Ah Yue stood up, went to the wall and looked at the tear-off calendar, turned it to today and read the tides printed at the bottom.

“Low tide half past eleven,” he said. “The fifty bottles for Saltflat go over the flats tonight. Saves half an hour. The union’s seventy for tomorrow morning go on the truck with them. We don’t unload when we get back. At daylight I drive to the fish-market gate and Old Hai’s men come and wheel them off. One trip.”

Old Qi spoke from the corner. “Wait for me.”

“The tide won’t wait,” said Ah Yue. “Mr Qi, I’ve been over the flats a hundred times.”

Old Qi glanced at the door and said no more. Ah Yue called Ah Chang, Ah Shui and two others, and the five of them crated a hundred and twenty bottles and wheeled them out by the track end onto the truck. The headlamps showed once outside the iron door and were gone.

Old Qi finished his bowl of tea, went to the wall, looked at the tides on the tear-off calendar, and went back and sat down.

Qiusheng banked the fire and covered the jar. The smell of grain was still there. He sat down at the workbench, opened the notebook and wrote: *2 November, sorghum, third pit.* He wrote no figure after it. He thought a moment and added a line of small writing beside it: *three crocks, buried.*

---

A little after one, headlamps shone in through the iron door at the track end and fell on the cellar stairs. Old Qi was the first on his feet. Qiusheng followed him up.

The truck had stopped beside the track outside the iron door. Ah Chang was driving. Ah Yue jumped down from the other seat and staggered a step as he landed. In the bed two of the boys were helping Ah Shui down. Ah Shui’s right arm hung from his neck in a strip of cloth torn from somebody’s singlet. The elbow bent inwards. It was wrong. Once he was down his left hand went for one of the empty crates stacked by the iron door.

“Sit down,” said Qiusheng.

Ah Shui drew his hand back, kept his head down, and squatted at the foot of the wall.

The bed was empty. A hundred and twenty bottles. Not even the crates were left.

Ah Yue came and stood in front of Qiusheng. He wiped his hands on his trousers, and wiped them again. His mouth opened first, and no words came.

“The gap on the flats. Two cars. Black. Waiting there.”

“How many men?” Old Qi asked.

“Six or seven. Iron bars. No guns,” said Ah Yue. “Ah Shui got in front of the door. One blow.”

“Did they chase you?” Old Qi asked.

“Didn’t chase,” said Ah Yue. “Loaded up and went. Took the sea wall.”

“They had no need to chase,” said Old Qi. “The tide table was enough.”

Ah Yue looked at him. Old Qi was not looking at Ah Yue. He was looking at the front of the truck.

“They set it at the bonesetter’s in the Old Town,” Ah Chang said from over by the truck. “The bone’s back together. A month in a sling.”

Qiusheng went over to Ah Shui and squatted down. Ah Shui did not look up. His left hand was pressed on his knee, and there was mud on the back of it.

“Does it hurt?”

“Not much,” said Ah Shui, very low. He thought a moment and said, “Teacher, I can still shift bottles with my left hand.”

“No hurry.”

Qiusheng stood up. Old Qi was still standing by the front of the truck. He was looking at the windscreen.

A little flag was stuck on the glass, the size of a palm, its bamboo stick pushed under the wiper. A white skull on black.

“The Black Flag.” Old Qi looked at the flag. “Their firewater won’t sell any more.”

Ah Yue walked over and looked at the flag. His hand came up, and went down again. Ah Chang shut the cab door. Ah Shui squatted at the foot of the wall, his left hand on his knee. Qiusheng stood in the iron doorway. At the far end of the goods yard a goods train went by, wagon after wagon, and was past.

The truck stood beside the track. The little flag stayed on the windscreen. No one went to pull it out.
