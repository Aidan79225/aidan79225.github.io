*7 September · White Dew*

First, early in August, there was a pot.

Ah Yue bought an old copper pot from a bankrupt sauce works in the Old Town. The shutter boards of the sauce works were nailed shut, and the owner’s wife led him in by the side door. The pot sat upside down on the stove, a coat of green rust on its sides, its bottom smoked black, and near the edge a scar where it had been patched. She asked three dollars. Ah Yue gave two and said the pot was broken. She did not argue. She took the money and went indoors. Ah Yue carried the pot out to Saltflat on the tricycle, and he and Qiusheng lifted it aboard with the deck boards creaking under it. Qiusheng worked on it with sandpaper for two days. By day the hold was hot. He carried the pot into the shade by the hatch and crouched over it, rubbing, and when a sheet of sandpaper wore bald he took another. The copper dust fell into the seams between the boards and shone. When Ah Yue came to look on the second day there was already a bright patch on the side of the pot, bright enough to show him his chin.

“Teacher, a pot’s for putting on the fire, not for looking at yourself in.”

“The rust gets into the spirit.”

“Did you rub the pots at the Office like this too?”

“At the Office it was glass.”

Ah Yue crouched down and watched him rub. The sandpaper went back and forth. The copper dust fell, layer on layer.

“Teacher, that something-anol. How do you get it out?”

“You’ll know when we distil.”

On the morning of the third day the pot would show a man his reflection. Qiusheng turned it over, bottom up, and rapped his knuckles once round the bottom. The sound was even. He turned the pot back and covered it with a dry cloth.

The condenser was copper pipe picked up on the docks. Ah Yue had chosen it from the scrap-iron heap in the goods yard, a straight length. Qiusheng bent it round a wooden post, an inch at a time. Bent too fast, it would flatten. An afternoon of bending gave him three bends. The pipe went out through the porthole, made one loop in the sea and came back in under the frame, its mouth over a collecting jar. At high tide the seawater covered the pipe and it cooled best. The thermometer was his own, old Assay Office issue. The scale had faded and he had gone over it in ink, mark by mark. It stood in a hole drilled through the middle of the pot lid.

The yeast Qiusheng went and bought himself, at an old sauce works in the Old Town. The sauce works was in the street behind the Tin Hau Temple. The front sold soy sauce and fermented black beans. At the back was a yeast house, the bamboo trays racked one above another to the roof, a sweet sourness in the warm air. The shopkeeper was a short, fat old man. He showed Qiusheng in and stood at the door himself, working a fan.

Qiusheng picked the cakes up one by one and smelled them. He smelled a cake of red yeast, put it down and took another. A cake of white he carried to the light at the door to look at, broke off a corner and smelled again. At the fourth tray the shopkeeper spoke.

“That’s not how you choose yeast, sir. Look at the colour. If it’s even, it’s good.”

“An even colour isn’t always clean.”

“I’ve made yeast for thirty years.”

“Mm.” Qiusheng picked up another cake, brought it up under his nose and wafted once with his palm.

The shopkeeper’s fan stopped. “What is it you’re making, sir?”

“Red lees.”

“For red lees, one cake of yeast will do thirty catties of meat,” said the shopkeeper. “You’ve been half an hour choosing, sir. Anyone would think you had a whole pig to do.”

“A lot of people at home.”

In the end he chose three cakes of red yeast and one of white. The shopkeeper wrapped them in straw paper and took the money. The fan started up again, faster than before.

“White yeast is for sweet fermented rice. It doesn’t go with the red.”

“I’ll use them separately.”

The shopkeeper held out the wrapped yeast and, when it was almost in Qiusheng’s hand, drew it back half an inch.

“Sir, make lees in this weather and they’re sour in three days.”

“Mm.”

“I’ve warned you.” The shopkeeper put the yeast in his hand.

The rice was three sacks of broken rice, bought with half a month of Ah Yue’s wages: second-grade native rice, a good many broken grains, two hundred catties, carried out by the back door of the rice merchant on the docks. The hand at the rice merchant’s asked Ah Yue what he wanted broken rice for. To feed pigs, he said. The hand asked where there were pigs on the docks. He said there were some at Saltflat. On the boat Qiusheng untied a sack, took up a handful and smelled it, took up another, stirred his fingers through the grains. No smell of mould. No weevils. He nodded.

Ah Yue stacked the three sacks in the forward hold and clapped the dust off his hands. “Ten dollars, Teacher. Half a month.”

“Mm.”

Water was the hardest. The well water at Saltflat was too salty, and the tap water tasted of chlorine. In the end Qiusheng had Ah Yue ride the tricycle every day to the spring behind South Slope and bring water back, four buckets at a time. The tricycle was borrowed from the trading house, to help a relative move house, he said, and the loan ran to a week. The spring was on the back hill, beside the Earth God shrine. The water came out through a length of bamboo pipe, a thin stream, a quarter of an hour to a bucket. The old women at the spring queued in front, each with a pair of buckets on a pole. Ah Yue queued last with the tricycle, his four buckets stacked in the bed. The old woman in front turned and looked at him, then looked at the buckets.

“Four buckets, young fellow. A wedding in the family?”

“That’s right.”

“Which family?”

“At Saltflat,” said Ah Yue. “My uncle.”

“Saltflat water’s salty. You do right to fetch it,” said the old woman. “A wedding, and you’ve been fetching for a week?”

“A lot of people.”

The old woman asked no more and shifted her carrying pole to the other shoulder. When Ah Yue’s turn came he crouched by the bucket and watched the water rise an inch at a time, and two more people joined the queue behind him. It was two hours there and back. Uphill he pushed. Downhill he scraped his feet along the ground. By the time he reached the mudflats the buckets were only eight-tenths full.

The day the first load came back, Qiusheng took the first bucket, scooped a dipperful, held it in his mouth and spat it out; scooped another and swallowed it.

“This one.”

“Teacher, water’s just water,” Ah Yue complained, back from his third trip up the hill. His singlet could have been wrung out. The four buckets slopped in the bed. They had spilled the whole way back.

“Spirit is seven-tenths water,” said Qiusheng. “Do you want the men on the docks drinking seven-tenths salt water?”

Ah Yue said nothing more. On his fourth trip up the hill he brought back an extra bucket.

---

On 8 August they filled the vat.

Soak the rice, steam it, spread it to cool, mix in the yeast, fill the vat. Qiusheng did every step as if it were an experiment at the Assay Office. He kept a notebook and wrote down the time, the temperature, the weight, the colour, the smell. The rice soaked overnight. It was steamed in the copper pot over a bamboo rack until the grains were cooked through, then tipped out and spread on the scrubbed boards. Ah Yue fanned it with a piece of cardboard, fanned until his sweat dripped onto the rice, and Qiusheng told him to stand further off. He laid the back of his hand on the rice. Only when it no longer burned did they mix in the yeast. The yeast was crumbled and scattered, a layer of rice, a layer of yeast. Ah Yue turned it over once with his hands and Qiusheng tested it once with the back of his. The vat was a coarse earthenware water vat bought at Saltflat, covered with a board, with a brick on the board.

The vat went in the forward hold, with the door left open for air.

That night Ah Yue did not go home. Beside the hatch he spread a piece of canvas his uncle had left on the boat, and lay down on it with his hands under his head. He said he wanted to listen to the vat. Every hour Qiusheng went down, laid his palm on the side of the vat, put the thermometer into the mash, read it, wrote. The third time he went down, Ah Yue was asleep on the canvas, one hand hanging over the edge of the hatch, his singlet pushed up to his chest. Qiusheng turned the paraffin lamp down.

After midnight there was a sound in the vat, a small sound. Early next morning they lifted the board. Froth had come up, a layer of white. Qiusheng put the thermometer in, read a figure and wrote it down. Ah Yue crouched by the vat watching. He watched the bubbles break one by one, and the corners of his mouth turned up.

“Teacher, it’s alive.”

“Mm.”

Ah Yue climbed up through the hatch. He said the trading house called the roll at six and he would come back at midday to look. Qiusheng stayed by the vat, put the board back and set the brick square.

Two more days, and the froth had surged to the rim of the vat. The board had been pushed up a crack and the brick sat crooked to one side. There was a sourness in the hold. Not the sourness of spirit, the sourness of vinegar, and it came up at him the moment he lifted the board. Qiusheng put a finger into the vat, took a little and laid it on the tip of his tongue. It was sour enough to numb the roots of his teeth. He pushed the thermometer down and read a figure. Six degrees higher than yesterday.

He closed the notebook and put both arms round the rim of the vat.

“Teacher?”

“Help me lift.”

The two of them carried the vat to the side. It was very heavy. The boat was half afloat on the tide and the boards sloped a little, and they went a step and stopped, a step and stopped. Qiusheng pushed the porthole open. Ah Yue held the bottom of the vat, and together they brought its mouth to the porthole and tipped it, little by little. The white mash slid out over the rim and fell into the sea, and the water sounded once beneath the hull. When it was poured clean Qiusheng turned the vat over, mouth down, and set it on the boards.

Ah Yue’s hands were still on the bottom of the vat. He had not let go.

“Teacher, that was a sack of rice. More than three dollars.”

“Mm.”

“Three days. Only three days!” He stood up, and crouched down again. “The hand at the trading house asked what I wanted the tricycle for and I said moving house. Ten days of moving house, Teacher. The way he looks at me—”

Qiusheng opened the notebook and on the day’s page wrote: *11 August, sour, poured away.* When he had written it he capped his pen. There was no number in front of the line.

Ah Yue leaned over to look. Each of the pages before it had the date, the temperature, the weight at the top, written close and small. This page had one line.

“Teacher, aren’t you giving it a number?”

“It never became spirit.”

Ah Yue crouched down with his back against the boat’s side. “It goes sour and that’s the rice wasted. Two sacks left. If another vat goes sour—”

“A vat in summer is in more of a hurry than a man,” said Qiusheng. He went to the hatch, stood at the foot of the ladder and looked up. The sky in the square hole was white. Not a breath of wind. “We wait for the wind.”

“Wait for the wind? Teacher, this is August!”

Qiusheng carried the upturned vat back to the forward hold and wiped its rim with a cloth. Ah Yue watched him wipe. He picked up the brick and put it back beside the vat.

---

For those two weeks he was at home by day.

After breakfast Su Qi went to the stall and Xiaoman went to school. He spread the Fog Harbour Daily on the table, turned to the situations vacant and read them line by line. Keeping accounts at a warehouse, dispensing at a chemist’s, supply teaching at a primary school. He marked them in the margin with a pencil, and when he had marked them he scored them out. At midday Su Qi came home to cook, saw him still sitting there, poured a cup of tea and set it beside the newspaper.

“Anything suitable?”

“I’m looking.”

“And the night shift?”

“Still doing it.”

She glanced at the newspaper, at the lines the pencil had marked. “The supply teaching. How much a month?”

“Sixteen dollars.”

“Not enough.” She carried the teapot into the kitchen. In that column of the newspaper, where the pencil had marked, the paper was torn through.

Another afternoon Xiaoman was out of school early. She slung her satchel onto the rattan chair and stood behind him reading the paper.

“Papa, the one at the night school who was better than me at algebra. What happened to him?”

“He’s on the docks.”

“A porter?”

“A delivery man.”

“What a waste,” said Xiaoman, and went into her room.

At night he still went to the boat. The last bus left the roundabout at eleven. He sat in the back row. The cardboard box had given way to a cloth bag, and in it were sacks and a coil of thick rope. He built the partition of the forward hold higher with wooden battens and hung wet sacks on it, one overlapping another, nailed along the top edge. Underneath he laid a bed of ballast sand. The sand came up out of the compartments of the bilge a dipperful at a time. He spread a layer and poured on a dipper of seawater. When the sacks dried he wetted them again. Ah Yue came two nights and helped him scoop sand. The third night he did not come. He said the trading house had cargo to unload. Qiusheng scooped alone. A salt worker on the sea wall asked across the mudflats what he was hammering at in the middle of the night. He said he was mending the boat. Every night he put the thermometer in the sand, then against the bulkhead of the after hold, and wrote the two figures side by side. The first night they were two degrees apart. The fourth night, four. He coughed for a while, his handkerchief pressed to his mouth. His other hand still held the sack.

Ah Yue came on the fourth night and stood in the door of the forward hold.

“Teacher, you’re building a house.”

“Mm.”

“The wind doesn’t come to Saltflat till White Dew. That’s what the old people say.”

“We wait.”

The fish truck at three in the morning. The same driver, and he did not ask. Qiusheng sat among the empty baskets. The ice in them had melted and the water ran along the floor of the truck to the edge of his shoes. He got down at the fish-market gate on the docks. It was still dark. He walked along the railway line and passed the corner of Warehouse No. 7. The pool on the gravel had long since dried and nothing showed. He waited for the first bus under the bus-stop sign at the corner.

End of Heat fell on 23 August. On the night of the 24th Qiusheng was walking on the sea wall when a salt worker over on the pans straightened up and said something, and the wind blew it to pieces. Qiusheng stopped. The wind was off the sea, and cool. It blew on his singlet and his back tightened. He went down off the sea wall and aboard, went first to the forward hold and pressed his palm to the wet sacks. The sacks were moving.

On 26 August they filled the vat a second time. One sack of rice, the yeast as before. The rice was spread on the boards to cool. Wind came down through the air vent, the hems of the sacks stirred, and the steam off the rice drifted away at a slant. Ah Yue had no need to fan. The cardboard lay on his knees. The vat went at the very back of the forward hold, on the sand, with the board over it. Ah Yue set the brick on top and kept his hand on the brick.

“Teacher, what if this one goes sour too?”

“There’s another sack.”

“And if that goes sour?”

“We buy more.”

Ah Yue asked no more.

---

The fermentation took twelve days. He coughed more often, yet he felt more alive than he had in ten years.

Every night he wrote it down: the hour, the temperature, the notch on the side of the vat the froth had reached, the smell at the moment the board came up. Ah Yue watched him write.

“Teacher, why write all this down?”

“Twenty years at the Office I wrote it down like this.”

“And is it any use?”

“The sour vat. It was some use there.” Qiusheng turned the notebook back to the August pages, put his finger on the temperature in the line for the 10th, and turned forward again.

On the fifth night the froth reached its highest, and he drew a line in the notebook.

“Teacher, what will it do tomorrow?”

“Slow down.”

On the sixth day the froth stood lower. Ah Yue stared at the vat, then looked at the notebook.

“How did you know?”

“I didn’t know.” Qiusheng closed the notebook. “I only wrote it down.”

On the tenth day the sound in the vat stopped. The smell had changed. Under the sourness there was something else, cool, that worked its way up the nose. Ah Yue put his head over the mouth of the vat, drew a deep breath, straightened up and blinked several times.

“So this is liquor?”

“Not until it’s distilled.”

Most other nights Ah Yue was not there. The trading house’s autumn cargoes had come in, shipload after shipload of rice and beans. He carried by day and unloaded by night, and when he did come out to the boat he dropped onto the canvas. One night he brought a bowl of fish-ball soup wrapped in a cloth, still warm when it reached the boat. Qiusheng drank it and gave him back the bowl.

“The noodle-stall owner asked me where the gentleman with the cardboard box had got to.”

“What did you say?”

“I said he’d gone.”

Qiusheng nodded, wiped the thermometer dry and put it back in the vat.

---

On the twelfth day, when daylight came, Qiusheng did not go home.

On the bulkhead of the after hold he copied out in chalk the times of high tide for the month, the tide table open on his knees, a line for each day, from 1 September to the 30th. He wrote very small. When he had done he drew a stroke under the line for the 6th: high tide, the 7th, 0.40. Low tide, the 7th, 6.50.

“We can’t start distilling till two hours before high tide,” he said. “Draw the fire before the ebb.”

Ah Yue looked at the row of figures with his head on one side. “The boat tilts and the pot tilts, I see that. But Teacher, a little tilt can’t matter.”

“If the pot tilts, the fire’s off centre. If the fire’s off centre, the temperature isn’t true.”

“Even the sea has to do as you say.” Ah Yue laughed.

“When your uncle reads the tide, what does he look at?”

“The waterline at the bow. When the water’s over the third plank, the boat’s level,” said Ah Yue. “He’s looked at it for forty years.”

“When the boat’s level, what time is it?”

Ah Yue opened his mouth.

“He looks at the boat.” Qiusheng went over the line for the 6th once more. “I look at the clock.”

Qiusheng took the tear-off calendar out of the cloth bag and nailed it to the bulkhead beside the pot. The nail went into the wood, one blow. The calendar was open at the page for 6 September.

Ah Yue went close to look. “It’s got the tides printed on it too.”

Qiusheng gave the nail another blow, stepped back half a pace to look, and straightened the calendar a little.

The paraffin lamp he took up outside the hatch and hung on the iron hook of the hatch cover. Its light came down from above. It reached the ladder and did not reach the pot. By the pot he left only a miner’s safety lamp, with wire gauze over the glass, which he had bought at a junk stall on the docks.

Ah Yue glanced at the charcoal stove beside the pot.

“There’s fire in the stove.”

“The stove has a lid,” said Qiusheng. “You stand over by the jar. Three paces from the pot.”

Towards evening they charged the pot. The fermented mash went in, liquor and all, a dipperful at a time. The rice sank to the bottom and the liquor floated on top. At seven-tenths of the pot they added no more. Qiusheng put his hand in and stirred once round. Nothing had caked on the bottom. He drew his hand out with the liquor clinging between his fingers.

Ah Yue held the empty dipper and watched the liquor rock in the pot. “Soup and all, Teacher?”

“The spirit is in the liquor.”

The lid went on, the thermometer went into its hole, and the rim was packed all round with strips of wet cloth. Ah Yue crouched by the jar and wiped its mouth and wiped it again. Qiusheng brought a bucket of seawater and set it by the stove with a wet sack draped over it. Then he cleared the clutter from the foot of the ladder: a path from the pot to the hatch, nothing to trip on.

---

After dark the tide began to come in.

First there was a sound from under the boat, and after a while another, the timber coming loose from the mud. After ten the hull came level, little by little. Qiusheng took out a pencil and laid it on the boards. The pencil did not roll. He picked it up and put it back in his pocket. Outside the hull the sea slapped, and slapped again, lightly, keeping a beat. The light of the safety lamp fell on the copper pot. A ring of brightness on its side, and in the brightness the figure of a man bent over.

Waiting for the tide. Ah Yue sat on the ladder, swinging his feet.

“My mother asked where I go at night,” said Ah Yue. “I said I was doing a run. It’s not really lying to her.”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He opened the stove door a crack and shut it again.

Outside the porthole it had gone white. The fog had come up. It came in close over the water. The lights on the sandspit opposite went first, then the sea wall of the salt pans. Ah Yue climbed up, turned the paraffin lamp up a notch and came down again.

At ten-forty Qiusheng lit the fire.

The charcoal reddened in the firebox. The hold was soon hot, and sweat dripped from his eyebrows onto the thermometer. Qiusheng crouched by the pot with his eyes on the thermometer and did not move. The mercury climbed slowly. Fifty, fifty-five, sixty. He closed the stove door a little. Sixty-four and a half degrees. The mercury stopped there a moment. His palm was pressed to the right side of his chest. He did not cough. The mercury went on up.

At the other end of the copper pipe Ah Yue crouched by the jar with his ear against the pipe.

“Teacher, there’s a sound in the pipe.”

“Don’t touch the pipe.”

The first drop of spirit fell from the mouth of the pipe, then a second, then a third, joining into a thin thread.

“Teacher, it’s coming!” Ah Yue called from beside the collecting jar.

“Pour it away,” said Qiusheng.

“What?”

“The heads. Pour them away.” Qiusheng did not turn round. “The first cupful, until the thermometer passes seventy-eight degrees. Pour all of it away.”

“That much? But it’s liquor—”

“It’s methanol.” Qiusheng turned to look at him, and the light of the safety lamp flashed on his glasses. “The heads are money too. The Black Flag can’t bear to pour them away. Whether I’m here or not, however short of money you are, that cupful gets poured away. Promise me.”

Ah Yue looked at him. Slowly he moved the jar aside and set a little cup under the mouth of the pipe in its place. A thin layer had already gathered in the bottom of the jar. He tipped the jar and poured that into the cup too. The thread of spirit fell into the cup, and the cup filled. He carried the cupful to the porthole, turned his wrist and poured it into the sea. The water sounded once below. The beat did not falter.

“I promise.”

Qiusheng watched the thermometer. The mercury passed seventy-eight.

“Cut.”

Ah Yue pushed the jar back under the mouth of the pipe.

The thread of spirit was a little thicker than before. Every quarter of an hour Qiusheng read the temperature and wrote it down. Seventy-nine, eighty, eighty-one. The stove door open a crack, shut a crack. Ah Yue had stopped talking. He kept one hand on the mouth of the jar and watched the spirit fall. The hold was hot. Both men’s singlets were soaked through, and neither took his off. After an hour Qiusheng began to cough. He held his handkerchief to his mouth and with the other hand pointed at the stove door. Ah Yue went over, closed the stove door by a hair and came back to the jar.

---

The heart ran for most of the night. It was nearly dawn.

The foghorn on the breakwater had sounded once a minute all night. Now it stopped. In the hold there was only the small noise of the charcoal stove, and the thread of spirit falling into the jar. Qiusheng caught half a small glass and held it up to the porthole. The eastern sky had only just turned pale, and the light came through the fog and into the glass. The spirit was so clear it could hardly be seen. A layer of fine beads clung to the inside of the glass, like dew on blades of grass in the morning.

He did not drink it. He handed the glass to Ah Yue.

Ah Yue wiped his hands on his trousers before he took it, carefully. He smelled it, glanced at Qiusheng again, and then took a small sip.

He closed his eyes. Outside the hull the sea slapped, and slapped again.

“Well?” Qiusheng asked. He found that his voice was shaking a little. In twenty years he had assayed thousands of glasses and given thousands of figures, and no one had ever asked him whether a glass was any good.

Ah Yue opened his eyes and turned the glass half a turn in both hands. He did not put it down.

“Teacher,” he said, “I don’t know how to say it. It’s very clean. When it’s gone down, it’s as if I… can see a long way.”

Qiusheng took back what was left of the half glass and took a small sip himself. His lungs burned for a moment. He coughed twice, and then he smiled. Ah Yue had never seen the Teacher smile.

“Ninety-two,” he said.

“What?”

“The assay reading. By the Assay Office’s standard, ninety and over is first quality.” Qiusheng put the glass down. “Ninety-nine and over, the old hands at the Office called First Dew. I assayed for twenty years and saw it only once.”

“Then ours—”

“Ninety-two. Our first vat.”

“The one time you saw it. Who made it?”

“I don’t know,” said Qiusheng. “It was seized. There was no name on it.”

Ah Yue stood up, went to the bulkhead, reached out and tore yesterday’s page off the calendar. The page beneath had two words printed on it in red.

“White Dew,” he read out, and looked up at Qiusheng. He slapped the torn-off page down on the lid of the pot. “Teacher, today is White Dew. We’ll call our liquor White Dew.”

Qiusheng raised the glass again and looked at it against the porthole for a while.

“Not yet,” he said.

“Not yet what?”

“It’s very clean,” said Qiusheng. “But all it has is the sweetness of the rice. Take a second mouthful and you’ll know. It can’t stand up. No backbone. White Dew…” He thought, and did not find the next word. “White Dew ought to be better than this.”

“Teacher, the men on the docks don’t know about backbone.”

Qiusheng did not take it up.

Ah Yue was already looking for paper and a pen to write a label for the jar.

At ninety-three degrees Qiusheng stopped taking the heart. The tails he caught separately, half a jar, cloudy, with a stale smell. He covered it with a cloth and said to keep it and put it back through the next run. It was full daylight. He shut the stove door tight and put out the charcoal with seawater, and white smoke went up out of the hatch. There was a sound from under the boat, and the boards began to tilt. He sat down by the pot, opened the notebook and wrote on the page: *7 September, first vat, rice wine, ninety-two.* He pressed harder than usual, and the nib scored a mark in the paper. He watched Ah Yue write “White Dew” on the jar, one careful stroke at a time, and did not stop him.

Ah Yue carried the labelled jar over under the porthole, the two words facing the light.

“Teacher, go home and get some sleep.”

“No hurry.”

“You haven’t slept for two nights.”

“Nor have you.”

Ah Yue lay down on the canvas with his hands under his head, and in less than a quarter of an hour he was asleep. Qiusheng carried the vat to the side, rinsed it clean with seawater and set it upside down on the boards.

---

That day they did not sleep long.

The bottles were old soda bottles Ah Yue had collected from a soda stall on the docks, two for a cent. The stallholder asked what he wanted empties for. For soy sauce, he said. The soda trade mark was still stuck on the shoulder of each bottle. Qiusheng picked one up, looked at it and handed it back.

“Scrape it off.”

“Teacher, the Black Flag use this kind of bottle too.”

“So scrape it off. Scrape it clean.”

Ah Yue crouched on the deck and scraped with a piece of broken glass. He scraped until his fingers blistered, and changed hands. When the bottles were scraped he washed them three times and stood them upside down on the deck to dry in the sun. Qiusheng picked them up one by one and looked at the bottoms against the daylight. One had a ring of white inside its neck. He put it aside. Ah Yue filled them through a funnel. Qiusheng stood by watching the level, and when it reached the neck he said “Stop.” New corks, and a ring of wax dripped round each mouth. Ah Yue counted as he filled. He counted to forty-two. A finger’s depth was left in the bottom of the jar, and the funnel leaned crooked in its mouth.

“Teacher, with no instruments, where did ninety-two come from?”

“The tongue.”

“Does a tongue count?”

“Twenty years of assaying.”

Ah Yue wrote White Dew on the paper label of every bottle. On the first few the writing was crooked. After twenty bottles it ran smooth. When he had finished he held the pen in his teeth and stood the bottles under the porthole in two rows, every label facing out.

“Teacher, look.”

Qiusheng looked. The crooked ones in front, the straight ones behind. He packed the bottles into crates one by one, the crooked ones first, at the bottom. Two crates, with sacks over the top.

Towards evening Ah Yue borrowed the small truck from the trading house, to deliver ice for the fish market, he said. Qiusheng sat beside him. The crates were in the bed, and every little way they knocked.

“Old Hai runs the union’s warehouse. Has done for twenty years,” Ah Yue said as he drove. “When a man dies on the docks, he’s the one who gets the funeral money together, knocking on doors, house by house. He lost that eye ten years ago. He drank one glass of firewater and next morning he couldn’t see out of it. When the men on the docks buy liquor, the first thing they ask is whether Old Hai drinks it.”

“Mm.”

The truck followed the railway line by the docks. It passed the Customs Assay Office. Over the white-painted sign at the door a new one had been nailed, black characters on white, Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau, and a strip of the old one showed underneath. Qiusheng did not turn his head.

The Dockworkers’ Union was behind Warehouse No. 3, a red-brick building with an alley at its back. Empty crates were stacked at the end of the alley with fishing nets drying over them, and the smell was fish mixed with diesel. Ah Yue drove in, stopped, switched off, got down and went to stand by the bed of the truck. Qiusheng sat where he was. At the other end of the alley a line of lamplight showed under the shutter boards of the drinking den.

Old Hai came out of the union’s back door. One eye, sixty or so, bald, the neck of his singlet rotted all round, a cigarette tin held in his fingers. He stood on the threshold of the back door and did not come down. Ah Yue lifted the sack, took out a bottle, walked over and handed it to him.

“A dollar fifty,” said Ah Yue.

Old Hai did not haggle. “Where’s it made?”

“At Saltflat—”

“Don’t tell me,” said Old Hai. “I’m not asking.” He pulled the cork. He did not smell it. First he raised the bottle to the lamp over the back door. He turned his head aside and with the one eye left to him looked at the light in the bottle. He looked for a while, lowered the bottle, and raised it again. He took a mouthful.

He said not a word. He pressed the cork back in, put the bottle back in Ah Yue’s hands, stepped down off the threshold, went round the front of the truck and came to the window. He tapped on the glass with the cigarette tin. Qiusheng wound the window down.

“Ah Yue calls you Teacher?”

“From the night school,” said Ah Yue behind him. He slotted the bottle back into the crate and pulled the sack over.

Old Hai took no notice of him. He was looking at Qiusheng.

“I hear you’re from the Assay Office?”

Qiusheng looked at him. At the end of the alley someone was dragging a crate.

Old Hai paused.

“Does an assayer pour away the heads?”

Qiusheng nodded.

“Poured away,” said Ah Yue. “Not a cupful kept back. Into the sea. I poured it.”

Old Hai laughed. The laugh showed two teeth gone, and every line in his face crowded towards the blind eye.

“I’ve seen a dozen or more who made liquor on the docks,” he said. “Not one would pour it away.” He put the cigarette tin in his trouser pocket and from the other pocket pulled a wad of notes, old and soft, held with a rubber band and smelling of fish. He pushed it at Ah Yue without counting it.

“Next week,” he said, “I want a hundred bottles.”

He turned and called through the door for two men to come out and shift the crates. One young, one old. The young one lifted a corner of the sack for a look and the old one smacked the back of his hand. They took a crate each and carried them in at the back door, and the door shut. Ah Yue stood by the truck hugging the wad of notes. He wiped a hand down his trousers, and only then remembered there was money in it.

All the way back to Saltflat Ah Yue talked.

“A hundred bottles, Teacher. A hundred bottles a week. This vat gave forty-two, so that’s two and a half vats a week. One vat, one pot, one sack of rice left.” He drove with one hand and slapped the wheel with the other. “Who on the docks doesn’t know Old Hai? He says he wants a hundred bottles, and by tomorrow the whole of the docks will know.”

“Mm.”

“Teacher, aren’t you in a hurry?”

Qiusheng watched the road ahead. The headlamps showed a length of sea wall. Beyond the wall it was black.

“No hurry.”

---

Back on the boat the tide had not yet come in and the boards were tilted. Ah Yue lit a small handful of charcoal, propped the pot lid over the mouth of the stove and undid the rubber band. Wrapped inside the notes was a handful of silver ten-cent pieces. Two rolled out and slid down the boards to the foot of the boat’s side. He fetched them back. He piled the silver by the stove and laid the notes out one by one on the pot lid. The lid warmed, and each note went soft as it touched and curled at the corners. One-dollar notes and fifty-cent notes, most of them creased, two with a corner missing. The smell of fish spread in the warm air. When they were all laid out he sat on the jar and looked at them.

“Teacher, that eye Old Hai looked at the liquor with—”

“Mm.”

“He looked longer than you do.”

Qiusheng gathered the notes up one by one. Sixty-three dollars in all, sixty in notes and three in silver ten-cent pieces. He counted the notes three times, divided them into two piles and pushed one pile across to Ah Yue. The silver he did not divide. He pushed it, clinking, over beside Ah Yue’s pile.

“Teacher, that’s too much.”

“It’s yours.” Qiusheng put his own pile into the inside pocket of his coat. “The silver is for cigarettes. Smoke them up top.”

Ah Yue picked the pile up and put it down, picked it up and put it down.

“Tomorrow,” said Qiusheng, “we go and buy a bigger pot.”

Ah Yue put the notes and the silver in his trouser pocket and climbed up through the hatch. After a while a match was struck above, and the wind brought the smell of tobacco down through the air vent. Qiusheng put the lid on the charcoal stove, put the pot lid back on the pot and put the notebook away in the cloth bag. The boards tilted a little more. The jar shifted in its ring of rope and did not fall.

---

When he got home it was full daylight. Mrs Chen’s wireless downstairs was giving the news. Xiaoman’s door stood open and she had already gone to school. Su Qi was in the kitchen making rice porridge. She asked whether the night shift had tired him. He said it had not.

Through the door she said, “Two nights.”

“A rush of cargo.”

She did not take it up. The ladle went twice round the pot.

“Your coat smells of fish,” she said. “I’ll take it and wash it.”

“No need.”

She asked no more. The lid of the pot rattled once.

He went into the bedroom and hid the pile of money in a tin box of old papers on the top shelf of the wardrobe. The box was one the Office had thrown out. It had held label paper, and he had brought it home more than ten years ago. At the bottom lay the copies of his reports that he had brought back from the Assay Office, twenty years of them, a stack of yellowed paper that Su Qi had always taken for old newspapers. The money lay on top of the reports. He pressed the lid down tight, pushed the box back onto the top shelf and hung the clothes back to hide it.

On the tin box, faded, one word was printed: Assay.
