*5 April · Qingming*

At Qingming the Dockworkers’ Union set out a long row of offering tables on the ground behind the warehouse. Old Hai had written forty-one names on red paper. “Every one of them went on firewater. Teacher, pour them a clean cup.”

He said it standing at the head of the table, across the first sheet of red paper.

5 April, a Sunday, not yet full light. Behind the union’s red-brick building the heap of empty crates at the end of the alley had been moved, and a patch of gravel showed, one end against the brick wall of Warehouse No. 3, the other end the quay, and at the quay’s edge an iron bollard. The union’s three long tables had been carried out. The Lu crew had brought two, the legs uneven, propped on bricks, and the five were set end to end in one line, from the foot of the wall to two paces short of the bollard. Red paper was laid on the tables. Sheet after sheet, a palm wide, a name to a sheet, in ink, the top end of each held down by a bowl. The sea wind came from the quay, and the tails of forty-one sheets lifted together with a rustle, and the ends under the bowls did not move.

Before each sheet a bowl, in the bowl a pinch of rice, in the rice three sticks of incense. The incense would not stay lit. A row was lit, the wind came and put out half of it, and a boy squatted under the tables with one lit stick, relighting them one by one. Beside each bowl a small cup, the coarse china cups the union used for selling by the cup, forty-one of them, mouths up, empty. Old Hai had set out the cups one by one, and when he had done he pressed his palm on the mouth of each to see that it stood firm.

At the head of the table was another sheet of red paper, bigger than the rest, with three words on it: June, last year.

The people stood back from the tables. On the wall side were the workers, two or three rows of them, padded waistcoats, singlets, and someone had leaned his carrying pole against the brick wall. On the quay side were the women and children, a wad of joss paper in each woman’s hand, the children holding on to the women’s trouser legs. Among the women were a few men, standing straight, their eyes not turned to the tables. One had a child leading him by the hand. Another held the edge of the table himself, his fingers feeling along it inch by inch, and when they came on a bowl they stopped, and the hand drew back half an inch.

Old Hai stood at the head of the table, beside the first sheet of red paper. Bald, padded waistcoat, the cigarette tin gripped in one hand, the tin unopened. Old Lu stood at the very edge, against the brick wall of the warehouse, his towel round his neck, his hands hanging at his sides, and behind him the men of the Lu crew stood in half a row. Ah Shui was by the last table, squatting, his left hand steadying a table leg. The brick under the leg was not level, and the table rocked whenever the wind came. Headman Ah Lu had come up from the ferry landing. He stood at the bollard end, furthest from the tables, his bamboo hat in his hand, his trousers rolled to the knee, one leg straight and no weight on the other. The young man from the paper leaned on the stack of crates, padded gown, glasses, his pad under his arm, unopened.

The wife of Ah Gui of No. 3 Wharf had not come. Old Qi had not come either.

Qiusheng came from the far end of the alley. Coat, shirt, a cloth bag in his hand, two bottles in the bag that knocked together a few times on the way. The truck was parked at the mouth of the alley. Ah Chang had driven, and stayed in the cab. Ah Yue had been gone three days. Qiusheng walked up to the tables and stood. No one spoke. On the women’s side a child asked something and was hushed.

Old Hai looked at him. The one eye rested first on the cloth bag, then on his face. He shifted the cigarette tin to his other hand and spoke those words.

Qiusheng nodded once.

“The young fellow from the paper wrote the names,” said Old Hai. “He copied the ledger once. I said one, he wrote one.” He pointed with the cigarette tin at the big sheet. “The ones from the month of June. The ones from July on are reckoned apart.”

The young man from the paper straightened up from the stack of crates. “Old Hai, shall I call the names?”

“I’ll call them,” said Old Hai.

The young man put the pad back under his arm.

Qiusheng hung the cloth bag in the crook of his arm and drew out a bottle. A soda bottle, the trade mark scraped off, two words on the paper label. He pulled the cork.

“Which vat?” asked Old Hai.

“The last vat. The heart.”

Old Hai nodded once.

“Ah Gui,” he said. “Of No. 3 Wharf. His wife’s gone back to the country, with the two children.”

Qiusheng held the mouth of the bottle over the first small cup and poured. Clear. He poured to a hair below the rim and stopped. He righted the bottle and moved one step along.

“Ah Shun.”

Poured. One step along.

“Ah Fa. Lu crew. Carried for the union after.”

By the wall Old Lu stirred.

Poured.

“Chen Jiu.”

“Ah Gou. That’s his old mother over there, with the child in her arms.”

Poured. On the women’s side the old woman with the child did not move. The child twisted once in her arms.

A name, a cup. Old Hai said one, Qiusheng poured one. Old Hai did not say them fast. Between one name and the next he left a beat, and his eye was not on the red paper, it was on Qiusheng’s hands. The red paper flapped in the wind, and not one name he said differed from the one written on its sheet. The young man from the paper opened his pad and shut it again.

At the twenty-second the first bottle was empty. Qiusheng put the empty bottle back in the cloth bag, drew out the second and pulled the cork. Old Hai waited for him. Another row of incense had gone out, and the boy crawled under the tables relighting it stick by stick.

“Ah Cai’s father.”

Poured.

“Old Cai.”

Poured.

Somewhere past the thirtieth Qiusheng began to cough. The bottle was still in his hand. He lifted its mouth clear of the cup, and with the other hand got out his handkerchief and pressed it to his mouth. He coughed once, twice, and bent double. Old Hai did not steady him. Old Hai stood where he was, the cigarette tin in his hand, and waited. Ah Shui’s left hand came off the table leg and he half rose. Old Hai’s one eye turned that way, and Ah Shui squatted again, his hand back on the table leg. On the workers’ side no one moved. When the coughing was over Qiusheng straightened, pressed his palm once to the right side of his chest and put the handkerchief back in his pocket. He lowered the mouth of the bottle over that cup.

“Old Cai’s younger brother,” said Old Hai. “The same day.”

Poured.

The cups after that he poured a little more slowly, and he did not stop again. When he came to the man holding the edge of the table, the man’s fingers found the cup first and rested against it, and did not lift it. Qiusheng waited for the hand to move away, and then poured. At the forty-first cup a finger’s depth was left in the bottle. Old Hai said the last name, not loudly. Qiusheng finished pouring, righted the bottle and looked at the bottom of it against the daylight.

“The rest,” said Old Hai.

Qiusheng poured that finger’s depth on the gravel, by the table leg. A patch of ground was wet, and in a moment it was gone.

Forty-one cups, each one full to a hair below the rim. The wind came from the quay, the red paper flapped, and the spirit in the cups did not move. Old Hai put the cigarette tin in his trouser pocket, took a step towards the tables, stood a moment facing the whole row, and did not bow. On the workers’ side someone took off his cap. On the women’s side the joss paper was lit, the flames close to the ground, wad after wad, leaning towards the wall when the wind came.

Qiusheng walked back along the tables to the head, the empty bottle in his hand. From his shirt pocket he took a sheaf of paper, four sheets, old forms, the backs covered with writing, small. He held it out to Old Hai.

Old Hai fished the cigarette tin out of his trouser pocket before he took the sheets. He held the paper up before his face, his one eye on the writing, and looked a moment. In his hand one sheet turned over, and turned back.

“What does the first line say?”

“The first cupful: pour it away.”

Old Hai folded the paper in four and put it in his cigarette tin. “I can’t read, but I can remember.”

The lid of the tin snapped shut, one click.

Qiusheng looked at the row of cups. The wind had raised a wrinkle on the spirit in the first cup.

“Tell them: if there is, it will taste the same. If there isn’t, remember the taste.”

Old Hai made no answer. His thumb pressed on the lid of the cigarette tin.

Qiusheng turned the empty bottle over, mouth down, and stood it beside the big sheet of red paper. The other empty bottle he took out of the cloth bag and stood beside it, the two bottoms to the sky. He folded the cloth bag and put it in his coat pocket.

He walked along the tables towards the quay end. He passed Old Lu. Old Lu said nothing, took the towel from his neck and held it in his hand. He passed the men whose eyes were not on the tables, and the one holding the edge heard the footsteps and turned his face, his hand still on the edge of the table. At the last table Ah Shui had his left hand on the table leg and his head down, and did not raise it.

Headman Ah Lu stood by the bollard.

“You too, Teacher…” he said, and stopped.

Qiusheng stopped.

“The ferry brought a parcel,” said the headman. “Mrs He… took it in.”

Qiusheng looked out past the quay. The fog lay close on the water, and the sandspit over by the ferry landing could not be seen.

“The boat,” said the headman. “I’ve set someone to watch it.”

“Mm.”

Qiusheng went on past the bollard and walked along the quay. The road by the quay led to the railway, and at the far end of the railway was fog. He did not look back.

The young man from the paper came up to the head of the table, his pad open, a pencil in his hand.

“Old Hai, that gentleman just now—”

“The one who poured,” said Old Hai.

The young man’s pencil stopped a moment. He shut the pad.

Old Hai held the cigarette tin and did not put it in his trouser pocket. Where the man was going, he had not asked.

The letter reached the branch office on Saturday afternoon. The doorman left it on Qiao Zheng’s desk, weighted down with the wooden rule.

Qiao Zheng was on duty that Sunday. The plaster came up to the elbow and hung across his chest in a cloth sling. The right sleeve of his uniform was empty, the cuff pinned to the shoulder. Seventeen paces along the corridor. Behind the door was a peg. He hung his coat on it with his left hand, and it took two tries before it held.

The envelope was thick, brown paper, the paste on the flap dry, the flap bulging. One line for the addressee: Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau, For the personal attention of Inspector Qiao Zheng. One stamp, the postmark of the South Slope post office, 3 April. The sender’s space was blank. He knew the hand. That pen had drawn a line across his counterfoil fifteen years ago.

He laid the wooden rule along the edge of the flap, pinched the corner of the paper in his left hand and tore along the rule. It did not tear straight.

The paper was torn from an exercise book, ruled in squares. He drew the sheets out of the envelope, knocked them square on the desk and counted them once. Thirty-four. At the top of the first, one word, his name.

He read.

The first dozen sheets or more were the process. Rice wine took up the greater part, from soaking the rice to filling the vat, and sorghum and molasses had a few sheets each. Two lines he read with the wooden rule laid under them: Sixty-four and a half degrees, put it down when it stops. Past seventy-eight, cut. It was written the way the counterfoils were, first what was seen, then what was measured. The last three lines were three figures, seven, two, one; beside the one, two small words had been added: not quite.

Futai, the siding past Warehouse No. 9, the side gate, a wooden sign by the iron door, fourteen stone steps. The cold store, the spiral stair, three layers of smell, a thin tube at the head of the column, and under that thin tube one line was written: this draws off the heads, cut to seventy-eight. Another sheet, a date, 10 April. A name, one he had seen three times on official letters from the provincial capital. Line after line, which ship, how many crates, what label was on them, who they were sold to.

June, forty-one. The alley behind the Nanji. A face.

The window was open a crack. Fog came in at the crack, and in the fog the crane chains sounded once. The two branch office men at the door changed shift, and the new one stamped his feet on the steps, three times. A corner of the paper on the desk lifted.

Then there was himself. The night of 26 March, the slope of South Slope, two men, one club. The writing in this line was the same size as what went before, stroke by stroke, and the pen had not stopped anywhere.

The wind came again and lifted half of that sheet. He raised the right hand in its plaster out of the sling and laid it on the paper. The plaster was cold, and it held down one corner of the thirty-four sheets. He turned the pages with his left hand.

The last sheet had three paragraphs. He read them once, and read them again. Below was the signature, three characters, the same as on the counterfoil.

The doorman’s wireless gave the time at six. He looked up. Outside the window it had gone grey, and the fog was still there. The lamp had not been turned off all night.

He folded the thirty-four sheets back along their creases and pushed them into the envelope. The bottom drawer. He pulled it open: a velvet-lined case, a brown paper envelope, the one that had come in December. He put this one beside the velvet-lined case, pushed the drawer in and locked it. The key went into the pocket of his uniform trousers. The records room was at the other end of the corridor. He did not go.

He drew a telegram form towards him and wrote with his left hand. It was addressed to a government office in the provincial capital, not the Bureau’s head office. Three lines: a name, a date, a place. When he had written it he read it through once, changed nothing, folded it and put it in his uniform pocket. The post office opened at seven on Monday.

From the far end of the corridor the doorman asked why the lamp had been on all night.

“Reading a letter,” he said.

9 April, a Thursday, ten at night.

The Nanji had shut early. The waiter upended the chairs onto the tables one by one. The wireless on the counter was off, and the cloth was over the birdcage. The table by the stairs was empty. There was no bowl on it, and the top was dry. The little stage in the corner was dark.

Qiao Zheng came in by the front door. The waiter looked at his uniform, then at the right hand in its sling, let the bolt drop and took him upstairs. Twelve steps.

The old newspapers on the board partition of the private room had yellowed a shade more. Counsel Liang sat in the host’s seat, his back to the window, a takings sheet spread before him and his fountain pen lying on it. A corner of handkerchief showed at the pocket of his suit, pink. On the table a pot of tea, two cups, no sugar jar. Qiao Zheng sat down opposite, his back to the partition. His coat hung over the back of the chair.

Counsel Liang spoke first.

“Inspector Qiao. That ten cents for the tea, last time. You left it by the pot. I entered it to your account, and I have never taken it.” His fountain pen tapped once on the table. “Today’s pot is my treat.”

From the inside pocket of his uniform Qiao Zheng took a brown paper envelope, a thick one, laid it on the table and pushed it to the middle.

Counsel Liang did not look at it. His eyes were on Qiao Zheng’s face. The fountain pen went back on the takings sheet, and both hands pressed the edges of the sheet and did not move.

“Mr Liang,” said Qiao Zheng, “I need a search warrant issued by the court. The place is the Wen cold store, and I need it before daylight tomorrow. The branch chief will not approve the branch’s own warrant. Even if he approved it, the provincial capital would know the same day.”

“Inspector Qiao, let me say this at the outset: I shall not look in this envelope,” said Counsel Liang. “If I look, the judge will ask me whether I have looked. If I answer that I have, he will ask me whose lawyer I am.”

“I am not asking you to look, Mr Liang.”

“Then what do you want of me, Inspector Qiao?”

“Fog Harbour has three judges,” said Qiao Zheng. “Two were sent by the provincial capital, and what touches the provincial capital they will not sign. The third is a Fog Harbour man. You have been twenty years in Upper Town, Mr Liang, and you know him.”

Counsel Liang’s gold tooth showed a moment and was gone again. He picked up the fountain pen and pulled off the cap.

“The judge is an old schoolmate of mine. His daughter is at the girls’ school, in the same class as Xiaoman,” he said.

Qiao Zheng’s fingers went towards his collar, got halfway, and went back to his knee.

“He is willing to sign?”

“He is willing to read,” said Counsel Liang. “Whether he signs depends on what the paper says. What your paper says, Inspector Qiao, I do not know, so I do not know whether he signs. That’s the going rate.”

From the inside pocket of his suit he took a card, laid it face down on the takings sheet and wrote one line on the back with the fountain pen: Upper Town, on the hill, a house number. The cap went back on. He pushed the card across.

“The bell. Ring twice,” he said. “He will wait until twelve.”

Qiao Zheng picked up the card, read it and put it in his left pocket. In that pocket there was another paper, folded in four. It had been there nearly two months.

“And the provincial capital?” asked Counsel Liang.

“On Monday morning I sent a telegram,” said Qiao Zheng. “Not to the Bureau’s head office. The reply came this afternoon. They move tomorrow morning.”

“And the branch chief?”

“The branch chief leads his own party at four tomorrow. Not to the cold store,” said Qiao Zheng. “The cold store, he has said, has someone in the provincial capital standing behind it. The branch office does not go there.”

Counsel Liang’s fountain pen tapped once on the table, stopped, and did not tap a second time.

“Your right hand, Inspector Qiao.”

“Plaster,” said Qiao Zheng. “I write with the left. So long as the judge can tell it is writing.”

Downstairs the waiter was sweeping, and the broom knocked against the legs of the upended chairs. Counsel Liang lifted the teapot and poured him a cup. His own cup he did not fill.

Qiao Zheng lifted it, drank a mouthful and put it down. The foot of the cup went back square on the ring it had left.

“Mr Liang,” he said, “where is Teacher?”

Counsel Liang laughed. The gold tooth showed in full. He drew the handkerchief from his pocket, spread it on his knee, and did not wipe his forehead.

“Inspector Qiao,” he said, “do you know what the charge is for questioning a lawyer without evidence?”

Qiao Zheng looked at him. There was no sound from beyond the partition, and the broom downstairs had stopped.

He stood up, took his coat from the back of the chair and laid it over the right arm in its sling. The envelope he took back and put in the inside pocket of his uniform. At the door he stopped and did not turn round.

“Tea is ten cents,” he said.

“Today is my treat.”

Qiao Zheng felt in his pocket for ten cents and put it on the ledge of the partition by the door. Down the stairs, twelve steps. The waiter drew the bolt for him. Outside, under the arcade, the fog had already crossed the square.

---

The Upper Town hill he climbed on foot. Along the iron railings of the Western-style houses there was a lamp at every other house. The number matched. He rang the bell twice. An old maidservant opened the door, asked nothing and showed him in. In the study a lamp with a green shade, and behind the desk a man of fifty or so, a woollen waistcoat over his dressing gown, his glasses hanging on the tip of his nose. On the tea table a schoolbook from the girls’ school lay open, a pencil across it.

Qiao Zheng put the envelope on the desk. The man said nothing. He pushed his glasses up, drew out the sheets and began reading from the first.

He read for an hour. Once in that time he got up, took a law code from the bookcase, turned to a page, held it down with a finger, read it, and shut the book. Qiao Zheng sat in the chair opposite, one hand on his knee. A clock ticked in the passage.

When he had finished, the man knocked the thirty-four sheets square, pushed them back and drew a printed sheet towards him. He wrote with a fountain pen. Place: the Wen cold store, the far eastern end of the docks. Grounds: one line. At the applicant’s space he turned the pen round, the barrel towards Qiao Zheng. Qiao Zheng took it in his left hand and signed. The man took the pen back and wrote the date. He got as far as 9 April, the pen stopped a moment, and after it he added one word: night. A signature. The seal came out of a drawer, was pressed on the ink pad and pressed on the paper.

“Inspector Qiao,” he said, “I shall be at home tomorrow morning.”

“Yes.”

The clock in the passage struck. By the time Qiao Zheng went out at the front door he had counted twelve.

---

The same night, Futai.

At six the workers above had knocked off and the big stove was out. The ice truck had come at midday, and the line of water on the flagstones at the side gate was half dry. The fire in the cellar was not lit. It had not been lit since 1 April. The row of vats along the wall, six of them: four had been washed and stood upturned on the brick floor, mouths down; two were left, and Ah Shui squatted by the stone trough in front of one, its mouth towards him, the brush in his left hand reaching in to scrub. Two pots. The big one had been wiped, a band of brightness round its side; the small one, the first one, bought from the sauce works for two dollars, was covered with a cloth. A row of crocks on the shelves, most of them empty, three full, their mouths sealed with clay. By the track, crates were stacked, twenty crates, covered with sacks, packed by the four boys, the two hundred bottles Old Qi wanted before daylight. The row of chalk on the wall ended at 31 March. The date on its own line below was still there. The tear-off calendar by the pot was turned to the page for the ninth.

Qiusheng was at the workbench, the notebook open, not writing. Old Qi was not there. He was at the goods yard.

The chain sounded, wrenched off in one pull. Old Hai came running in by the iron door at the goods-yard end, a film of water on his bald head, a wet patch down the front of his padded waistcoat. He reached the workbench, propped both hands on it and panted. The cigarette tin was in his hand, one corner crushed in.

“Teacher,” he said. “One of the union men cleans at the branch office. He says they’re hitting Futai tomorrow.”

Ah Shui’s brush stopped.

“What time?” asked Qiusheng.

“Before daylight. Four.” Old Hai took a breath. “He carried tea into the branch chief’s room this afternoon, and heard it. Futai first.”

Qiusheng’s eyes came to rest on the date on the wall.

“Tonight,” he said.

“I’ve brought men,” said Old Hai. “Six, waiting at the goods yard.” He straightened and wiped the water off his head with the back of his hand. “That caretaker. South Slope man, his aunt lives on the slope. He says for this one he wants no money.”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He shut the notebook and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

The iron door sounded again, lightly this time. Old Qi came down the stone steps, not fast, a film of fog on the shoulders of his railway uniform. At the bottom he glanced back at the door, and only then spoke.

“One sharp, up,” he said. “Flat wagon on the tail. The Saltflat crossing. Three minutes’ stop.”

“Can it be stopped?” asked Old Hai.

“The men on that train call me Captain Qi,” said Old Qi. He tipped his chin towards the track end. “Headman Ah Lu’s men are at the head of the sea wall. High tide at two.”

“After the head of the sea wall?” asked Qiusheng.

“The village boats,” said Old Qi. “A few trips.”

He walked to the upturned vat in the corner and did not sit. The tea bowl was not on the floor. His eyes moved from the pots to the shelves and came back to Qiusheng.

“What goes?”

Qiusheng stood up. He went to the pots and lifted the cloth off the small one. The copper pot, a patch of brightness on its side, and near the edge of the bottom the scar where it had been patched.

“What came from the Fair Wind goes back to the Fair Wind,” he said.

Old Qi went to the foot of the stairs and took the truck keys down from the nail, the faded length of red string hanging from the iron ring. “The twenty crates I’ll deliver before daylight,” he said. He went up the stairs.

---

Old Hai’s six men came in from the goods-yard end, padded waistcoats, hemp rope, carrying poles. Ah Chang and Ah Cai came down from the iron door, and two more of the boys behind them. All at once the cellar was full of men standing. No one spoke. They waited for Qiusheng to point.

He pointed at the small pot. Four men lifted it, the hemp rope lashed round it, and took it out by the track end. Then he pointed at the three upturned vats by the stairs, the three brought from the Fair Wind, bottoms up. They were turned over one by one, two men to a vat. When it came to the three clay-sealed crocks on the shelves, Ah Chang went to lift them, and Old Hai told him to wait.

It was then that Old Lu came down from the iron door. He had not gone back to the east end of the goods yard after work. He was stripped to the waist, the towel still round his neck, and he stood on the last step and looked the cellar over. Two vats fewer, one pot fewer.

“Old Hai sent for me,” he said.

He went first to the very back. Three bricks lay flat on the earth. He squatted and patted the bricks with his palm, one by one, very lightly. When he had patted them he put his nose to the gap between the bricks, drew one breath and got up.

“Too early yet,” he said.

He went to the shelves, looked the three crocks over and patted the side of each with his palm, one by one. The third he patted twice.

“This one I’ll carry.”

Old Hai’s men took the other two full ones, one each, held against the chest, and went ahead. Old Hai stood at the mouth of the track and watched them pass, his lips moving, counting.

Old Lu bent, got both hands under the crock and heaved, and the crock left the shelf and came to rest against his chest. He made for the track end, the mouth of the crock upward, step by step. At the iron door he looked back.

“Our three crocks.”

“They stay,” said Qiusheng.

Old Lu nodded.

“Sunday I’ll come as always.”

“The door will be locked,” said Qiusheng.

“I’ll stand at the door,” said Old Lu. He went out.

The rest went quickly. A dozen or more empty crocks, hemp rope threaded through their ears, two to a pole. The glass tube was taken off the big pot, the cloth at the joint unwound turn by turn, and Ah Shui wrapped it in sacking with one hand, three layers. Qiusheng took the tin box down from the shelf, the one with Assay printed on it, opened it and laid the lid upside down on the workbench. Across the top of the reports lay a length of copper pipe, three bends. He took the copper pipe out and gave it to Ah Chang.

“Put it in the pot.”

Ah Chang went up with the copper pipe.

There were two more boxes on the shelf, with no lettering, and he took those down too. He opened them. Empty. He stacked one on the other and put them in the last vat, and the vat was carried out. There were no boxes now on the top shelf. The one with the lettering was on the workbench. He did not put it back.

Trip after trip along the track. A hand trolley went out of the cellar and was pushed to the goods yard, sacks piled on it, and under the sacks vats and crocks; the pot had gone on the first trip. No one hooked the chain back. It hung by the door. At the goods-yard end there was a lamp, yellow, no more than a point in the fog, and Old Qi stood under that point and counted each trip as it passed. At eleven-ten a down goods train came in and the whole goods yard shook for a while. The men pushing the trolley stood still, waited until it had gone by, and pushed again.

Old Hai came back once. He came in from the track end, stood at the foot of the stairs and went no further in.

“Six trips,” he said. “The flat wagon’s full, sacks over it. Old Qi had it roped three times round.”

“And the men?”

“My men ride with it. At the head of the sea wall they unload, hand over to Headman Ah Lu’s men, and come back,” said Old Hai. “They don’t board the boat.”

He glanced at the workbench. The tin box stood open, the lid upside down beside it. He did not ask. He fished the cigarette tin out of his trouser pocket, weighed it in his palm, put it back, and went out by the track end.

The twenty crates were carried by Ah Chang and the others. Up the stairs, to the truck outside the side gate, crate by crate, and the sacks over them. Ah Cai was the last down, and asked if there was any more.

“No more,” said Qiusheng.

The last trip was past twelve. Ah Shui stood up from the stone trough. He had finished washing the last two vats and turned them over, mouths down on the brick floor, next to the one already there. He wiped his left hand once on his knee. The back of the hand was red. He came to the workbench with his head down.

“Teacher, the vats are washed.”

“Mm.”

“Teacher, do I ride along?”

“With Mr Qi.”

Ah Shui went up the stairs. The iron door sounded once.

Qiusheng was alone in the cellar. Three vats upturned on the brick floor, mouths down. The big pot in its place, its side bright, no tube on it. On the shelves a row of empty crocks, the ones left, mouths up. At the foot of the wall at the very back, three bricks lay flat on the earth.

He walked over. The three bricks Old Lu had patted. He squatted, lifted them one by one, carried them to the foot of the wall and stacked them. The earth showed, half an inch lower than the bricks around it, and darker. He stood up and trod the earth over with the sole of his shoe, tread by tread, trod it flat. Earth was still earth and brick was still brick, and the colour was not the same. He did not tread it again.

He went back to the foot of the wall by the stairs. The row of chalk, from 12 October, eighteen degrees, to 31 March, a line a day. He rubbed with his palm at the date on its own line below. He rubbed once, and there was a white smear; he rubbed again, and it was still white. He wiped his hand on his coat. The row of temperatures he left alone. The two lines in the corner of the wall, Wen, union, and under them the word Owed, he left alone too.

The tear-off calendar by the pot he took down. The nail stayed in the wall. He did not tear off the page for the ninth. The calendar went folded into his coat pocket. He opened the thermometer’s case, took the thermometer out, glanced at it and put it back, and the case went into the other pocket. The notebook was in the inside pocket. He pressed it once through the coat.

On the workbench the tin box stood open, the lid upside down beside it. In the box a stack of yellowed paper, twenty years of reports, and under them a few old forms never used. He straightened the top few sheets. The one on top: sample number, place of seizure, the drinking den behind the union, specific gravity, boiling range, methanol seven per cent. In the verdict column one word, in fountain pen, stroke by stroke. Below it the signature. He squared that sheet to the edge of the box. The notebook was not in it.

From the bottom of the box he drew one unused form, turned it over, back up, and uncapped his fountain pen. He wrote two lines, and when he had written them he blew on them. The paste left over from the labels was in a bowl at the corner of the workbench, skinned over. He broke the skin with the end of the pen, dipped, turned the sheet back to the side with the printed boxes and dabbed paste on the four corners. The brick wall above the workbench faced the stairs. He pressed the sheet to it and smoothed it with his palm from the middle out to the edges.

All spirit made at this still has been sold. For the process, apply to Inspector Qiao Zheng, Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau.

He stepped back half a pace and looked. One corner of the sheet was lifting. He pressed it again.

The tin box he did not close.

He went to the foot of the stairs and looked back. Three lamps, the upturned vats, the bright pot, the empty crocks on the shelves, the row of writing on the wall, the white smear under it, the open box on the workbench, the sheet of paper above the box.

Fourteen steps. At the seventh he began to cough, a hand on the wall. Once, twice, and it stopped. He stood a moment and went on up. He pressed the switch at the head of the steps and the three lamps went out together. He pulled the iron door open and fog poured in from outside, cold, smelling of sauce. He stepped out, drew the iron door to, hung the lock back on its ring and put the key in his trouser pocket. The wooden sign was by the door, a film of fog on it.

Outside the side gate nothing could be seen. The lamp at the goods yard was gone. Out by the breakwater the foghorn sounded once.
