*31 July*

It was the last day of the month.

Half past seven, and the footsteps the same as ever. Yet the doorman looked up. Qiusheng had a cardboard box in his hand. Corrugated, the kind measuring cylinders came in, printed with the name of the glassworks in the provincial capital.

Coat on the third peg, box under the bench, oversleeves on. Two crates that morning, twenty-four reports. Young Zhou wrote and Qiusheng called out the figures. At half past eleven the crates were empty.

He took his coat down from the third peg, folded it in four and laid it in the bottom of the box. The drawer slid open. The velvet-lined case opened. He held the hydrometer up to the window. The July daylight was white and hard. There was no need to turn to one side. One look showed whether there were bubbles in the tube. There were none. He put it back in its velvet bed, shut the lid and pushed the case, chamois and all, to the far end of the bench, by Young Zhou’s hand. He took off his oversleeves, folded them, put them in the drawer and pushed the drawer shut.

“Wipe it with the chamois once a month. Don’t let the new men measure soy sauce with it.”

The new men were at the other two benches. Two young men the Prohibition Bureau had sent for the takeover, the badges at their collars still bright, were lifting the measuring cylinders out of the cupboard one by one and ticking them off against a list. One of them looked up and asked Young Zhou, “And this is?”

“Master He,” said Young Zhou.

The young man nodded and bent to his counting again. Young Zhou drew the velvet-lined case in front of him and held it down with both hands.

Young Zhou stood up. “Master He—” Only the two words came out. Qiusheng had already bent and picked up the box. It was not heavy. On top of the coat lay a tear-off calendar, its edges curled, every page printed with the solar term and the tides; a blue-covered tide table with this year’s date on the front; three pencils worn to stubs; an old cloth for wiping measuring cylinders. The chief accountant’s envelope had come at nine. Brown paper, the flap not gummed down. He did not open it. He put it in his shirt pocket.

The director’s door was shut. He did not knock.

It was seventeen paces along the corridor to the front door. He had walked it for twenty years, and today it was still seventeen. This time the doorman stood up.

“Going, Master He?”

“Going.”

Outside the front door the fog had drawn back beyond the breakwater. The new sign the painter had measured for was not yet nailed up. The old one could be seen whole, white paint, black characters, and the character for assay stood there with every stroke in place.

He did not look back.

---

He walked back along the railway line by the docks.

The line ran close to the water. The sun at the end of July pressed straight down, and the rails were hot enough to throw off a white glare. On his left a crane’s chain clanked link by link and a netful of sacks hung turning in mid-air; below it men were calling a work chant. The southern work chant was short, one call on the heels of another, nothing drawn out. On his right a freighter lay moored. She gave one blast on her whistle, low, long, drowning every other sound, and it stopped. A row of sacks lay sunning on the loading platform with their mouths open, and the smell of soya beans and mould rose as the sun worked on them. Carriers went past him one after another, their carrying poles springing, the bamboo creaking on their shoulders.

No one looked at him. A man with a cardboard box: the docks saw a hundred of them a day. Behind him, from the direction of the Assay Office, someone was driving nails, one blow and then another, far off and slow.

By Warehouse No. 3 he was wet with sweat and the back of his shirt was stuck to him. At No. 5 he coughed, once. He did not let go of the box. With the other hand he took out his handkerchief and pressed it to his mouth. When the cough was done he put the handkerchief back in his trouser pocket, changed the box to the other hand, and pressed the freed palm for a moment against the right side of his chest.

At the corner of Warehouse No. 7 a delivery tricycle had gone over. Its cargo bed faced the sky and one wheel was still turning. A crate had burst, glass bottles had rolled everywhere, and a clear liquid was running into the gravel between the rails, giving off a sharp sweetness.

Qiusheng stopped. He did not need to go closer to know that sweetness.

A young man was crouched on the ground picking up bottles and swearing. He picked with one hand and with the other gathered the unbroken ones against his chest, five or six of them, and put them into a crate beside him that had not burst. He lifted the crate in his arms and looked up.

When Ah Yue recognised Qiusheng, he had his arms around a crate he should not have been holding.

“Teacher He?”

Qiusheng set the cardboard box on the ground. The night school’s English and arithmetic class, four years ago. The back row, the seat by the window. A dock boy who was always late and always handed in the best algebra in the class.

“Chen Yue.”

“Ah Yue will do.” The young man put the crate down, stood up, wiped his hands on his trousers and glanced again at the bottles on the ground. “Teacher, you—you didn’t see this.”

Qiusheng bent and picked up a bottle that had not broken. A soda bottle, the trade mark half scraped off. A few more lay on the ground with stoppers of all sizes, one of them a twist of newspaper. He pulled the cork from the one in his hand, brought the mouth of the bottle up under his nose and wafted lightly once with his palm.

“Who gave you this?”

“Teacher—”

“Who gave you this.”

Ah Yue pushed a piece of broken glass over to the rail with his foot. “Black Flag men. Thirty cents a crate, to take it to the drinking den behind the Dockworkers’ Union.”

“What’s in it?”

“Firewater, isn’t it.” Ah Yue shrugged. “Everyone drinks it. If they don’t drink this, what do they drink? A bottle of imported liquor costs me more than half a month’s wages.”

Qiusheng held the bottle up to the daylight. The liquid was very clear, clear as water. That was the most dangerous kind, because it looked as though nothing were wrong with it.

“Forty-one men died on the docks last month,” he said. “What’s in this one bottle is enough to blind three.”

Ah Yue did not move. “Three.” He looked down at the pool still seeping into the gravel, at the bottle in Qiusheng’s hand, and up again.

“How do you know?”

“I’ve assayed it for twenty years.”

Ah Yue bent and took the bottles out of the crate one by one, held each to the light and put them back one by one, half as fast as before.

A carrier came round the corner with two baskets of coal briquettes on his pole. He stepped over the glass, went round the pool and did not stop. The sweetness off the gravel followed him a few paces and thinned away.

Qiusheng pushed the cork back in, put the bottle in the crate and picked up his cardboard box. Ah Yue righted the tricycle by himself. The chain had come off. He crouched to hook it back on. It took him two tries, and both his hands were black.

“Seven broken,” he said. “The hand at the den keeps a tally. If Scarface Cheng hears I spilt a crate, thirty cents won’t cover it.” He looked up at the sky, then at the box in Qiusheng’s hand. “Teacher, what are you doing walking the line with a box in broad daylight?”

“Have you eaten?” Qiusheng asked.

Ah Yue pushed the tricycle ahead, the bottles in the bed rattling all the way. He talked as he walked. The den wanted its goods in the afternoon, so he did the run at midday, when the owner of the trading house took his nap and nobody was watching the tricycle. The hand had told him there was no need to go looking for a man who hid. The docks were only so big. Qiusheng followed with the cardboard box.

---

They sat a whole afternoon at the noodle stall by the docks.

The noodle stall stood at the corner of Warehouse No. 6. A sheet of canvas, propped up, made a patch of shade, and under it were one low table and two benches. The noodle-stall owner was stripped to the waist with a towel round his neck, minding a big pot in which fish balls rose in the boiling water and sank again. Ah Yue leaned the tricycle against a telegraph pole, left the crate in the bed and covered it with a sack. He sat on the side with his back to the sea. Qiusheng sat opposite, his back to the warehouse, the cardboard box at his feet.

“Two bowls of fish-ball noodle soup.” Ah Yue spoke first, calling over towards the pot. “Boss, two extra fish balls.”

The noodles came, ten cents a bowl. Ah Yue’s chopsticks moved fast, three mouthfuls of noodles to one of soup. He lifted the bowl and looked at Qiusheng over the rim.

“Teacher, I heard about the Office.” He put the bowl down. “It’s all over the docks. The Assay Office is going into the Prohibition Bureau and the old hands are leaving one by one. And I thought, not Teacher He, surely.”

“The new establishment,” said Qiusheng.

“What right have they? Teacher, how many bottles a day did you assay at the Office?”

“Thirty-six.”

“Thirty-six bottles, bottle by bottle?”

“Bottle by bottle.”

Qiusheng picked up a fish ball and put it in his mouth. It was real fish, a little strong, the salt just right. He did not mention the X-ray, did not mention the school fees.

“So you’ve no work now.”

“No.”

Ah Yue glanced down at the cardboard box under the table. “Twenty years, and just the one box?”

“Just the one box.”

Ah Yue slapped his chopsticks down across his bowl. “My mother had a stroke last year. Her right side won’t move, and her medicine eats up more than a dollar a month. My brother’s at the municipal primary school, sixth year, he finishes next summer. I pay his fees. After school he comes to the trading house and helps me count sacks, counts till dark, two cents a day. The trading house pays twenty dollars a month. Carrying rice, delivering oil, delivering coal briquettes, from six in the morning till the market shuts. Twenty dollars. Take off eight for the rent, the rest goes on rice and medicine, and it’s gone. Black Flag goods pay more than a dollar a night. Over a month that’s more than the trading house. You tell me, Teacher. If I don’t run this, what do I run?”

He talked fast, as if he were afraid that if he slowed down someone would cut him off. When he had finished he picked up his bowl and drank the soup to the bottom in one go.

“The month my mother was struck down last year, the hand from the den was waiting for me at the door of the trading house. Said there was a night run, thirty cents a run.” He put the empty bowl down. “The first run, my hands shook. The second, they didn’t. When the night’s done the den hand pours me a glass. No charge. I drink it too.”

“Don’t drink it.”

Ah Yue gave his bowl half a turn. “Mm.”

“How many crates a night?”

“Four. Thirty cents a crate, four crates a night. Teacher, that’s what my mother’s medicine costs for a month.”

“Where are the Black Flag’s dens?”

“Four of them.” Ah Yue tapped the table with a chopstick, one tap for each. “The one behind the union is the biggest. The dock men all head there when they knock off. One behind Warehouse No. 8. One at the Saltflat turning. And one on the edge of the Old Town roundabout. The Bureau men set up a checkpoint at the temple gate and the den opens all the same.”

“Who is Scarface Cheng?”

“The head of the Black Flag.” Ah Yue put his chopsticks down and lowered his voice a little. “He was selling firewater on the docks ten years ago. Thirty-odd men under him, and all four dens are his. I’ve never seen him. The ones who run firewater only ever see the den hands. They take the goods, they pay, and that’s all. The hand says it’s Scarface Cheng’s rule: he doesn’t see delivery men.”

Qiusheng nodded.

“The one behind the union, they carried three out of it last month.” Ah Yue went on of his own accord. “Two blind, one gone. The hand opened up just the same. Next day there was a queue just the same, and some of our lot from the trading house were in it. The union men pass the hat for the funerals. Last month there wasn’t a day they didn’t.” He picked up his chopsticks and put them down again. “Teacher, doesn’t the Bureau do anything?”

“They’ve sealed a few hundred crates.”

“And what if they have. The dens open the next day just the same.”

Qiusheng pushed away the half bowl of noodles he had left.

The owner came over to top up their soup. One sweep of the big ladle through the pot, and two fish balls dropped into Ah Yue’s bowl. Ah Yue pulled up the hem of his singlet and wiped the sweat off his face.

“This damned weather. Not a breath of wind.”

“No wind’s a good thing,” said the owner. “The old saying on the docks: ‘The fog comes in at White Dew and lifts at Frost’s Descent; on the days between, a ship goes by its ears, not its eyes.’ When that time comes, you lot who work the docks will know what hardship is.”

Qiusheng heard it and did not take it up. The lid of the cardboard box was not quite shut, and a corner of the tear-off calendar showed. At the foot of today’s page was printed the date of the next solar term.

“Boss, you’ve been saying that for ten years,” said Ah Yue.

“Thirty years,” said the owner. “Once the fog’s in, the Saltflat boats don’t put out, the work on the docks drops by half, and it’s the dens that get their doorsteps worn flat.” He went back to his pot.

Ah Yue ate the two fish balls and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. His chopsticks tapped twice on the rim of the bowl. He had tapped like that at the night school: a problem on the blackboard, the others still copying it down, and his pen already rapping on the edge of the desk.

“Teacher, that stuff. What’s in it, really?”

“Methanol. From the industrial spirit.”

“Can it be got out?”

“We’ll see.”

Ah Yue did not press. He pushed his bowl aside and propped both elbows on the table. The sun had slanted across the canvas and the shade had moved to the other end of the table. The owner wired up a light bulb and hung it on the canvas pole, not yet lit. Over on the docks the shift changed. The work chant stopped for a while and started up again. A group of men off shift walked past the noodle stall with their singlets over their shoulders and turned into the alley behind the union. Ah Yue watched them go by. His lips moved. He was counting. Halfway through he stopped counting, glanced at the sack by the telegraph pole and turned his face back.

“Teacher,” he said, “what if you made it?”

Qiusheng did not take it up. Ah Yue did not wait for him to.

“You said just now that firewater kills people because it’s got that something-anol in it.” Ah Yue dropped his voice. “If you made it, you’d know how to get it out. The liquor you made wouldn’t kill anyone?”

“It would not.”

“Then that’s good liquor.” Ah Yue leaned forward and nearly knocked his bowl over. He put out a hand and held the bowl down. He did not sit back. “Teacher, there are thousands of workers on the docks, and every one of them wants a drink when he knocks off. They don’t want to drink poison. There’s nothing else. You make the good stuff, I’ll deliver it. The Black Flag sell a bottle for ten cents. We sell at a dollar fifty and they’ll still buy. A dollar fifty for a pair of eyes, Teacher. That’s not dear. Transport I’ve got. The trading house’s tricycle is mine at night. And I know people inside the union.”

“It’s against the law.”

“And firewater isn’t?” Ah Yue laughed. “Teacher, the Temperance Act even polices the medicinal wine at the chemist’s, and still men die on the docks every day. Where the law can’t reach, somebody has to.”

Qiusheng looked down at the cardboard box by his feet.

“I need a place,” he said. “Not in town. It has to have water, it has to have air, and it has to be somewhere things can be hidden.”

Ah Yue wiped his hand on his trousers. When he had wiped it, the hand stayed on his knee.

“My uncle has a boat.”

He waited a moment, saw that Qiusheng did not move, and went on: “The Fair Wind. Three years tied up on the mudflats at Saltflat. Nobody goes aboard, not even thieves. She stinks too much. The hold’s big. Ten men could sleep in it.”

“Mm.”

The owner came to collect the bowls. Ah Yue got in first. He pulled a handful of copper coins from his trouser pocket and counted them out on the table one by one, the one-cent pieces and the two-cent pieces in separate rows, made up twenty cents and pushed it across. Qiusheng’s hand went towards his pocket. Ah Yue laid one hand flat on the coins.

“Teacher, today I’m paying.”

“Flush today, Ah Yue,” said the owner.

“My teacher.”

The owner gave Qiusheng a look and swept the coins clinking into his apron pocket.

“The boat’s on the mudflats at Saltflat.” Ah Yue stood and pushed the bench back under the table. “The tide’s in now, you can’t walk out to her. Low tide tonight isn’t till past twelve.” He thought. “Eleven o’clock, the South Slope roundabout, the last bus. Teacher, you go home first and rest—”

“At the roundabout.”

Ah Yue wheeled the tricycle out from beside the telegraph pole. Under the sack in the bed, the bottles left in the crate clinked. He did not say where those bottles were to be delivered. Qiusheng did not ask.

Qiusheng walked back from the docks to South Slope with the cardboard box. He took the outer road. It passed the wall of the municipal primary school, where lessons were long over. It passed the market, where the stalls were all packed away, Su Qi’s too, the boards stacked against the wall and tied with a length of hemp rope. He stood by the wall a moment, changed the box to his other hand and went on towards the roundabout. The chemist’s at the foot of the slope was still open, and the little sign at its door, “Liquor Sold on Prescription”, showed white in the evening sun. He walked past the door.

---

It was full dark. The street lamps of the South Slope roundabout came on, a ring of them, lighting a ring of empty stone benches. Qiusheng sat on the one by the bus stop with the cardboard box on his knees. Up the slope the flats lit up one household after another. He could count his way to the window that was his own: the one on the balcony, the light on, a figure crossing it and crossing back.

He did not go up the slope.

The cicadas shrilled in the banyan on the roundabout and did not let up until ten. Damp rose out of the ground and the stone bench was sticky. A patrol car made one slow circuit of the roundabout. A face in its window turned towards the benches and turned away again, and the car drove off.

Qiusheng lifted the lid of the box. The tear-off calendar was on top with today’s page still hanging on it: 31 July. It was not yet past twelve. He did not tear it off. He pressed the lid down tight.

The last bus left the roundabout at eleven. It swung up from the foot of the slope, dark green, with the door open and the conductor on the step, a leather ticket satchel slung across him, calling Saltflat, Saltflat. Ah Yue came at a run and did not pant until he had jumped aboard. He sat down beside Qiusheng. The fare was ten cents, two tickets. Ah Yue’s hand went for his trouser pocket. Qiusheng had already put twenty cents in the conductor’s palm. The conductor tore two tickets from the leather satchel, drew a pencil stroke across them and said last bus, no run back from the terminus.

There were seven or eight people on the bus, all going home to Saltflat. Their baskets smelled of fish, and someone slept against the window. The bus went round the Old Town roundabout. The checkpoint table at the temple gate had been taken in. One lamp was left. On the edge of the roundabout a door stood half open. Inside an oil lamp was burning, a row of men sat on a bench, and a den hand was dipping a long-handled ladle into a vat. Ah Yue pointed with his chin. “That’s the one.” A man came out of the door, stood a while with a hand on the wall, and squatted down. The bus went on past.

The bus left the town. There were no street lamps and outside the windows it was black. Only the water of the salt pans gave back a little light from the sky, pan after pan going by. Ah Yue did not talk. His fingers tapped on his knee, tapped the beat of the work chant from the docks.

The Saltflat stop was the terminus. The metalled road ended at the stop. Behind the sign a dirt road ran off towards the sea, deeply rutted. Ah Yue said the fish trucks used it, and people going to the ferry landing. It was three li and slow going on wheels.

They did not take the dirt road. They went up onto the sea wall of the salt pans. The wall was rammed earth, with the salt pans on one side and the mudflats on the other. Two figures were still out on the pans raking salt, and the sound of the rakes scraping through the grains, shh, shh, carried a long way. Ah Yue said that in the heat the salt workers all worked by night. By day a man could not stand out on the pans. One of them straightened up and called towards the wall, “Ah Yue! Looking at your uncle’s boat?”

“Having a look,” Ah Yue called back.

“At night?”

“No time in the day.”

The man gave a laugh. “Looking to see if she leaks? She’s been leaking three years.” The rake began to scrape again.

Ah Yue walked on a few paces and said quietly, “Everybody in Saltflat knows this boat. They know the boat. They don’t know what’s done on her.” The wind came in off the sea with salt in it and scraped at their faces, and each scrape left the skin a layer rougher. They walked twenty minutes. The sea wall ended, and below it were the mudflats.

The tide was out. A great stretch of mud lay bare, black and shining, with an oily gleam under the moon. The smell came in three layers: the rankness of mud on top, salt in the middle, and underneath a layer of rot that rose whenever the wind dropped. Ah Yue lit the hurricane lantern he had brought. Its light swept across the mud, which was thick with small holes. Fiddler crabs stood with one big claw raised, saw the light, and one by one drew back into their holes.

“That sandspit across the water is the fishing village.” Ah Yue pointed the lantern at a line of dark shapes out towards the sea. “The ferry makes three crossings a day, morning, noon and evening. It comes over from the docks and puts in at the ferry landing over there.” He lowered the lantern. “Don’t let the ferry see what goes on aboard. Ferry people talk, and the ferryman’s tongue is quicker than his boat.”

At the end of the sandspit there were three or four points of light. Su Qi’s mother’s house was there. Qiusheng had been twice. He did not say so.

“Teacher, look at these flats.” Ah Yue shone the lantern over the mud. “For the two hours of low tide the mud’s hard. You can drive straight across here and save half an hour to the docks. Once the tide comes in it’s gone. No road at all.”

Qiusheng looked down at his shoes. The soles had sunk half an inch into the mud. He pulled one out. The mud was hard.

---

The Fair Wind was an old forty-foot fishing boat with barnacles all over her hull. Ah Yue’s uncle had not been to sea since his stroke three years before, and she had been tied up ever since on the mudflats at Saltflat. At low tide she lay tilted in the mud and at high tide she floated again, like an old man who could not sleep well.

They boarded from the low side. The gunwale there was waist-high. Ah Yue swung himself over first, turned to take the hurricane lantern, then pulled Qiusheng up. The deck sloped, and at every step they had to grip the seams between the planks with the soles of their feet. The hatch was amidships, under a board cover.

“Uncle says the boat’s mine to use.” Ah Yue’s hand rested on the cover. “He says he won’t go to sea again in this life, so let her rot in the mud. What I’d use her for, he never asked. Not a word.”

He lifted the cover. It was black underneath, and a smell came up at them, fish mixed with diesel.

Ah Yue hung the hurricane lantern on the iron hook at the hatch, and the light fell from above. The hatch was two feet square. The ladder had four rungs, the third loose, tipping to one side underfoot. Qiusheng went down the ladder and measured the width of the hatch with his hands. A pot would come down through it.

The boards of the hold sloped too, and a man standing on them slid towards the low side. He steadied himself against the bulkhead and laid his palm flat on it. The wood was cool and wet. When he took the hand away there was a film of water on the palm. He looked up. The air vent was in the roof of the hold, towards the bow, a square hole the size of a hand, letting in a little moonlight. He took the old cloth for wiping measuring cylinders out of the cardboard box and held it up under the hole. A corner of the cloth stirred and drifted towards the hole. There was a draught. He put the cloth back in the box.

He crouched and knocked on the boards of the bilge, once, once more, then moved and knocked again. Solid. He walked once round the bulkheads counting his paces: twelve long, five wide. A door shut off the forward hold. It was not locked. Inside it was darker, with a few bundles of rotten net in a heap. He went in and laid his palm on the bulkhead of the forward hold. It was a good deal cooler than the one outside, and the beads of water were thicker.

In the seaward bulkhead of the after hold there was a porthole, a foot and a half across, iron-framed, its hinges rusted. He came back and pushed at it. It stuck halfway and opened only when he pushed harder. The sea lay below. With the tide out it was a dozen feet or more from the hull, and the sound of the water was very faint. He put his hand out through the porthole and measured. A pipe would go out through it.

He took a pencil from the cardboard box and laid it on the boards. The pencil rolled. It rolled all the way to the low side and stopped only when it met the bulkhead.

“At low tide the boat lies tilted,” he said. “The pot has to be level. We can only distil at high tide.”

Ah Yue put his head in at the hatch. “Then high tide it is.”

“What time is high tide at this boat?”

Ah Yue opened his mouth. “Depends on the day, doesn’t it… once in the morning, once at night, which day at what time—”

Qiusheng did not wait for him to finish. He drew the blue-covered tide table out of the cardboard box, turned to August and ran his finger down the column. The 1st: low tide at 0.50, high tide at 6.40. The 2nd, three quarters of an hour later. The 3rd, later again. Turning the pages, he looked up and saw the cigarette tucked behind Ah Yue’s ear. Ah Yue’s hand was on its way to the ear.

“No fire down here.”

Ah Yue’s hand stopped halfway. He tucked the cigarette back behind his ear, tucked it tight. “Teacher, where did that book come from?”

“Office issue. The Customs has to watch the tides.”

“A lantern isn’t fire?”

“The lantern’s up there.”

Ah Yue gave a laugh and sat down at the hatch with both feet hanging.

“Water?” Qiusheng asked.

“There’s a tank in the bow that catches the rain. When that’s gone you carry it from the well at the far end of the sea wall.” Ah Yue got up and went forward, and in a while came down with a gourd dipper half full of water.

Qiusheng wet a finger and put it to the tip of his tongue. Salt. Not strong, but it sat on the root of the tongue and would not leave. He handed the dipper back.

“Well water?”

“It’s a Saltflat well, isn’t it.”

“No hurry.”

Ah Yue set the dipper down by the ladder. “What do you think?”

“The pot goes here.” Qiusheng pointed to the lowest part of the hold. “The condenser goes out through the porthole and the sea cools it. The fermenting vats go in the forward hold. It’s darker there and the temperature is steadier.”

“So you’ll do it?”

Qiusheng crouched and with one finger drew a diagram in the dust on the boards: a pot, a bent pipe, a collecting jar. He drew it with great care, the angle of the pipe, the place of the jar, where the thermometer went in, stroke by stroke, the way he had once drawn the figures attached to his reports at the Assay Office. When it was done he wrote two times of day beside it, one for high tide, one for low.

Ah Yue came over and crouched beside the diagram. The hurricane lantern was overhead and pressed the two men’s shadows down onto it. He pointed at the short line beside the pot.

“What’s this?”

“The thermometer.”

“You need a thermometer to distil?”

“You do.”

“And the pot?”

“Copper. A small one first.”

“There are bankrupt sauce works all over. I’ll go and look.” Ah Yue’s finger traced once round the circle of the pot without touching the dust. “And the rice?”

“Broken will do. It has to be clean.”

“The rice merchant on the docks has broken rice at the back door. Cheap.”

“I’ll pick the yeast myself.”

Ah Yue counted the things off on his fingers: pot, rice, yeast, water. He looked at the diagram a while longer, and slowly he smiled.

“Teacher, you’d thought it all out long ago.”

Qiusheng picked up the pencil and put it back in the cardboard box.

There was a sound from under the boat. Timber shifted in the mud, and shifted again.

“It’s coming in,” said Ah Yue. “If we don’t go now there’ll be no flats left.”

They left the boat by the low side. The mud was softer than when they had come, and their soles came out of it with a sound. Across the water the lights on the sandspit were all out. Ah Yue said the first ferry was at half past six and passed these flats on its way out from the ferry landing. At that hour there must be no one about the boat. At the foot of the sea wall they looked back. The Fair Wind still lay there, and the water had already closed round her stern.

At the head of the sea wall by the salt pans a fish truck was parked, the empty baskets in the bed stacked as high as the cab roof, the driver dozing at the wheel. It would leave at three for the fish market on the docks. Ah Yue knocked on the door. The driver took his cap off his face.

“Take a man along with you. As far as the fish-market gate.”

“It stinks of fish.”

“He won’t mind.”

The driver put the cap back over his face, which did for a yes. Ah Yue turned and beckoned. Qiusheng climbed onto the bed and sat among the baskets with the cardboard box in his arms. Ah Yue did not get in. When the truck pulled away he stood at the head of the sea wall. He did not wave.

---

The baskets in the bed were empty, and full of the smell of fish. The truck passed the Old Town roundabout. That door was shut, and a man lay on the stone step in front of it. The driver sounded his horn once and did not stop. The fish truck put Qiusheng down at the fish-market gate on the docks. He walked up South Slope. It was still dark, a quarter to four. The light on the stairs was out and he felt his way up by the banister. There was light under the door.

Su Qi was still under the lamp. A girls’ school uniform, some other family’s, was spread on her knees with the cuff unpicked. She held two pins in her mouth, points outward, and the needle in her hand went up and down. It was to be collected at seven. Twenty cents. Xiaoman’s door was shut.

He took off his shoes by the door. The mud on the soles had dried and fell on the floor in pieces. He put the cardboard box down beside the rattan chair. Su Qi glanced at the box, then at the shoes by the door.

“From the Office?”

“Mm.”

“There’s rice in the pot.”

“I’ve eaten.”

He sat down in the rattan chair and said he had found work on the docks, a night shift, checking cargo for a trading house.

Su Qi’s needle did not stop. “Which trading house?”

“On the docks.”

She took the pins from her mouth and stuck them back in the pincushion one by one. “Mm.”

The needle went through the cloth, the thread was drawn right through, her hand pressed the cloth once. “From what time till what time?”

“Eleven till daylight.”

She said that was good. She wound the thread twice round her finger and snapped it. “Xiaoman waited for you to test her vocabulary. She waited till ten.”

“Mm.”

The needle went through again. “And the cough?”

He said it was much better.

It was the first lie he told her. Later he reckoned it up: that year he told her more than three hundred lies in all, and each one was a little easier than the one before.
