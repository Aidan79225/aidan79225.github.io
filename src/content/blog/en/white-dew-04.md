*September · Before the Autumn Equinox*

In under three weeks White Dew already had a name on the docks.

The workers called it White Dew, and they called it “Teacher’s liquor”, or simply “liquor that won’t blind you”. Old Hai wanted two hundred bottles a week. The fish truck reached the fish market in the early morning. The union men waited at the fish-market gate with a handcart, and Old Hai stood behind the handcart with his one eye on the bed of the truck. When the crates had been carried across he took from inside his shirt a wad of notes held with a rubber band and handed it over. He did not count them.

“Where’s Teacher?”

“On the boat,” said Ah Yue.

Old Hai nodded. The handcart was pushed away, its wheels loud on the flagstones of the fish market.

A nightclub in Upper Town called the Paramount sent a man to the union to ask about supply. The man who came wore a suit. He stood outside the red-brick doorway and would not step in, the toes of his shoes an inch from the threshold. Ah Yue went out to talk to him.

“How much a bottle?”

“A dollar fifty on the docks,” said Ah Yue. “Two dollars in Upper Town.”

“A dollar fifty on the docks. Why two dollars in Upper Town?”

“Upper Town is far. I have to get past the roundabout, and there’s a checkpoint at the roundabout.” Ah Yue wiped his hand on his trousers. “Two dollars. A cent less and I don’t deliver. What you sell it to your customers for, I don’t ask.”

The suit asked how many bottles he could have in a week. Ah Yue said a hundred. The suit said he would take three hundred to begin with, delivered over three weeks. He left an address and went. The toes of his shoes never did touch the threshold.

From that week on, one night a week, Ah Yue rode to Upper Town. The delivery tricycle was still the one borrowed from the trading house. The story for borrowing it had changed from moving house to delivering vegetables for someone, and the hand there took ten cents a night from him and asked no more. The crates went into sacks, and on top of the sacks went two baskets of vegetables. The owner of the trading house went to bed early and had never asked why the tricycle in the back yard was wet at night.

The hold of the Fair Wind could take no more. From a bankrupt sauce works in the Old Town Ah Yue hauled back two more vats and a bigger copper pot. The pot was bought by weight, and vats and pot together came to eleven dollars. The last of the rotten net was cleared out of the forward hold, the bed of ballast sand was laid a stretch further out, and the two vats were set in a row beside the first. Distil at high tide, fill the vats at low tide: Qiusheng lived by the times chalked on the bulkhead. Some days he could not get back to South Slope even by noon, and slept two hours on the canvas by the hatch. The canvas was salt. He woke covered in dew, coughed first, then got up.

There was more and more money. The top shelf of the wardrobe had no more room, and at an ironmonger’s in the Old Town he bought a second box, the same size, with no lettering. He had nowhere to spend this money, and he could not spend it: an assayer who had been made redundant and could suddenly pay school fees and afford a doctor would set even Mrs Chen downstairs asking. When he got back to South Slope in the morning Su Qi had already gone to her stall. On the table stood a bowl of rice porridge with a saucer over it, and under the saucer a slip of paper: Salted eggs in the pot. He ate, washed the bowl and put the slip in his pocket, on top of the bus tickets.

That night Ah Yue came back from the docks. At the foot of the ladder he slapped a wad of notes down on the lid of the pot, then stood an empty bottle beside the notes. The paper label on the bottle was in his own hand. Someone had gone over the two words with a fingernail, pressing deep.

“Teacher, this week Old Hai took two hundred bottles, three hundred dollars. The Paramount a hundred bottles, two hundred dollars, and they say it’ll all be settled together before Mid-Autumn. Old Hai sells it by the cup inside the union, five cents a cup, sold out in a night. Old Hai’s lot call it White Dew. The porters call it Teacher’s liquor. The ones queuing for a cup don’t trouble with all that, they call it Won’t-Blind-You. Teacher, the name is already on the street. Whether you own it or not, that’s what they call it.”

“Let them.” Qiusheng opened the notebook. “The ledger still says rice wine.”

He wrote: *19 September, fifth vat, rice wine, ninety-one.* Then he closed the notebook and put it under the thermometer case. Ah Yue picked up the empty bottle and looked at it, peeled off the paper label, folded it into a small square and put it in his trouser pocket.

---

The north wind came as soon as the day of White Dew was past, and it blew for ten days. The water in the salt pans wrinkled. The mud of the flats grew harder by the day. On the sandspit across the water the fishing boats were hauled up onto the sand one by one, and their masts lay flat there. For ten days not one was raised.

On the eleventh day high tide came in the early morning, and Qiusheng was in the hold drawing the fire. Something knocked against the hull outside. It was not water. It knocked again. He climbed up on deck.

A small boat lay against the seaward side of the Fair Wind. In the bow a man stood holding a pole. Sixty or so, in a bamboo hat, his trousers rolled to the knee, white lines of salt down his calves. In the boat were an empty bamboo basket and another basket covered with sackcloth.

“Teacher,” the man said.

Qiusheng knew him. Headman Ah Lu of Saltflat Village, the village Su Qi’s family came from. He had seen him twice at the ferry landing, and both times the headman had called him Mr He.

“The north wind has blown for ten days and the boats can’t put to sea,” said the headman. He spoke slowly, stopping twice in a sentence. “The old ones in the village… come winter they cough. Other years, what they drank was the kind from the docks.”

Qiusheng stood at the gunwale.

“The old folk have a winter to get through. Give us the spirit now; the money comes with the spring fish.”

“How many bottles?”

“Fifty.” The headman thought. “With fifty we’d…”

Qiusheng went below. Fifty bottles filled three crates. He carried them to the hatch one by one, then lowered them over the side one by one. The headman took them in the small boat, and with each crate the small boat sank an inch. When the last crate was steady the headman felt inside his shirt and brought out a cloth bundle. He untied it. Money. Copper coins, a few old notes, a few silver ten-cent pieces. He crouched on the boards and counted, laying them one at a time on the lid of a crate. He filled a row, swept it apart and began again from the start.

“No hurry,” said Qiusheng. “In spring.”

The headman did not look up. “Teacher has…” He counted into the second row and stopped. “Teacher has a winter to get through too.”

Qiusheng’s hand pressed down on the gunwale for a moment.

The coughing in the hold at night went out through the porthole and over the flats, and when the wind was right it carried as far as the sandspit. The headman did not say he had heard it. He pushed the counted money to the edge of the crate, seven dollars forty in all, and said the rest would be made up in spring. Then he lifted up the basket covered with sackcloth, and Qiusheng took it. Dried fish, a basketful, dried right through.

“Teacher, the flats…” The headman took the pole in his hand and looked at the barnacles on the hull. “This boat, I’ve been out in her. The years Ah Yue’s uncle was still working her.”

Qiusheng nodded.

“The people on the ferry say the boat on the flats has a light at night.” The headman planted his pole. “I tell them I’ve set someone to watch the boat.”

He drew a bottle from a crate and held it up to the daylight. The liquor in the bottle was so clear it was like nothing at all. He looked a while and pushed it back into the crate. One touch of the pole and the small boat left the hull and went with the falling tide towards the sandspit.

Qiusheng had seen Old Hai only once, through a truck window, in an alley at dusk. The headman, standing in his small boat, was the first whose whole face he saw by daylight.

He took the seven dollars forty below, laid it on the lid of a crate, opened the notebook and wrote under yesterday’s line: *Saltflat, fifty.* He looked at what he had written and added two words: *In spring.* He coughed for a while with the handkerchief pressed to his mouth, and when that was done he carried the basket of dried fish to the shade of the forward hold, well away from the vats.

At noon Ah Yue came along the sea wall, and the first thing he smelled was the dried fish.

“Teacher, where’s this from?”

“Headman Ah Lu.”

“The Saltflat headman?” Ah Yue lifted the cover of the basket and put it back. “How does he know there’s liquor on the boat?”

“The boat is on his flats.”

Ah Yue picked the copper coins off the crate lid one by one and dropped them into the tin. Halfway through he looked up. “Fifty bottles, seven dollars forty?”

“In spring.”

The rest of the copper coins went in too. Ah Yue shook the tin and listened to it.

“What does he call it?”

“Nothing.”

Ah Yue pressed the lid onto the tin, and pressed it tight.

---

The Paramount was on the ring road in Upper Town. It was Ah Yue’s third delivery, and as before he rode the tricycle only as far as the mouth of the road and got down to push. Motor horns hurried him from behind, one blast after another, and a tram bell rang ahead. Over the door a row of neon tubes, red ones and green ones, spelled the name in foreign lettering, and only under that came the three Chinese characters. He did not use the front door.

The back alley was on the other side of the building, narrow, stacked with soda crates, the ground covered with lemon peel and crushed ice. The drum of the foreign band leaked through the crack of the kitchen door, thud, thud, thud, and with a door in between it sounded like someone inside carrying cargo upstairs. Two dancing girls sat on the soda crates smoking, their qipao slit to the thigh. They watched him unload the crates one by one and said nothing.

“From the docks?” one of the dancing girls asked.

Ah Yue set a crate down by the door and did not answer. The girl passed her cigarette to the other, and the other took a pull and handed it back.

The floor manager came out of the kitchen door. White shirt, black waistcoat, hair oiled to a shine, a book in his hand.

“Round the back, don’t let the guests see.”

Ah Yue carried the crates in through the door. The floor manager counted crates and made marks in the book with a pencil. At the fifth crate he drew out a bottle, looked at it against the kitchen light and slid it back.

“Three trips, three hundred bottles,” he said. “Anything short I put down here.”

“Not a bottle short.”

“Boss Ding says this lot’s settled before Mid-Autumn.”

“Last time he said that too—”

“Boss Ding says it’s settled before Mid-Autumn.” The floor manager shut the book, tore off a chit and held it out. The chit had only the figure on it, no name.

At the end of the passage inside the door was the back of the bar, with the lights on. A man in a white suit stood by the bar, a gold watch showing at his cuff. He took a bottle of White Dew from the barman’s hand and pulled the cork. The nails on the fingers that held the cork were trimmed and very clean. He sniffed once at the mouth of the bottle, gave the bottle back to the barman and said something, and the barman nodded. He did not look towards the back door.

Ah Yue set the last crate down and pushed the empty tricycle out of the alley. Behind him a dancing girl’s cigarette end fell on the crushed ice with a hiss.

On the ring road a Prohibition Bureau patrol car came slowly from the other direction. Its headlamps swept over the tricycle’s empty cargo bed and swept on. Ah Yue did not stop, and he did not hurry. An empty tricycle has nothing to fear from a search. He had learned that in the days when he ran firewater. Only past the roundabout did he get on and ride, downhill all the way, the wind filling his shirt, the drum still in his ears.

---

The trouble started with Boss Ding.

Boss Ding of the Paramount had taken three hundred bottles of White Dew, with payment agreed before Mid-Autumn. Seven days before Mid-Autumn, a letter came. The floor manager brought it to the union door and handed it to Ah Yue. A brown paper envelope, with the Paramount’s name in foreign lettering printed in the corner. The letter said there was a problem with the quality of the goods, the customers got headaches, and the bill would be paid at seventy per cent. It was typewritten. The name signed at the bottom was the floor manager’s. Boss Ding’s name appeared nowhere in it.

Ah Yue took the letter back to the boat and read it out by the miner’s safety lamp. When he came to seventy per cent he slapped it down on the lid of the pot.

“My arse.” He snatched the letter up and crushed it into a ball. “Our liquor doesn’t give anyone a headache. He just wants to dodge the bill.”

“I know.” Qiusheng took the ball of paper, smoothed it flat on his knee and read it again. “He knows we can’t go to the police.”

“Then what do we do? I’ll take a few of the brothers from the docks and—”

“And then?” Qiusheng looked up at him. “You beat up Boss Ding, Boss Ding goes to the police, the police follow you to the boat and the boat to me. You’re arrested, I’m arrested, and the men on the docks go back to drinking firewater.”

Ah Yue said no more. He clenched his fists until the knuckles went white.

Qiusheng folded the letter and put it in his pocket. “We need a man. A man who knows the law and doesn’t care about the law.”

Ah Yue’s fists opened. “The one in the paper every day?”

“Mm.”

---

Counsel Liang’s chambers were on the first floor of a red-brick building in Upper Town. Below was a shop selling Western dresses, where a gramophone played from morning till night, and however the records were changed it was always the same woman’s voice. The stairwell was papered with his advertisements: COUNSEL LIANG. NO CASE TOO LOST. DIVORCE, DEBT, CRIMINAL, TRADE MARKS. ALL HANDLED. Beside the words was drawn a little man in a top hat with his thumb up. The advertisements were pasted one over another, and of the little man on the bottom sheet only one hand still showed.

As Qiusheng and Ah Yue went up, a woman came down the stairs with a handkerchief pressed to her eyes. She squeezed through between the two of them, and her heels rang all the way to the street. The door on the first floor stood open, and on the door was a brass plate: LIANG HENIAN, BARRISTER.

The chambers were one room and two desks. Behind the big desk by the window sat a man, and behind him stood a bookcase, leather-bound books in rows, not one letter of the gilt on their spines worn away. At a small desk by the door a lady typist sat in front of a typewriter with her spectacles on the tip of her nose. Her hands were still. Her ears were not. There were two chairs before the big desk. Qiusheng took one; Ah Yue stood behind the other, holding to his chest a bottle of White Dew wrapped in newspaper.

Liang Henian in person was fatter than in the advertisements. He wore a pale grey suit, with a pink handkerchief in the pocket. He spoke first.

“Teacher. The ‘Teacher’s liquor’ they talk of on the docks. This is the gentleman?” He did not wait for an answer. His fountain pen tapped once on the desk. “Sit down, sit down. You too, Mr Chen.”

Ah Yue did not sit. He told the business of Boss Ding from the beginning: three hundred bottles, two dollars a bottle, payment before Mid-Autumn, seventy per cent. Counsel Liang tapped the desk with his fountain pen as he listened, keeping a beat. At seventy per cent the beat stopped for a moment, then went on. When the telling was done he put the pen down.

“The letter?”

Qiusheng took the letter out of his pocket. The paper had been crushed and its creases had gone soft. He pushed it across the desk. Counsel Liang unfolded it and read fast, his eyes resting only on the signature at the bottom. He picked up the fountain pen and tapped the name with the butt of it.

“Signed by the floor manager,” he said. “You see, Mr Chen, Boss Ding won’t sign his own name even to dodge a bill. I have seen plenty of his kind. Half of any street in Upper Town.” He folded the letter along its creases and pushed it back. Qiusheng put it in his pocket.

“Three hundred bottles at two dollars a bottle. A bill of six hundred dollars,” said Counsel Liang. “I take twenty per cent.”

“A hundred and twenty dollars?” said Ah Yue. “A hundred and twenty dollars for one letter?”

“One letter.” Counsel Liang nodded. “Mr Chen, a hundred and twenty dollars will not buy a decent desk in Upper Town. On the docks it is a hundred and fifty days under the carrying pole. If you do not pay this hundred and twenty, you will not see a dollar of that six hundred, and you are out three hundred bottles of liquor into the bargain. Work out for yourself which way pays.”

Ah Yue worked it out. He said nothing.

“You can make him pay?” he asked.

“I can make him pay, I can make him apologise, and I can make him order five hundred more next month.” Counsel Liang laughed, showing a gold tooth. “Teacher, let me say this at the outset: I am not going there to reason with Boss Ding. A man you can reason with never comes my way. Boss Ding’s nightclub has no business licence, it fails the fire regulations, and half his dancing girls’ registrations are false. All I have to do is send one letter. The letter need say nothing at all. It need only carry my name.”

The lady typist’s hands moved. The typewriter clacked through one line and stopped. She drew the sheet out, got up and laid it on the big desk. Counsel Liang signed it without a glance and folded the sheet in three.

“Envelope.”

The lady typist handed him an envelope. Counsel Liang put the letter in and licked the flap. Then, as if he had remembered something, he took his handkerchief and wiped the corners of his mouth, and while he was about it wiped his forehead.

“Upper Town business is done by Upper Town people,” he said. “Teacher, what a man like Boss Ding does, you on the docks call dodging a bill. Up here we call it trying the price. He tries you once, to see whether there is anyone behind you. If there is, next month he orders as before; if there is not, next month he pays fifty per cent.”

Qiusheng looked at the fountain pen on the desk. The cap was gilded too.

Ah Yue put the bottle of White Dew he had been holding on the corner of the desk. The newspaper fell open and the bottle stood there. The lady typist’s spectacles slid down a little, and she pushed them up. Counsel Liang did not touch the bottle.

“Leave it there,” he said. “I don’t drink in front of clients.”

He turned his chair to face Qiusheng squarely.

“Teacher, one question I ought not to ask. This bottle. What does it cost you, in cents?”

“We’ll see.”

“You needn’t say.” Counsel Liang’s fountain pen tapped once more. “When I act for a man, I look first at his capital, then at his nerve. Where the capital is thin, all the nerve in the world is no use; where it is ample, a little nerve is enough. Which kind Teacher is, I shall know after two days’ watching.”

He drew a card from the drawer and held it out between two fingers.

“The chambers have a telephone. Upper Town 237. If anything comes up, ring that. Don’t ring from a public telephone. The girl on the public exchange comes from the same part of the country as one of the clerks at the Prohibition Bureau.”

Qiusheng took the card. White card, black print, the back blank.

“One more thing.” Counsel Liang leaned back in his chair, and the chair-back creaked. “The papers say Parliament is to reconsider the Temperance Act next year.”

Qiusheng put the card in his shirt pocket and stood up.

“Twenty per cent,” he said.

“Twenty per cent.” Counsel Liang stood too and put his hand out across the desk. The hand was soft. “That’s the going rate.”

As they went out the lady typist stopped Ah Yue. “Where is the answer to go?”

“The union door. Ask for Ah Yue.”

She wrote a line in her book, and her spectacles slid down again. Downstairs the record on the gramophone had been turned over. Still the same woman.

Three days later Boss Ding came to the docks to pay in person, with a box of egg tarts.

The motor car stopped outside the union. It was black, and its body was cleaner than anything else beside the railway line. A few porters stopped to look, their carrying poles across their shoulders. Old Hai came out through the union’s red-brick doorway and stood on the threshold, his one eye on the car, the cigarette in his hand unlit. Ah Yue stood outside the door with his hands empty. The floor manager got out first, Boss Ding after him. It was the floor manager who handed over the money, a wad of notes in one of the Paramount’s paper bands, six hundred dollars, not a cent short. It was the floor manager who carried the egg tarts too, a cardboard box tied with a red string. Boss Ding stood by the car door, white suit, gold watch, and gave Ah Yue one look, from his head to his shoes. He did not speak to Ah Yue. Before he got in he turned his head and asked the floor manager, “This is the one who delivers?”

The floor manager said it was.

“Five hundred bottles next month.” The floor manager turned to Ah Yue. “Boss Ding says so.”

“Five hundred bottles, two dollars a bottle,” said Ah Yue. “Settled on delivery.”

The floor manager did not answer. He got into the car. The car drove off. Ah Yue tore the paper band off the wad of notes and counted it there in the street, six hundred, and counted it again. Boss Ding had paid, and had remembered Ah Yue’s face. Old Hai lit his cigarette and went back into the union. The porters shifted their carrying poles to the other shoulder and walked on.

There were six egg tarts. Ah Yue took them back to the boat, three each. Qiusheng ate one and pushed the other two across. When Ah Yue had finished he undid the red string from the box and wound it round his finger, three turns.

Counsel Liang’s message came on the fifth day. Ah Yue came back from Upper Town and said Counsel Liang invited Teacher to tea, Sunday afternoon at three, the Nanji in the Old Town.

“Why the Nanji?”

“He says the tea’s cheap there,” said Ah Yue. “And he says Teacher will know when he gets there.”

---

The second time Counsel Liang met Qiusheng, it was in the private room of that teahouse.

The Nanji Teahouse stood at the edge of the Tin Hau Temple square in the Old Town, under the arcade. In the middle of the square was the Tin Hau Temple, its gate facing the sea, with a couplet carved on its stone pillars. Wide the sea: a thousand sails in the goddess’s keeping. Bright the lamp: one point of light to bring the boats home. In front of the temple the incense seller, the peanut seller and the umbrella mender each had a flagstone of his own. The arcade side was a row of old shopfronts, and the Nanji had two of them, the main room below and the private room above. At the foot of the stairs hung the price board, written in chalk: Tea, ten cents a head. Dim sum from two cents.

Qiusheng and Ah Yue arrived at three in the afternoon. The waiter came to meet them and said Mr Liang had booked the private room upstairs and was not here yet, would they please go up. There were seven or eight tables of customers in the main room. Another waiter came out of the kitchen carrying bamboo steamers and calling the dishes as he went, roast pork buns, prawn dumplings, fried savoury dumplings, drawing the last syllable out long. On the counter a wireless was playing Cantonese music from the provincial capital, the erhu thin and high. There was a spittoon on the floor at every third table, and on a pillar hung two birdcages, the thrushes hopping inside. In the corner a little stage had been put up, with one stool and one lamp, and on it a singer in a pale qipao held a pipa, her head bent, tuning the strings. As the two of them crossed the main room towards the stairs she looked up once and bent her head again. A peg turned half a turn. Ah Yue walked in front, his step lighter than usual.

The private room was upstairs, closed off by a board partition. The partition was papered with old newspapers, and the dates on the newspapers were still last year’s. The waiter brought the tea up, one pot, three cups, and went out, pulling the door to. Qiusheng sat in the seat by the partition with his back to the stairs; Ah Yue did not sit, and leaned by the door.

“Teacher, I’ve been here before,” said Ah Yue. “It’s cheap downstairs. Ten cents and you sit all afternoon.”

“Mm.”

“Business is bad.” Ah Yue tapped the floorboards with his heel. “The waiter says the owner owes people money.”

Qiusheng lifted the lid of the pot. The leaf was broken. Tea sweepings. He put the lid back.

Downstairs the pipa sounded. The singer’s voice came up through the board partition, not loud, every word clear: “Fog comes in on the harbour, white on white, a lamp at the landing waits for my love tonight. Oil for the lamp till the night of Frost’s Descent, the fog will lift, the boat come home with fish packed tight.”

At the third line Ah Yue’s hand stopped on his trousers and did not wipe. When the song ended someone downstairs clapped twice, and it was quiet again.

It was then that Counsel Liang pushed the door open and came in. He looked first down the stairs, then at Ah Yue by the door, and the gold tooth flashed.

“That girl is Ah Yue’s,” he said to Qiusheng, and did not wait for a reply. “She sings well. Mr Chen, you keep the door. I’ll have a few words with Teacher.”

Ah Yue glanced at Qiusheng and went out. The door was not shut tight.

Counsel Liang sat down opposite Qiusheng and looked him up and down.

“Teacher,” he said. “Last time, in chambers, it was the account I looked at. I did not look closely at the man. When word of this liquor went round the docks, I thought it must be some doctor of chemistry back from abroad. One old gentleman in an old shirt.”

Qiusheng lifted the pot and poured him a cup of tea.

“I have drunk your liquor.” Counsel Liang raised the teacup, smelled it and put it down again. “To be honest, of all the imported liquor I have drunk, not one bottle was cleaner. Teacher, you have a craft. But you have a problem.”

“What problem?”

“Your money can’t stand the daylight.” Counsel Liang drew the pink handkerchief from his pocket and wiped his forehead. “With a man out of work, if the money is not in the bank it is in the wardrobe. Tin boxes. Teacher, a wardrobe is for clothes. Money kept in a wardrobe will be found one day—by your wife, by a thief, by the Prohibition Bureau. And then how will you explain why an assayer out of work has thousands of dollars in cash in his wardrobe?”

Qiusheng’s hand stopped for a moment.

“What do you want?”

“I want to help you.” Counsel Liang smiled. He took a sheet of paper from the inside pocket of his suit and spread it on the table. On the sheet, line after line, were figures worked on an abacus. “This teahouse, the Nanji Teahouse. The owner is a client of mine. Business is poor and it is close to going under. He owes me three years of fees and offers me the teahouse against them. What do I want with a teahouse? I know nothing about selling tea. But a teahouse has one virtue: what comes in is customers, what goes out is spent leaves, and how much was sold in between, only the ledger knows. Look. Eighty pots of tea a day, a hundred and twenty steamers of dim sum, and at the end of a month, with the waiters paid, the singer paid, the goddess’s lamp oil paid, there is not thirty dollars left. You put your money in, as an investment. Every day the teahouse sells tea, sells dim sum, has the singer sing. The books say two hundred pots, three hundred steamers, and who is to count them? The extra takings are your clean money. I charge a fee of twenty-five per cent. Six hundred dollars a week is the most that can be washed. Any more and someone will notice.”

“Twenty-five per cent.”

“That’s the going rate.” Counsel Liang spread his hands. “Teacher, your liquor is good, but a man can’t eat good liquor. Only when the money is clean can you take it and pay school fees, see a doctor, buy your wife a new overcoat.”

Qiusheng picked up the takings sheet and read it through. The rule in Fog Harbour had always been that whatever a man was short of, that was what cost dear. He put the sheet back on the table.

“Six hundred a week. And what’s over?”

“Six hundred is six hundred.” Counsel Liang pushed the takings sheet a little towards him. “When it comes to three thousand, we draw up a receipt: Nanji Teahouse, investment, three thousand dollars exactly, in black and white, received by me, Liang Henian. If the Prohibition Bureau comes looking, what it finds is a retired civil servant who has invested in a teahouse. The part over the six hundred, Teacher, goes back in the wardrobe and waits.”

Of school fees and doctors Qiusheng had not said one word to him.

“You’ve had me looked into.”

“I have every client looked into.” Counsel Liang finished his tea, stood and straightened his suit. “That too is the going rate. Teacher, think it over. You know where to find me.”

He went to the door, laid a hand on it, and turned back.

“By the way,” he said, “an inspector from the Prohibition Bureau has been asking about White Dew lately. Name of Qiao. Do you know him?”

The hand in which Qiusheng held his teacup did not move, but a very fine ring of ripples rose on the tea in the cup.

Counsel Liang glanced at the cup of tea. He put the handkerchief back in his pocket and went downstairs. His steps sounded halfway down the stairs and stopped. Below, his voice asked the waiter for a steamer of prawn dumplings and said to put it on the account.

Qiusheng set the teacup down. The surface of the tea went flat. The waiter pushed the door open with a kettle of boiling water in his hand and asked if he should fill the pot.

“No.”

The waiter looked at the takings sheet on the table, then at him, and pulled the door to. Beyond the partition there was no more singing. Downstairs only the wireless was left, the erhu still playing the same tune. Qiusheng folded the takings sheet in four and put it in his pocket, with Boss Ding’s letter.

---

When Qiusheng came downstairs the singer was no longer on the stage. The stool was empty, the pipa leaning against its leg. Ah Yue was waiting for him by a pillar of the arcade. On the other side of the pillar was the peanut seller.

“Teacher, what did he say?”

“On the boat.”

Ah Yue went to the roundabout end to fetch the tricycle. Qiusheng stood under the arcade with the square in front of him. The temple gate was open. The smoke from the incense burner went straight up and did not scatter until it reached the eaves. Fog was coming in from the sea, and the roof ridge of the Tin Hau Temple was the first thing to grow faint.

“Peanuts, sir.” The peanut seller pushed a packet towards him. “The fog’s early this year. Other years it has to be past Mid-Autumn before you can’t see the temple roof.”

Qiusheng shook his head. The peanut seller drew the packet back and went on shelling the one in his own hand.

It was Qiusheng who had trained Qiao Zheng. Fifteen years before, Qiao Zheng had just left the technical college and been posted to the Assay Office, and he could not so much as hold a hydrometer. He put the glass tube into the measuring cylinder and let go. The tube struck the bottom of the cylinder with a clink, and he snatched his hand back, red to the roots of his ears. Qiusheng guided his hand and taught him: how to smell, how to look, how to write down the figure at the moment the thermometer stopped.

Qiao Zheng learned fast. Half a year later he was writing reports the director did not change by one word. Qiusheng remembered what he himself had said at the time: “One day that boy will be the best assayer in all Fog Harbour.”

The tricycle’s bell rang twice at the roundabout end. Qiusheng pressed his shirt pocket. The card was still there. He walked over.

---

High tide that night was at eleven. They were on the boat by nine, and before the tide was full they lifted the lids of the two vats in the forward hold and smelled each in turn. Ah Yue crouched by the vats with the week’s accounts spread on his knee, working them out. The pencil stub was very short, pinched in three fingers.

“Old Hai three hundred, the Paramount two hundred,” he said. “Take off rice, yeast, coal, take off the fish truck’s money, and there’s four hundred and seventy. Teacher, half is two hundred and thirty-five.”

“Mm.”

“Counsel Liang’s hundred and twenty. Which side does it come out of?”

“Both.”

Ah Yue crossed the figures out, worked them again, and when he had done stuck the pencil behind his ear. The tide reached the boat and the hull came level. The pencil fell from his ear, rolled to the bulkhead and rolled no further.

The hold was hot. The fire under the copper pot had just caught. Qiusheng rinsed the glass in the bucket, stood it upside down beside the pot and crouched, his eyes on the thermometer. A sweaty hand had smeared one corner of the times chalked on the bulkhead. Tonight’s line could still be read. Ah Yue leaned out of the porthole to look at the condenser. The pipe lay in the seawater, and each time a wave struck, the pipe swayed.

“Teacher, how did it go with Counsel Liang?”

“We have to pay him twenty-five per cent.”

“Twenty-five per cent!” Ah Yue jumped up from the porthole and his head struck the beam overhead. “That’s robbery! Twenty-five dollars out of every hundred. Teacher, I get twenty dollars a month at the trading house!”

“He is selling something.” Qiusheng kept his head down, watching the thermometer on the pot. “We sell liquor to the men on the docks because they have nothing else to drink. Counsel Liang sells cleanness to us because we have no other way to be clean.”

Ah Yue rubbed the top of his head, thought about it, and sat back down on the crate.

“Then what about Boss Ding’s five hundred bottles next month?” he said. “Teacher, three vats and two pots. The hold does three hundred a week at the very most. Old Hai two hundred, the Paramount a hundred, and the fifty for Saltflat were squeezed out as it was. Five hundred bottles. We can’t distil them.”

“We’ll see.”

“We’ll see, we’ll see.” Ah Yue picked the pencil up from beside the bulkhead. “Teacher, every time you say we’ll see, two days later there’s a new pot.”

Qiusheng did not take it up. He raked the fire a little more even. The mercury climbed past sixty and the mouth of the pipe began to drip, a drop, then a drop. Ah Yue watched the mouth of the pipe. His fist on his knee opened, and closed again.

“And the one named Qiao?”

Qiusheng watched the thermometer. The mercury passed seventy and slowed, climbing one mark at a time. In the hold there was only the sound of the fire, and the sound of the pipe dripping, drop by drop. Ah Yue waited. Seventy-four, seventy-six. The sea slapped once on the outside of the hull, and slapped again. Seventy-eight.

“Cut.”
