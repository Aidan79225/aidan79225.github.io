*December · Greater Snow*

In the first week of December dark came another quarter of an hour earlier than in November.

Half past five. Qiusheng was changing his shoes beside the rattan chair; at six he had to go. Two sets of footsteps came up the stairs, one heavy, one light, and stopped at the second-floor door. The knock was very light. Twice.

Qiusheng opened the door.

Ah Yue stood outside holding something wrapped in blue cloth, holding it to his chest, both hands under it. Half a step behind him stood Ah Lian, in a cotton blouse, her hair down, her cloth bundle hung on her elbow. The stairwell lamp was on the half-landing below, and by the second floor only a little yellow was left of it.

“Teacher,” said Ah Yue. He looked into the room, drew his eyes back and let them rest on the door frame. “Ah Lian’s qipao. Bought in the Old Town. It’s too big. She says it’s for the stage.” He held the blue cloth bundle out half an inch and drew it back. “Teacher’s wife is good with a needle—”

Inside, the tap was turned off. Su Qi came out of the kitchen wiping her hands on her apron. She came to the door, stood beside Qiusheng and looked at Ah Yue once, from his head to his shoes. Her nose moved. The smell on that jacket she had washed out of Qiusheng’s coat once, in October.

She did not ask who he was. She put out her hands and took the blue cloth bundle.

“Come in,” she said to Ah Lian.

Ah Lian looked at Ah Yue. Ah Yue stepped back half a pace, back to the head of the stairs.

“Ma’am, I’ll wait here,” he said. “The motor’s at the bottom of the slope.”

“What motor?”

It was Xiaoman who spoke. Her bedroom door had opened a crack, and half her face was in the crack.

“A truck,” said Ah Lian. She stopped. “Ah Yue drives a truck.”

Xiaoman opened the crack a little wider.

Su Qi had already gone to the table and undone the blue cloth. The qipao was pale grey, satin, a line of fine piping round the collar, and spread on the table it had a sheen under the lamp. She picked it up, held it to the lamp, turned it front and back, took the cloth of the side seam between her fingers and rubbed it.

“Stand here.”

Ah Lian stood under the lamp. Su Qi took the tape measure from her neck, put two pins in her mouth and measured first the shoulders, then the waist, then the hips. She pinched the tape at one figure, then at another. Ah Lian’s hands hung at her sides, and her fingers gave the cotton of her blouse a small pinch.

“Take in the waist two inches. Leave the shoulders,” said Su Qi, the pins between her teeth, the words coming out one at a time. She turned the qipao inside out and rubbed the lining between thumb and forefinger. “This lining’s too thin. It won’t hang right.”

“Mrs He, the light’s dim on the stage,” said Ah Lian. “It won’t show.”

“It will show,” said Su Qi. “The first time you sit down it will show.”

“Mrs He, if it can be altered, that will do.”

“Where are you from?”

“East Ridge,” said Ah Lian. “In the hills to the east.”

“From the hills.” Su Qi put the tape measure away, took the pins from her mouth and pinned them into the waist of the qipao, felt in her apron pocket for the tailor’s chalk and drew two lines close by the pins, one left, one right. “Three days.”

“Mrs He, how much—”

“Come for it in three days.”

At the door Ah Yue’s hand went towards his trouser pocket. Su Qi did not look at him. She folded the qipao, put it in the cloth bundle by the door and hung the tape measure back round her neck.

Qiusheng stood by the rattan chair with his shoes on and his coat over his arm. Ah Lian passed him and dipped her head.

“Teacher He.”

“Mm.”

The two of them went down the stairs, one heavy, one light. Xiaoman’s door stood open. She stood beside it watching the stairs, and only when the footsteps were gone did she turn round.

“Mama, what does that young lady do?”

“She goes on a stage.”

“What stage?”

“A stage for singing,” said Su Qi. “Homework.”

Xiaoman shut the door.

Su Qi sat down under the lamp, took up the qipao and unpicked the side seam. She did not look at Qiusheng. Qiusheng put on his coat and went out.

---

The first night she took out the lining. Once it was out the old lining went limp, and lay across her knees like a dishcloth. The second night she put in the new, a piece of slightly heavier silk lining she had brought back from the stall, sewn in stitch by stitch, the stitches hidden under the piping. The third night she took in the waist. The tailor’s chalk drew a white line on the satin and the needle went along the line. Qiusheng was there every evening. He came home at half past five and left at six, and watched her sew for a quarter of an hour. Xiaoman learned her vocabulary at the other end of the table and did not ask.

On the evening she took in the waist, Qiusheng was changing his shoes by the door. Su Qi drew the last stitch home, bit off the thread, shook the qipao out and looked it over against the lamp. The waist ran straight. The lining lay against the satin without a pucker. She folded the qipao.

Su Qi spent three evenings on it and would not take a cent. She put her needles back in their box. “Next time, you ask me yourself.”

The lid of the box closed. Qiusheng’s hand stopped on his shoelace.

She said no more. She put the qipao in the cloth bundle, stood up and went into the kitchen.

Ah Lian came for the qipao herself on the evening of the fourth day, with Ah Yue at the bottom of the slope. She put a small paper packet on the table. Su Qi pushed the packet back without opening it, took the qipao out of the cloth bundle and put it into her hands.

---

By eleven the main room of the Nanji was down to three tables.

The waiter was upending the chairs onto the empty tables one by one, legs in the air. The wireless on the counter was off. The cloth was over the birdcage and the thrush had stopped calling. The table by the stairs was empty, the bowl cleared, the top wiped, the wet marks not yet dry. On the little stage in the corner there was one lamp, and Ah Lian sat on the stool with the pipa in her arms. She was wearing the pale grey qipao. The waist was straight, and it did not pucker when she sat.

She sang the last song slowly.

“Fog comes in on the harbour, white on white, a lamp at the landing waits for my love tonight.”

Of the three tables one was listening. The other two were settling up, copper coins set out in rows on the table. She sang the four lines through, the pipa closed on its last note, and she rose and dipped her head to the room. Somebody clapped twice.

“One more,” said someone at that table.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

She slipped the pipa into its cloth cover and leaned it against the leg of the stool, went behind the board partition and changed out of the qipao, folded it and put it in the innermost layer of the cloth bundle. When she came out she was in her cotton blouse, with her hair down. The waiter was clearing the bowls from the last table.

“Miss Ah Lian, that one today,” said the waiter, and pointed his chin at the bundle.

“It’s been altered.”

“You sing sitting down, and it hangs better than it would standing.”

“Mm.”

“Which way is Ah Yue going tonight?”

“The goods yard,” she said. “He’ll be late.”

“Thick fog.”

“Mm.”

She went out by the back door. The waiter followed her to the door, waited until she was down the three stone steps, and let the bolt drop. The bolt was new, iron, and it dropped with a single sound.

It was a night in December, the fog very thick, when Ah Lian, who sang at the Nanji Teahouse, finished her last song and came out by the back door to go to the docks and wait for Ah Yue to finish work. She never got there.

The next morning a street sweeper found her beside the rubbish heap in the alley behind the Nanji. She was alive, but her face had been cut three times, and stuffed in her mouth was a little flag, a white skull on black. In her left hand she gripped a sheet of paper folded in four, gripped it very tight. The paper was an old Assay Office form, with pencil writing on the back, one large character and a line of small, all of them the same. The street sweeper could not read, and did not prise her hand open.

---

It was Ah Chang who brought the news down the stairs.

A quarter to six, not yet light. The fire in the cellar was banked, and Qiusheng was at the workbench writing down last night’s temperatures. Old Qi was on the upturned vat in the corner, his tea bowl on the floor at his left hand. Ah Chang came down from the iron door, took the fourteen steps in three jumps, the fog wet on the soles of his shoes.

“Teacher.” He stood at the foot of the stairs, panting. “Miss Ah Lian. The alley behind the Nanji. A street sweeper found her.”

Qiusheng’s pencil stopped.

“Where is she?”

“The mission hospital in Upper Town. Ah Yue’s there.” Ah Chang braced his hands on his knees a moment. “Ah Yue finished work and went to the docks to wait for her. He waited till past four and she didn’t come. We drove to the Nanji and they were just carrying her out. He went with her to the hospital and told me to bring the truck back.” He took a breath. “The waiter says, her face. Three cuts. In her mouth—”

Old Qi stood up.

“The back door.”

The tea bowl stayed on the floor. He went up the stairs.

Qiusheng put the pencil down. The chalk was at his hand, and on the wall today’s line had only the date, with nothing after it. He did not go and write the temperature. Ah Chang stood at the foot of the stairs, waiting for him to speak. Qiusheng drew the thermometer out of the vat, wiped it dry, put it back in its case and shut the lid.

“The truck?”

“Outside the iron door.”

“It doesn’t go out today.”

“The union’s two hundred—”

“Tomorrow.”

---

On the second night Qiusheng went to the hospital.

The mission hospital stood on the hill in Upper Town, red brick, the corridor very long, a lamp at each end, the smell of medicine spread from one end of the corridor to the other. A bench stood against the wall. Ah Yue sat asleep at one end of it, his head against the wall, his mouth open, his hands hanging between his knees. He still had on the jacket he had driven in that night. On the cuff was a brown mark, dry. Qiusheng stood a moment and did not wake him.

The ward was at the end of the corridor, four beds, three empty. The nurse raised her head.

“Family?”

“Her teacher.”

She drew the curtain half open and withdrew.

Ah Lian lay with her face wound in gauze from the forehead to the chin, only her two eyes showing. The eyes were open. By the bed was a stool, on the stool her cloth bundle, and on the bundle lay the sheet of paper folded in four, a little brown along the folds.

Qiusheng stood by the stool. He did not sit.

“Teacher He.”

The voice came from under the gauze, muffled, softer than it had been on the stone step in the alley.

“Mm.”

Her eyes turned to look at him. A hand came out from under the blanket, and her fingers pressed once on the edge of the paper on the bundle and let go.

“Teacher He, it doesn’t matter,” she said. She stopped a moment. “Will you still teach me the characters?”

Qiusheng looked at the paper.

“I will.”

Her eyes closed a moment and opened.

“I can write Lian now,” she said. “White Dew…” She stopped. “White Dew I have to write again.”

“Mm.”

“He hasn’t been home to sleep.” Her eyes moved a little towards the door. “Teacher He, tell him to go home and sleep.”

“Mm.”

Outside the curtain the nurse said time was up. Qiusheng put his hand on the iron rail at the foot of the bed, pressed it once, and took it away. He walked out of the ward. At the far end of the corridor Ah Yue was still asleep, his head fallen to the other side. Qiusheng walked past him, his leather shoes on the floor tiles, step by step, and Ah Yue did not wake.

Downstairs the registration window was still half open. He asked what the last few days came to, counted it out from his inside pocket and pushed it through the window. The man inside asked whose name to put on the receipt.

“No need.”

Outside the hospital door was fog. He did not wait for a bus. He walked on down the hill, through the Old Town, along the edge of the Tin Hau Temple square. The Nanji was dark, half a signboard hanging under the eaves. He went round to the alley behind.

There was no lamp in the alley. In the middle of the alley stood a man, his back to the back door, his face to the roundabout at the alley’s mouth, a film of fog on the shoulders of his railway uniform. Old Qi heard the footsteps and did not turn. Three stone steps, scrubbed, a patch whiter than the flagstones beside it, the water not yet dry. Qiusheng stopped in front of the steps. Old Qi turned round, looked at him, then looked at the back door. The door was shut, the bolt on the inside.

“Come,” said Old Qi.

The two of them walked towards the docks, one ahead and one behind, and neither spoke. Past the level crossing at the goods yard, the barrier up, the red lamp unlit.

It was Old Qi who opened the iron door at Futai. Fourteen steps, a press of the switch at the head of the steps, three lamps. Old Qi sat back down on the upturned vat in the corner.

Qiusheng walked to the workbench and looked up. On the top shelf, the tin box, the one with Assay printed on it. Since the money had been moved to the Nanji, this box had followed him down to the cellar. He reached up, took it down and put it on the workbench. He did not open the lid. He sat down on the stool with his hand on the lid.

Old Qi looked at the box, then let his eyes drop back to the tea bowl on the floor.

The fire was banked. In the cellar there was only the small sound in the vats.

---

Ah Yue kept watch at the hospital three days and nights, and on the morning of the fourth day he came back to the cellar.

He came in by the iron door at the goods-yard end, and the sound of the chain came first. He was unshaven, his eyes all bloodshot, the jacket still the same one. Old Qi was on the vat in the corner, his tea bowl lifted halfway. Qiusheng was at the workbench with a thermometer in his hand, just drawn from a vat; the tin box was at his elbow, its lid on.

Ah Yue walked up to the workbench and stopped. He did not wipe his hands on his trousers.

“Teacher,” he said, and his voice was very level, “I’m going to kill Scarface Cheng.”

Qiusheng put down the thermometer.

“Ah Yue—”

“You’re going to say it’s against the law. You’re going to say I’ll be caught. Teacher, I poured the heads away. I changed the routes. I beat the barrier. They couldn’t find me, so they went and found her.”

He stopped for a beat.

“She’s only twenty-one.”

The innermost vat made its small sound. The heart collected last night stood in its jar, not yet covered. Old Qi’s tea bowl had gone back to the floor.

“I’ll handle it,” said Qiusheng.

“You?” Ah Yue laughed, an ugly laugh. “How are you going to handle it? You’ve never so much as killed a chicken.”

“I don’t kill people,” said Qiusheng. “But I know how firewater is made.”

He opened the lid of the tin box at his elbow. Inside was not money but a stack of yellowed assay reports. Ten years of them: every batch of Black Flag firewater he had assayed, the methanol content of every bottle, where it was seized, who was carrying it, all of it here. He took the stack out and laid it on the workbench.

Ah Yue looked down. He could make nothing of the columns. He knew a place name written in one of them: behind the union.

“Qiao Zheng is looking for White Dew,” said Qiusheng. “But what he would sooner find is the firewater that killed forty-one men.”

Ah Yue looked at the stack of paper. He put out a hand and touched the edge of the top sheet, and his fingers stayed there.

“Send them to him?”

“Mm.”

“He follows these to the Black Flag,” said Ah Yue. “When he’s done with the Black Flag, he comes for us.”

“We’ll see.”

Ah Yue’s hand came away from the paper. From the corner Old Qi glanced at the door and said nothing.

---

Qiusheng did not hand the reports to Qiao Zheng directly.

At three that afternoon he went to the Nanji, carrying the pastry box he carried on Wednesdays.

Counsel Liang was in the private room upstairs, his fountain pen lying across the takings sheet. He saw the box, and the pen tapped the table once.

“Teacher, today is not Wednesday.”

Qiusheng put the box on the table and lifted the lid. Counsel Liang looked in. Paper, a yellowed stack of it, the columns printed close, the writing done by hand. His pen stopped.

“Send them to Qiao Zheng,” said Qiusheng.

Counsel Liang picked up the top sheet and read it very fast. His eyes paused at the column with the address, went on down, and stopped at the last column.

“Teacher, this stack. How many years?”

“Ten.”

“Ten years, every drum of Scarface Cheng’s.” He put the sheet back and picked up a second, a third. At the fifth he stopped reading, pulled out his handkerchief, wiped his forehead once and kept it gripped in his hand.

“Teacher, let me say this at the outset: what has gone in the post cannot be got back,” he said. “Your name is nowhere on these sheets, but every one of them is you. You trained Inspector Qiao.”

“Mm.”

“And if he recognises it?”

“We’ll see.”

Counsel Liang put the handkerchief back in his pocket, and took it out again.

“It cannot go in handwriting,” he said. “The typewriter in my chambers cannot read. It only knows keys. Nothing it has typed, not one word, can be laid at my door. The envelope says For the personal attention of Inspector Qiao Zheng. The sender is a client.”

“Which one?”

“There is no such person.” Counsel Liang shut the lid of the box and drew it over in front of him. “The Upper Town post office, Thursday morning. The stamps are on me.”

He put the fountain pen back in his pocket and stood up, the box held to his chest. At the door he looked back.

“Teacher, Thursday morning,” he said. He held the box a little tighter and went downstairs.

The reports had the addresses of the Black Flag’s four drinking dens, the recipe for the firewater, the source of the industrial spirit—a chemical supplier at the east end of the docks, whose owner was Scarface Cheng’s cousin. The lady typist’s typewriter went for a day and a half, sheet after sheet, not a word changed. The sheets with the handwriting Qiusheng took back and put in the tin box again; the typed stack went into a brown paper envelope and was posted on Thursday morning from the Upper Town post office.

---

On the fourth night after it was posted, past one o’clock, Old Qi suddenly stood up.

The fire in the cellar was burning high. He walked to the foot of the stairs and listened with his head tilted back, one hand on the wall. Qiusheng watched him. Ah Yue straightened up from beside the pot.

“Cars at the mouth of the siding,” said Old Qi. “Three. No lights.”

“The Black Flag?” Ah Yue asked.

“The Black Flag don’t turn their lights off.”

Old Qi listened. After a while the sound of the engines went past, eastward, a little way beyond Futai.

“Bank the fire,” he said.

Qiusheng shut the stove door tight.

---

The Prohibition Bureau’s cars reached the door of the chemical supplier at two in the morning.

Three of them, lights off, stopped on the side road at the east end of the docks. Qiao Zheng got down from the first, a black raincoat over his khaki uniform, the badge at his collar half covered by the raincoat. The fog lay close along the ground. The chemical supplier was a two-storey brick building, its iron door drawn shut, an enamel number beside it.

The brown paper envelope had reached the branch office three days before. The sender was a trading firm. He had sent a man to ask, and there was no such firm in Upper Town. In the envelope was a stack of typed sheets, in the Assay Office’s format: sample number, place of seizure, specific gravity, boiling range, methanol content, and in the last column, verdict.

He rapped once on the bonnet. The driver put on one lamp. He took the envelope from inside his raincoat, drew out the first sheet, held it close under the headlamp and checked it against the number by the door. The number was right. He turned to the second sheet and checked it against a line of faded sign lettering beside the iron door. Right. He kept turning, down to the column at the bottom.

That one word he saw at the first glance.

He pulled that page out and put it at the very bottom, slid the whole stack back into the envelope and the envelope back inside his raincoat. Tonight what was wanted was the address.

The doors of the two cars behind him opened. The Bureau’s agents got down, the handcuffs chinking thinly in a canvas bag. He had brought every pair of handcuffs the branch office could find, twenty-seven pairs. The leather case with the measuring cylinder was in his own hand.

The iron door was rammed open.

Inside was a sweetness, and it came pouring out of the door and drowned the fog. He had smelled that sweetness over a measuring cylinder for fifteen years; once smelled, it was never mistaken again. Tin drums stood row on row against the wall, with painted lettering on their sides: INDUSTRIAL USE. Upstairs someone was treading the floorboards. Someone ran for the back door. There were men outside the back door too.

He did not go in. He stood in the doorway, set the case on the threshold, opened it, took out the measuring cylinder and held it up to the headlamp. Not cracked.

The men were brought out one by one. The eleventh was a tall thin fellow with a scar down the left side of his face from the brow bone to the corner of the mouth, his hands cuffed behind him, his head down. The agent bringing him paused a step in front of Qiao Zheng.

“Scarface Cheng.”

Qiao Zheng looked at him once. The man did not raise his head.

He had not seen the newspapermen arrive. A magnesium flash went off, white, and caught the enamel number, the measuring cylinder and his face together.

The letter was posted on a Thursday. A week later the front page of the newspaper carried a large photograph: PROHIBITION BUREAU SMASHES FOG HARBOUR’S BIGGEST BOOTLEG RING. THREE THOUSAND BOTTLES OF FIREWATER SEIZED, TWENTY-SEVEN ARRESTED. In the photograph Qiao Zheng stood at the door of the chemical supplier with a measuring cylinder in his hand, his face grave.

Scarface Cheng was in the corner of the photograph, in handcuffs, head bowed.

---

It was Ah Yue who brought the newspaper down to the cellar. He had bought it at the fish-market gate for a cent, and read it all the way back.

Ah Yue stood holding the newspaper, his hands covered in ink. He spread the paper on the workbench and pressed his palm on the corner of the photograph, on the man with his head bowed.

“I never saw him. A year of runs, and I never saw him.” He took his palm off the newspaper. There was a black patch on it.

“Teacher, why didn’t you do this sooner?” he said.

Qiusheng folded the newspaper. He had asked himself the same question. Those reports had lain in the tin box for ten years. When he was at the Assay Office, the reports went up, the crates were sealed, and then nothing happened. Now, with the same reports, he had brought the Black Flag down in a week.

The difference was that now he wanted the Black Flag brought down.

Ah Yue wiped his hand once on his trousers. The ink came off on the trousers, and there was still some on his hand. He walked to the pot, squatted down, opened the door of the banked stove a crack and looked at the charcoal inside.

---

On the third day after the newspaper came out, Counsel Liang went down into the Futai cellar for the first time.

Past six. The big stove above was out. His motor car stood outside the side gate, and the driver had not switched off the engine. He stood at the iron door and looked down: fourteen stone steps, three lamps. The handkerchief came out of his pocket and stayed gripped in his right hand. He did not wipe his forehead. He came down a step at a time, his left hand on the wall.

Qiusheng was at the workbench. Old Qi was on the upturned vat in the corner. Ah Yue stood leaning against the pot with a rag in his hand, wiping the lid and wiping it again. At the very back of the cellar Old Lu’s three bricks lay flat on the earth.

Counsel Liang reached the bottom and stood on the last step. He came no further. He looked the cellar over: six vats, two pots, the crocks on the shelves, the lines of chalk on the wall. His eyes followed the figures and came to rest in a corner of the wall by the stairs: union, two hundred, and under it one word, Owed.

“Teacher, these stairs are steeper than the ones at the Upper Town court,” he said.

Old Qi looked at the door first, and only then spoke.

“Don’t leave the car at the side gate. Next time, the goods yard.”

Counsel Liang nodded. He walked to the workbench, and Qiusheng gave up a bench to him. He sat down, the handkerchief still in his hand.

“A man from the paper came asking who it was that gave the Black Flag away,” he said. “I stood him two pots of tea and told him nothing. Upper Town people stop asking once they have had tea enough.”

“Mm.”

“The bill at the mission hospital. Somebody has settled it,” said Counsel Liang. “The man at the registration window says a gentleman in an old suit, who wanted no receipt.”

Qiusheng looked at the notebook on the table.

“Teacher, when Upper Town people do a good deed they leave their name,” said Counsel Liang. “The one who leaves no name is afraid of being known.”

Qiusheng turned a page of the notebook.

“Scarface Cheng has been locked up some days at the branch office and will not open his mouth.” Counsel Liang shifted the handkerchief to his left hand. “Teacher, keep a man locked up long enough and he opens his mouth. When he opens his, there will be three words in his statement: Assay Office reports.”

He looked at Qiusheng.

“The sheets were done on a typewriter, but that last column, the one word, there is only one man in all Fog Harbour who writes it that way. Scarface Cheng will not know it. Inspector Qiao will,” he said. “Teacher, let me say this at the outset: you struck him with your own name, and sooner or later Qiao Zheng will recognise the handwriting.”

“I know.”

Counsel Liang looked at him a moment. The fountain pen had not come out, and there was nothing on the table to tap.

Qiusheng stood up, took a small glass down from the shelf, walked to the nearest jar, drew half a glass and came back and set it in front of Counsel Liang. Ah Yue’s rag stopped.

Counsel Liang lifted the glass, held it up under the lamp and looked at it for a while.

“Teacher, I have never drunk in front of you.”

“No hurry.”

He drank. A small mouthful, held, swallowed.

“The one that is a dollar fifty on the docks. This is it?”

“A dollar fifty.”

“The one that is twelve dollars a bottle in Upper Town, I have drunk that too.” He put the glass back on the table and the handkerchief beside the glass, and both his hands were empty.

“My first case,” he said. “I took a drinking den to court for a workman who had gone blind on firewater. I was twenty-six. The witness was his wife. The hand at the den said he had never seen the man. I did not win.” He pushed the glass a little towards the middle of the table. “Since then, no case I have taken has been too lost.”

In the cellar there was only the sound of the pot. Old Qi lifted his tea bowl, drank a mouthful and put it back at his left hand.

Counsel Liang stood up and put the handkerchief away in his pocket. He walked to the foot of the stairs and laid his hand on the wall.

“Twenty-five per cent,” he said. “From now on, make it twenty.”

“The going rate?” Qiusheng asked.

Counsel Liang did not look round. He went up one step, then another. The sound of his leather shoes reached the top, and the iron door sounded once. Ah Yue looked at what was left of the half glass, and did not go to clear it away.

---

The day before the Winter Solstice was a Sunday.

Seven in the evening. Supper was eaten and the table wiped. Su Qi was at the table rolling rice dumplings. A basin of kneaded glutinous-rice dough stood at her hand. She pinched off a piece, closed both palms on it, turned it three times, and a white one went onto the bamboo tray. There were two rows on the tray already, for the offering in the morning. Xiaoman was doing her homework at the other end of the table, her pencil scratching in the arithmetic book. Qiusheng sat in the rattan chair with his coat over his knees. He had not gone yet.

Halfway through a line she looked up.

Xiaoman asked, “Big brother Ah Yue who drives the truck, can we have him round for dumplings at the solstice?” Su Qi was rolling the rice dumplings and did not look up.

The one in her hands finished its three turns and went onto the bamboo tray.

“Have him round.”

Xiaoman bent her head and went on writing.

Qiusheng looked at the two white rows on the bamboo tray. He picked up his coat and put it on.

“Six o’clock,” said Su Qi. Her fingers pinched off another piece.

That night in the cellar, when Ah Yue came back from the goods yard, Qiusheng said one thing from the workbench.

“Tomorrow evening. South Slope.”

“Teacher?”

“Dumplings.”

Ah Yue stood at the foot of the stairs. He did not say he would come, and he did not say he would not.

Old Qi spoke from the corner. “Tomorrow night I ride with the truck.”

Ah Yue went up the stairs and locked the truck.

Qiusheng opened the notebook and wrote down tonight’s date and the number of the vat, and before the reading the same two words as ever: rice wine. The tear-off calendar on the wall was turned to the page for the twenty-first, with tomorrow’s solar term printed at the bottom. He closed the notebook and put it under the thermometer case.

---

On the day of the Winter Solstice Su Qi came back from the market at half past five.

The market basket in one hand and the cloth bundle in the other, she scraped her shoes at the door. Qiusheng was in the rattan chair with the newspaper spread on his knees. Xiaoman was in the kitchen washing rice. Su Qi put the cloth bundle on the chair by the door. The basket was still in her hand. She had not put it down.

“They’re all talking about a spirit called ‘White Dew’. They say a schoolteacher makes it.”

Having said it, she carried the basket into the kitchen. The tap began to run. Qiusheng turned a page of the newspaper.

At five to six, one set of footsteps on the stairs, stopping at the second floor, and two knocks on the door. Xiaoman went to open it.

Ah Yue stood at the door, shaved, his collar buttoned to the top. In his hand was a paper parcel tied with hemp string, two catties or so. He took off his shoes at the door, put them together and set them beside the door. The shoes had been brushed. The toes were still wet.

“Ma’am,” he said towards the kitchen. Then, turning to the rattan chair, “Teacher.”

“Come in and sit down.” Su Qi came out of the kitchen wiping her hands on her apron. She looked at the parcel.

“Sesame.” Ah Yue held the parcel out. “From the rice merchant on the docks. New crop.”

Su Qi took it, weighed it in her hand and carried it into the kitchen. In a while there was a sound in the pan, sesame jumping in a dry pan, and a toasted smell came out of the kitchen door. Ah Yue went and stood by the kitchen door.

“Ma’am, let me grind it.”

“Sit down,” said Su Qi. Inside, the mortar began to sound, stroke after stroke.

Xiaoman set out the bowls, four places.

The table stood against the balcony door. Ah Yue sat on the side facing the balcony door; Su Qi gave him the place, saying it was the guest’s. Qiusheng had his back to the front door; Xiaoman sat next to Ah Yue, her stool moved over a little; Su Qi was by the kitchen door. On the table were a basin of kneaded glutinous-rice dough, a bowl of toasted ground sesame mixed with sugar, a saucer of ground peanuts, and an empty bamboo tray. The dumplings were to be rolled first, and supper after.

Su Qi spoke first. “Roll.”

Ah Yue had turned up in a clean jacket. He showed Xiaoman how to roll the dumplings round.

He pinched off a piece of dough, put it in Xiaoman’s palm, laid his own palm over it and took her hand round with his.

“Palm flat. Don’t use your fingers. Press with a finger and it dents,” he said. “It’s like stacking sacks. Keep your hands loose and the thing comes out round.”

“Sacks are square,” said Xiaoman.

“Stack a hundred and they’re round.”

Xiaoman gave a laugh and rolled her own. The first was flat. The second split along a seam and the sesame leaked out. The third was round. Ah Yue rolled fast, one after another, all of a size, lined up on the bamboo tray as if they had been measured. Su Qi sat on the side by the kitchen door putting in the filling, both hands never stopping, and her eyes rested a moment on Ah Yue’s hands. Those hands: thick knuckles, a layer of callus on the palms, an old scar along the outside of the thumb. Hands that carried cargo, not hands that checked it. She pressed a lump of dough flat, spooned in sesame and closed it up.

“Big brother Ah Yue, were you Papa’s student at the night school?”

“I was.”

“The one who’s better than me at algebra?”

Ah Yue glanced at Qiusheng. Qiusheng was rolling a dumpling and did not look up.

“Teacher said that?” Ah Yue asked.

“Papa says algebra is clever,” said Xiaoman. “I say algebra doesn’t count.”

“Teacher says the same about me,” said Ah Yue. He put a finished one on the bamboo tray. “And I say it doesn’t count either.”

“Papa, your turn.”

Xiaoman had on last year’s dark blue winter uniform. The sleeves were a length too short and her wrists showed, and halfway through the rolling she pushed the sleeves up further still. Su Qi glanced at that length of wrist. The dumpling in Qiusheng’s hands stopped a moment, and turned another half turn before it went onto the bamboo tray. A size too big, dented on one side. Xiaoman moved it to the corner of the tray.

“Papa’s goes in last.”

Xiaoman took off the uniform jacket, patted the flour from its sleeves and hung it over the back of her chair, the sleeves hanging down.

The water boiled. The dumplings went in, sank, and floated up one by one, white. Su Qi ladled out four bowls, in sugar water, six to a bowl. In Ah Yue’s bowl there were seven; she had ladled him one more. Qiusheng’s dented one was in Xiaoman’s bowl.

Xiaoman talked about school. The normal school examination was in June. On Sundays she went to the clerk for English, twenty cents a week, and the clerk said her *th* had come right. The Chinese teacher had a new pair of glasses, worn on a string round his neck, and when he bent to read out the lesson the glasses hung swinging in mid-air, and the whole class watched the glasses and nobody looked at the book.

Su Qi laughed.

“Next Mid-Autumn we’ll buy two more pomelos,” she said.

“What have pomelos to do with glasses?” Xiaoman asked.

“More people.”

Ah Yue bent his head and ate his dumplings.

“The school play’s over,” said Xiaoman, her chopsticks pushing the dumplings about in her bowl. “Our teacher said Scene Three stood best of anyone.”

Nobody took it up. Ah Yue raised his head, glanced at Qiusheng and lowered it again. Su Qi got up to ladle more soup.

“Big brother Ah Yue,” said Xiaoman, “what about that young lady?”

Ah Yue put his chopsticks together and laid them on the table.

“After the New Year,” he said, “she goes back to the hills.”

Su Qi set the bowl back in front of him. “Eat.”

Ah Yue ate. He ate all seven and drank the sugar water to the last drop.

“Big brother Ah Yue, is a truck easy to drive?”

“Easy,” said Ah Yue. “Three days to learn. I hit two posts.”

“Papa can’t drive.”

“Teacher doesn’t need to.”

At half past eight he left. He put on his shoes at the door and tied the laces twice. Su Qi divided off half of the sesame that was left, wrapped it in paper and pushed it into his hands. He made to give it back. Her hands had already drawn away. He stood at the door with the packet held to his chest.

“Ma’am, Teacher’s cough. I’m keeping an eye on it.”

Su Qi looked at him.

“The slope is slippery,” she said.

He went down the stairs. Heavy steps, one after another, to the bottom, and the street door sounded once.

Qiusheng took his coat from behind the door. Su Qi did not look at him.

“What time?”

“Daybreak.”

He went. The door shut.

Xiaoman cleared the bowls and went into the kitchen. Su Qi cleared the table. Two raw ones were left on the bamboo tray, and she covered them with a bowl. Over the back of the chair hung the uniform, the dark blue one, last year’s. She picked it up, shook out one sleeve, laid it against the edge of the table and measured with her fingers to the cuff.

“Too short.”

She folded the uniform and put it in the cloth bundle by the door. In the bundle there were two other pieces, other people’s.
