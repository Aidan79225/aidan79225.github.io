*10 April · Fog*

On the tenth of April Fog Harbour had the thickest fog of that year. The fog had come ashore the night before, and in the small hours another layer rolled in from the sea; by four the red lamp at the goods yard was no more than a point, and a man could put out his hand and touch the wall of Warehouse No. 7 and not see it.

The Prohibition Bureau’s great sweep began at four in the morning. Twelve patrol cars went out at once and sealed off the ring road in Upper Town, the slope of South Slope, the goods yard on the docks, the dirt road at Saltflat and the Old Town roundabout. On the docks the first thing to sound was the hand-cranked siren at the door of the branch office, wound up turn by turn, drawn out long and falling; under the siren lay the horns of the patrol cars, coming from the goods-yard end, one car after another. The foghorn on the breakwater still sounded once a minute. The siren crowded in between, the two sounds lay one on the other, and there was no telling which came first. The workers asleep in the warehouses sat up, pulled on their singlets and went to the doors, and in the fog there were only headlamps going by, one ball of light after another.

The cellar of the Futai Soy Works was the first to be searched, and the branch chief led the party. The side gate was not locked. The dog in the yard barked twice and stopped. The foreman came out of the works building, a lined coat thrown over his short jacket, bean pulp on the bottoms of his trousers. The branch chief asked about the cellar. “The bookkeeper has the key,” said the foreman. “I’ve never been down.” The branch chief asked him nothing more. The iron door was locked, the lock was prised off, and the wooden sign fell from its nail and landed on the flagstones with the writing down. Fourteen stone steps, the switch at the head of them pressed, three lamps. The Bureau men rushed down the stairs, and the cellar was bare: the vats washed clean and upturned on the floor, the pot wiped till it shone, the crocks standing row on row on the shelves, every mouth empty. There was no tube on the pot. The bottom of the stone trough was still wet. On the wall by the stairs was a row of chalk writing, from October to March, a line a day, a date and a temperature; under that row a patch had gone white, rubbed over. The agents knocked under the vats, shone a light behind the shelves, felt about in the stone trough and came up with a brush. An agent picked up the wooden sign at the head of the stairs, turned it over, looked at it, and put it down again. At the very back of the cellar four bricks were missing from the brick floor and a patch of earth showed, trodden flat, darker than the bricks beside it. No one went to look at it.

On the workbench a tin box, open, the lid upside down beside it. In the box a stack of yellowed paper, the top sheet with its columns printed close, the fountain-pen writing stroke by stroke, and in the verdict column one word: Poisonous. On the brick wall above the box a sheet of paper was pasted, paste at the four corners, one corner lifting, the writing in the same hand as the sheet in the box: All spirit made at this still has been sold. For the process, apply to Inspector Qiao Zheng, Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau. The branch chief read the sheet out once by the lamp, and did not read it a second time. He turned over two pages of the stack in the box, put the lid on and handed the box to the agent beside him. “Back to the branch office. On Inspector Qiao’s desk,” he said. The agent went up the stairs with the box in his arms, fourteen steps, and the box knocked once against his chest.

At six the wireless station came on the air. The first item of news said that this was the largest operation since the Temperance Act came into force, and that its target was every bootleg still in Fog Harbour.

---

The cold store was the second, and it was not the branch chief who led the party but Qiao Zheng. He carried a search warrant issued by the court, dated late on the night of 9 April, and the judge who had signed it was an old schoolmate of Counsel Liang’s.

Two cars, lights off, stopped where the railway broke off. Twenty past four. The row of trucks at the gate stood dark, the wire-netting gate was shut, and beside it stood two doormen in long gowns, one on the left and one on the right, each carrying a storm lantern. The fog pressed the lamplight into two balls. Qiao Zheng got down from the first car. Khaki uniform, a black raincoat over it, the right sleeve empty, pinned at the shoulder; the plaster on his right hand came up to the elbow and hung across his chest in a cloth sling, and the fingers showed at the end of the plaster. Six agents got down from the car behind, the handcuffs chinking in a canvas bag.

The doorman on the right spoke first. “Madam Wen has given orders. No one is to go in.”

Qiao Zheng handed him the search warrant and said nothing. With his left hand.

The doorman took it and brought the storm lantern close. He looked at the seal on the paper, the lantern moved a little towards the hand Qiao Zheng had in the sling, and he turned his head to look at the one on the left. The one on the left drew the gate open a crack. The agents pushed the gate all the way back, and the wire netting rattled for a while in the fog. The doorman on the right handed the search warrant back, turned and walked in, walked fast, and only when he reached the iron-sheathed door did he break into a run. Qiao Zheng did not call him back.

The iron-sheathed door stood open. Half the lamps in the freezing room were lit, and the fish on the iron hooks hung row on row, white with frost. The cold struck his face, and the fingers in the plaster went cold first. He walked through, not fast. At the far end of the freezing room was the iron railing, behind the railing an opening, the spiral stair rust red, going down turn after turn.

Three layers of smell. The letter had said so. The first turn, ammonia, his eyes stinging, the compressor running, the whole stair shaking with it; the second turn, coal, hot, the stoker standing at the mouth of the boiler with his shovel stopped in mid-air, and one agent stayed beside him; further down, the iron door stood open a crack.

He pushed the door open. The smell of spirit.

Twenty stainless-steel fermenting tanks, four distilling columns two storeys high, a whole wall of thermometers and pressure gauges. The first column was running, every section of its body hot, and under the condenser at the head of the column a thread fell into the collecting tank without a break. The lamps were white. Beside the column, against the wall, some dozens of crates of square bottles were stacked, mouths up, the stag label already on them, the bottles empty.

Ye Mingde stood beside the first column, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, a small glass in his hand, the glass half full. He saw Qiao Zheng, and what he looked at first was the hand in the sling. He did not run, and he did not speak. He did not put the glass down.

Three agents came in and held the iron door, and two followed behind Qiao Zheng. The workers on the cold store’s night shift came crowding down the spiral stair, four or five of them, and crowded outside the iron door, not daring to come in. The doorman who had run in stood just inside the iron door, his back flat to the wall. One of the agents following glanced at the column that was running.

“Inspector, do we shut it down?”

“Leave it.”

Qiao Zheng went to the workbench. A white porcelain top, a spirit lamp, a distilling flask, a row of measuring cylinders, a hydrometer lying on velvet, and at the top of its glass tube a bubble, very small. That one he did not touch. From the left pocket of his uniform he took a velvet-lined case, put it on the bench and opened it. The hydrometer. The glass tube had been in use twelve years, and there was not one bubble in it. The thermometer was in the other pocket. He took that out too and laid it alongside.

He went to the collecting tank. The spirit in the tank had reached the first mark. He caught half a small glass and held it up under the lamp. What was in the glass was as clear as every bottle of firewater he had assayed these fifteen years.

He did not drink it. An assayer does not drink.

He put the glass on the bench, lowered his head, brought his nose to the rim and wafted lightly once with his palm. The sweetness of the rice. Under it a thread of cool. Under that, he wafted a second time, and nothing came up.

He poured a measuring cylinder half full and lowered the hydrometer in. The glass tube bobbed twice on the surface and held. He bent to read the scale. A young agent craned his neck to look with him. The man beside him tugged his sleeve, and he stood straight. Qiao Zheng called out a figure. No one wrote it down. He kept it in his head.

Spirit went into the distilling flask, the stopper in, the thermometer through the hole in the stopper, the mouth of the tube over a clean measuring cylinder. He held the matchbox between the fingers that showed at the end of the plaster, drew a match with his left hand, and struck it. The first one broke. The second caught. He used Ye Mingde’s spirit lamp from the workbench, and the wick went blue at a touch. He moved the flame under the flask.

Everyone in the basement was watching him. The Bureau’s agents, the workers outside the iron door, Ye Mingde by the column. The needle at the head of the column trembled, and the thread went on falling into the collecting tank. The mercury climbed, slowly. Fifty, fifty-five. By the wall the doorman shifted to his other foot. Outside the iron door one of the workers squatted down, and stood up again.

Upstairs the compressor reached the end of its run and stopped. The hum was gone, and the spiral stair no longer shook. The basement went quiet, so quiet that the first drop could be heard falling to the bottom of the measuring cylinder.

There were heels on the spiral stair. Down step by step, not fast. The jade knocking on the rail, once, and again. The workers outside the iron door drew back to either side. The heels reached the iron door, came in, stopped on the last step and came no further. The jade knocked on the rail one last time. The bookkeeper was two steps behind her, the book held to his chest, its corners bound in brass.

The doorman looked at her, not at Qiao Zheng. Qiao Zheng did not look up.

She wore a black qipao, and the jade at her wrist showed green under the lamps. Her eyes came to rest on the column, stayed a moment, and then came to rest on the glass in Ye Mingde’s hand.

The bookkeeper did not open his book. Sixty, sixty-three. Qiao Zheng watched the mercury in the thermometer climb to sixty-four and a half degrees, stop, and climb again. In the measuring cylinder the oily sheen did not appear.

In the beat that the mercury stopped, Ye Mingde’s Adam’s apple moved. Madam Wen’s eyes left the glass and came to rest on Qiao Zheng’s face.

Qiao Zheng raised his head and looked at Ye Mingde. “You cut the heads.”

Ye Mingde put down the glass in his hand. He nodded.

“Seventy-eight degrees,” he said. “Cut too much rather than too little.”

He pushed up his spectacles. “Teacher said so.”

Qiao Zheng’s left hand stopped a moment by the measuring cylinder. In the basement there were only the extractor fans, and the sound of the thread falling into the collecting tank. He took his hand away. The mercury passed seventy-eight, and he let it go on climbing, climbing as far as it would go, and only then moved the flame away.

The sheet for the search record lay on the bench. He wrote with his left hand. Sample number, place of seizure, specific gravity, boiling range, methanol content. The writing was crooked, each stroke separate, the last stroke of the Zheng dragged out too long. The workers outside the iron door could not see the paper, and they looked too. Ye Mingde looked. The doorman looked at her, and she looked at the paper. In the last column, the verdict, he wrote one word.

Drinkable.

Outside the iron door someone let out a breath. Qiao Zheng put down the pen. In the corner there was another crate of square bottles, sealed, the labels on, the spirit in them caramel-coloured. “That crate,” he said. An agent brought a bottle over. Qiao Zheng drew the stopper and smelled: caramel, water steeped in wood chips, and under them, clean, a thread of cool. The same lamp, the same distilling flask, another measuring cylinder. Half an hour. Sixty-four and a half degrees, a stop, and on. No oily sheen. On the record he began a new line, sample number, Stag, the verdict column, and again wrote that one word. Madam Wen stood all the while on the last step.

He drew another sheet towards him and wrote the seizure list. The tanks, one by one. He had written as far as the fourteenth when there were footsteps on the spiral stair again, hurried, two steps at a time. An agent came down with a tin box in his arms, and printed on the box was one faded word.

“Inspector, from Futai. The branch chief said to put it on your desk, and the doorman at the branch office said you were here and told me to bring it.” He put the box on the workbench. “There was a sheet of paper pasted on the wall. It had your name on it.”

Qiao Zheng lifted the lid. A stack of yellowed paper. The top sheet: the columns, the drinking den behind the union, seven per cent, one word in the verdict column, a signature below. He glanced at it, put the lid back on, and pressed his palm on the lid.

“One thing more,” said the agent. “The doorman at the branch office took a trunk call from the provincial capital, at seven. The other end said it was done, and rang off.”

Qiao Zheng took his hand from the lid of the box. Then he turned to Madam Wen.

“Mrs Wen Fengyi,” he said, “you are suspected of contravening Sections Three and Seven of the Temperance Act, and of bribing a public servant. The Director-General was taken into custody in the provincial capital this morning. I must ask you to come with us.”

Madam Wen looked at him for a long time, and then she smiled. She straightened the cuffs of her qipao, the left, the right, until they lay quite flat. “Inspector Qiao,” she said, “next month the Temperance Act will be repealed.”

“I know,” said Qiao Zheng. “It hasn’t been repealed yet. Not today.”

She came down off the last step and walked past the first row of fermenting tanks, her heels sounding on the terrazzo floor one by one. No one went to take her arm. She walked to the workbench, passed Ye Mingde, and stopped. She glanced at the record on the bench, the left-handed writing, two lines, the same one word. She slipped the jade off her wrist and put it on the workbench, beside the measuring cylinder. It rang once on the white porcelain.

“Your pay.”

Ye Mingde stood where he was. She walked to the iron door. The doorman followed behind. On the stair the bookkeeper turned sideways to let her pass, then followed. Two agents came last. The heels went up the spiral stair, step by step, and nothing sounded on the rail again.

Ye Mingde did not touch the string of jade. He went to the column and shut the brass valve. The heat went out of the column from the bottom, section by section. The wall of needles, one by one, slowed and stopped.

Qiao Zheng tucked the tin box under the right arm in its sling and took the record in his other hand. He went up the spiral stair, the boiler banked, the ammonia still there, the frost in the freezing room still there. Of the two storm lanterns by the wire-netting gate, one had gone out. The fog had not lifted.

---

And to tell of noon that day: the fog lifted. Out by the breakwater the sound that came once a minute stopped. At the Old Town roundabout the newsboy was crying “Extra!”, a cent a sheet, and the cry was one line: SHIPPING QUEEN WEN FENGYI TAKEN IN BY THE PROHIBITION BUREAU; COLD STORE SEALED. Many were buying, and the newsboy’s voice had already gone hoarse.

Qiao Zheng went to the docks. He did not go back to the branch office. From the cold store he walked west along the railway, past the Lu crew’s iron pot, past the Futai siding. A branch office car stood at the mouth of the siding, the side gate was open, and inside men were carrying vats. He did not turn in. He stood at the corner of Warehouse No. 7, the tin box still under his right arm. A train went slowly by on the railway, and the barrier at the level crossing came down and went up again. The noon sun lay on the gravel, and the gravel was dry. Under the cranes a row of workers sat eating their midday meal, lunch tins on their knees. One of them, a young one, his right arm hanging at his side, the elbow not straight, brought a small bottle out from inside his shirt, gripped it between his knees and drew the stopper with his left hand. He took a mouthful and passed it to the man beside him. That man drank and passed it on.

There was no label on the bottle.

Qiao Zheng walked over. The row of men saw the uniform, and the chopsticks stopped over the lunch tins. The bottle had reached the fourth man’s hands, and there it stopped. Qiao Zheng stood in front of the young man.

“What is this?”

The young man looked at his uniform, then at his face, and lowered his head. “White Dew,” he said. “The last batch. Teacher says there won’t be any more.”

“When was it delivered?”

“Before daylight,” said the young man, his head down. “Twenty crates, behind the union.”

“Where is Teacher?”

The young man shook his head. At the end of the row someone said the hills, someone said a boat. No one could say for certain. The young man wiped his left hand once on his knee, looked up at Qiao Zheng, and looked down again.

“Teacher,” he said. He stopped, and began again. “Half a year we’ve drunk it, and no one on the docks has gone blind since.”

The old one at the end of the row spoke. “Inspector, this bottle. Is it against the law?”

“Today it is,” said Qiao Zheng. He did not put out his hand.

The bottle came back from the fourth man’s hands, hand to hand, to the young man. The young man gripped it between his knees again, put the stopper in with his left hand, pressed it tight, and put the bottle back inside his shirt. The people of the docks had poor memories, but the taste of a liquor they remembered.

The red lamp at the level crossing began to flash. The barrier came down. On the far side of the railway, beyond the barrier, stood an old man. Not tall, a little stooped, a railway uniform washed nearly white, buttoned to the top. The one from the table by the stairs at the Nanji. His hands hung at his sides, and there was no tea bowl in them. He looked at Qiao Zheng across the railway, looked a moment, and nodded once. Qiao Zheng looked at him. The whistle came, and the engine passed between the two of them, one wagon, two, the wagons joining into a wall. When the train had gone by the barrier went up, and there was no one on the far side.

From the left pocket of his uniform Qiao Zheng took a folded paper. The page torn from the report counterfoils in February, folded in four, the creases pressed very flat. He pinched a corner of it in the fingers that showed at the end of the plaster and opened it with his left hand. A line through the word Fail, beside it one word, in another pen. On the ‘Checked by’ line a signature. He looked at it for a long time. Then he tore the paper in two, and in four, and threw it into the sea.

In May Parliament voted, and the Temperance Act was repealed. Overnight every bar in Fog Harbour opened its doors. The hotels in Upper Town hung out signs for imported liquor, and the Fog Harbour Daily carried a half-page advertisement on its second page, a stag, and under it one line: Stag Whisky, three dollars a bottle. It was the stag that hung at the door of the Paramount, the bottles still the same batch from the factory in the South Seas, square, the labels on very straight. The floor manager stood at the door counting the customers, his book under his arm; when a customer asked for White Dew he said there was none, the Paramount sold imported liquor. Boss Ding stood half a pace in front of the floor manager, white suit, gold watch, and for each customer who came in he made a little bow. In the trade of Upper Town the signboard changed every year, and the face that met the customers at the door did not.

The cold store was sealed. Two seals across the wire-netting gate, the branch’s, dated 10 April; the row of trucks at the gate drove off one by one until two were left, their tyres flat. In the basement the four columns stood cold and the twenty tanks stood empty. The string of jade was on the workbench, beside the measuring cylinder. It was inside when the seals went on, and it had a line to itself on the seizure list. Wen & Co.’s sugar ship came into harbour in May and the molasses was unloaded on the docks. No one came to haul it away, and in the sun a drum split down one side.

Ye Mingde went unpaid. In the middle of May he packed up, one leather case, one annual report with its blue cloth cover faded to grey, and from the gate of the cold store walked west along the railway, past the Futai siding, to the red-brick building behind Warehouse No. 3. Old Hai was on the threshold.

“Sir,” said Ye Mingde, pushing up his spectacles, “I can calculate, I can’t taste. A column I can run, a vat I have never touched. Does the union want a man like that.”

Old Hai looked at him a moment with his one eye, then at the book under his arm. “Calculating will do,” he said. “For tasting, the docks have a thousand mouths.”

The co-operative opened on the patch of gravel behind the union’s warehouse, where the offering tables had stood at Qingming. One copper pot, bought by weight from a bankrupt sauce works in the Old Town; three coarse earthenware vats, from Saltflat. The ones who made the spirit were the five boys Ah Yue had left behind: the four who had done runs with Old Qi, and Ah Shui. Old Hai spread the four sheets from his cigarette tin in front of Ye Mingde. Ye Mingde read them through once and said he could follow them. Ah Shui washed the bottles, with one hand, and stood them upside down on a board one by one; Ye Mingde squatted beside him counting, counted to forty-two, and said that was enough for a vat. The first vat he distilled himself. The thermometer was set in the lid of the pot. He kept by the pot and did not move, and collected only once it was past seventy-eight. Old Hai drank a mouthful, did not frown, and put the bowl down.

“This one the docks will know,” he said.

The bottles were soda bottles and the paper label was printed, two words: White Dew. Beside the two words a drop of water was printed, the drop Old Hai had drawn, cut by the printer after his drawing. Old Hai pasted the forty-one sheets of red paper on the wall of the co-operative, one against the next, in a row. Above the red paper a receipt was nailed, back outwards, a single drop of dew drawn in pencil; on the wall beside it another drop was drawn in chalk, a big one. The workers, when they bought, looked first at the row of red paper and then paid. A dollar fifty a bottle. Old Lu came and bought once, one bottle, paid cash, and carried it off.

No one knew who had registered the trade mark. In the register of trade marks the applicant’s column read “Nanji Teahouse”, and the agent was Liang Henian, barrister. The description of the mark read: Made by the old method, heads and tails cut away; methanol content nil. One half of the surplus each month to be donated to the Fog Harbour Dockworkers’ Mutual Aid Society. What was registered in April was tea. In May it was changed to liquor, and the line that changed it Counsel Liang wrote with his own hand; when he had written it he put the fountain pen back in his pocket, and the handkerchief did not come out. The co-operative’s accounts went through the teahouse, and in the column for the fee Counsel Liang wrote nought.

In June the sign of the Fog Harbour branch of the Prohibition Bureau was taken down. The white-painted sign nailed underneath it showed again, black on white, Customs Assay Office, the paint still good; someone had touched up one stroke of the character for assay, the new stroke laid over the old, a little blacker than the strokes beside it. Qiao Zheng went back to work beneath that sign. In at the door at half past seven, and the doorman was still the same one. The assay room faced the sea. Three benches in a row, and Young Zhou on the high stool alongside. The coat went on the second peg. The third peg stayed empty. The drawer slid open, the velvet-lined case opened, once over with the chamois, and the glass tube held up to the window. In June there was no fog, and the light was bright.

The side gate at Futai was locked, and the lock was the branch’s. The men in the sauce-vat yard had a new owner and lifted the bamboo lids at four the same as ever. Every Sunday afternoon a northerner stripped to the waist stood at the side gate, a towel round his neck, stood a quarter of an hour, said nothing, and turned and went back to the east end of the goods yard.

Many years later, when the old people of Fog Harbour spoke of that year, they would always come round to a teacher. Some said he was an assayer, some said he was a chemist, some said he was a man with a disease of the lungs who wanted to do one good thing before he died. Some said he lived to a great age in the sanatorium in the hills and watched the fog from his window every morning; some said he got on a boat and went somewhere very far away, and opened a little still there. The people of the docks did not argue with them. They called that liquor White Dew, and they call it that to this day.

---

In September, on the day of White Dew, the primary school in Saltflat Village held its opening ceremony for the new term. The school stood on the high ground of the sandspit, one classroom, one flag, and the open ground in front of the classroom where the nets were dried was the playground. The nets had been taken in, and their print was still on the ground. The benches had been carried out of the classroom and set in four rows, the parents sitting in front, the children behind, and those who could not find a seat standing. The school had only the one schoolmaster, thirty or so, in a long gown, the cuffs worn white, and he taught every year from the third to the sixth. He stood on the steps. Since the spring Xiaoman had taken the English lesson for him, and today he had asked her back to say a few words.

Xiaoman wore the uniform of the normal school, white blouse, black skirt, and stood beside the schoolmaster. The uniform was issued by the school. The sleeves had been half an inch too long, and Su Qi had taken them up last week. On ordinary days Xiaoman lived in the normal school’s dormitory and came back by the ferry on Saturdays. Today she had come back for this. She spoke fast.

“I sat in the back row of this classroom. I sat there half a year. From the back row you can see the back of everyone’s head. The schoolmaster can’t see who is dozing. I can.”

The children below laughed. She did not wait for the laughing to finish and went on. “English isn’t hard. What’s hard is learning it by heart every day. Begin your words at A. When you’ve learned a page, turn down the corner. When the whole book is corners, you know it.” She stopped a moment. “The schoolmaster says my English is better than his. He isn’t wrong.”

Beside her the schoolmaster nodded, and below they laughed again. Xiaoman had passed into the normal school. The deposit and the school fees came every month from Liang Henian’s chambers, and never missed a month. The envelope was printed with the Nanji’s name, and written on it was the word dividend.

Su Qi stood at the edge of the playground, at the classroom end, the tape measure still round her neck. By the ferry landing she altered clothes for people, twenty cents a piece, and the village women brought their old things to be let down; she had been halfway through one this morning, and had put it down and come. She watched Xiaoman on the steps. Xiaoman spoke without notes, her textbook gripped in her hand, unopened, and stood without moving.

Headman Ah Lu sat in the front row of the parents’ benches, his bamboo hat on his knees, and beside the hat a parcel, brown paper, tied with hemp string. The front row was all old people, and the headman had one leg stretched out straight.

At the end of the back row of benches sat a man who was not from the village. A white shirt, no badge at the collar, both hands on his knees, the left hand over the right, the fingers of the right hand not opened out. His hair was cut short and he sat straight. Su Qi glanced at him across the playground. The one who brings the pomelos. He was not looking at her. He was looking at Xiaoman on the steps.

The ceremony was over, and the children scattered from the benches and ran for the classroom. The schoolmaster came over and said, Mrs He, Xiaoman spoke well.

“She spoke fast,” said Su Qi.

The schoolmaster laughed and went back into the classroom. Headman Ah Lu got up, the parcel in one hand, the other hand holding his bamboo hat to his chest, and came slowly over until he stood in front of Su Qi.

“Mrs He,” he said. “At the ferry landing… the postman left it.”

Su Qi took it. The parcel was not heavy, and there was something hard inside it. She glanced at the postmark, two words, East Ridge.

“And a book,” said the headman. From under the bamboo hat he brought out a book, in hard covers, new, twice as thick as the textbook in Xiaoman’s hand. “That gentleman… asked me to give it to Xiaoman. He said there was no need to mention him.”

Su Qi looked up. At the far end of the playground the back row of benches was empty. The man stood at the top of the stone steps of the ferry landing, his back to the sea, looking this way. Su Qi nodded once across the playground. He nodded once too, turned, and went down the stone steps. The ferry’s awning was green, and the ferryman was in the bows taking in the mooring rope.

Xiaoman came out of the classroom, saw the book, took it and turned to the flyleaf. On the flyleaf was one line: For Xiaoman, to keep. Godfather. The writing was very upright, each stroke separate, a size larger than in last year’s book. She looked towards the ferry landing.

“Mama, Godfather was sitting in the back row.”

“I saw.”

“Why doesn’t he come over.”

“The ferry is leaving,” said Su Qi. “Keep the dictionary safe.”

The ferry left the stone steps. Xiaoman stood at the edge of the playground hugging the dictionary. Su Qi watched the boat go out and round the tip of the sandspit. The tide was out. The mudflats lay spread wide, black, shining like oil. An old fishing boat that had lain on its side on the flats was gone. The village people said that on the night of a spring tide in April someone had taken it out, and no one had seen who. Since then Headman Ah Lu had set no one to watch the boat.

---

The goose at Grandmother’s lay by the threshold and would not move for anyone. Su Qi stepped over it, put the parcel on the table and undid the hemp string.

Inside the brown paper was rice straw, and in the straw a bottle. A soda bottle, the trade mark scraped off, no label. Round the neck of the bottle a white thread was tied, cotton, three turns and a knot; the end of the thread ran through a sewing needle, and the needle was stuck into the side of the cork, its eye end outwards. A slip of paper hung from the neck too, tied on with the other end of the thread, the writing crooked:

“Mrs He: like Teacher told me, it reaches you on White Dew. He says open it at Frost’s Descent. I poured the heads away. Ah Yue.”

A needle, with a thread through its eye. The one she had drawn on the crock in tailor’s chalk, and this time it was real.

Su Qi held the bottle up to the window. The sun came in aslant and shone into the bottle. A layer of fine beads clung to the inside of the glass, like dew on blades of grass in the morning. She put out her hand to draw the needle. Her fingers took hold of the eye end and stopped a moment. The needle came out, and left a small hole in the cork. She drew the cork, brought the mouth of the bottle up under her nose, did not waft, and smelled it just as it was. The sweetness of the rice on the outside, the sorghum inside holding it up, and at the very bottom a layer that was very thin, and she could smell what it was—the lining. Seven parts, two, less than one.

She pushed the cork back in and pressed it tight. The needle she stuck back into the hole it had left in the side of the cork, the thread still tied to the neck of the bottle. She did not drink it.

Xiaoman was in the doorway, the dictionary hugged to her chest, the goose at her feet. “Mama, what is that?”

“Big brother Ah Yue sent it.”

“Who for?”

“For me,” said Su Qi. “Go and shoo the goose away.”

Xiaoman went to shoo the goose. The goose would not go, and so she stood there too, turning the pages of the dictionary.

The cupboard stood by the bed, wooden, a brass ring on its door. In the cupboard hung a pale grey qipao, its waist taken in by her own hand; under the qipao lay folded a dark blue winter uniform, last year’s, the cuff half unpicked, and under the uniform lay the few lines. What had come in the parcel the ferry brought over in April she had put in here piece by piece, and had not taken out again. She put the bottle in the cupboard, with the half-altered uniform. The needle was still stuck in the cuff. She had never pulled it out.

The cupboard door shut. Outside the window nets were drying on the stone steps of the ferry landing, and at the far side of the flats the tide had begun to come in again.

Frost’s Descent was forty-six days away.

(The End)
