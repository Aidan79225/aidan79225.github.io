*February · Rain Water*

Now, as to Qiao Zheng: it was past nine that night when he got back to the branch office from the town hall.

The branch office was still the same building on the docks. The sign was nailed two deep, the new one on top, black on white, Fog Harbour Branch, the Prohibition Bureau; a strip of the old one showed underneath. When the fog came in off the sea, that strip was the first thing to get wet. The doorman sat inside the door with one lamp and a pot of tea, and looked up at the footsteps.

“Young Qiao. So late.”

“The town hall.”

“How did the talk go?”

“One gentleman fell asleep.”

The doorman gave a laugh and bent to top up his tea.

It was seventeen paces to the end of the corridor. Qiao Zheng’s office was at the end, on the right. It had been the accounts room. The filing cabinets had been moved out, and the wall still kept their mark, a patch whiter than the rest. Across the corridor was the director’s office. After the director went it had stood empty for some months, and since November it had been the interrogation room. The frosted paper on the glass door had not been torn off. He switched on the lamp, put the annual report on the desk, took off his uniform jacket and hung it behind the door. Behind the door there was only one peg.

The annual report was bound in blue cloth faded to grey, its corners knocked round, and inside the cover was the Assay Office’s library stamp, with one corner of the stamp missing. The day the Assay Office’s things were taken over, twenty years of annual reports had been packed in a crate to go to the provincial capital. He had pulled this one out of the crate and signed a slip for it. The slip was still in his drawer. He turned to that report. ‘The Behaviour of Methanol in Fractional Distillation’, eight pages, one figure, a curve climbing from the bottom left corner, and two numbers on the horizontal axis: sixty-four and a half degrees, seventy-eight. He had held this page up before more than a hundred people at the town hall. Now he looked at it alone.

After the figure came a table: twelve samples, seized on the docks in the summer twelve years before. Sample number, place of seizure, specific gravity, boiling range, methanol content, and in the last column, verdict. In print, every letter square, twelve lines. He counted them once: Poisonous, nine; Drinkable, three. The annual report was the director’s to edit. That year the director had not changed this column, and it had been printed as it stood.

He shut the annual report.

He pulled open a drawer, the bottom one. A brown paper envelope. It had come in December. The sender was a trading firm in Upper Town; he had sent a man to ask, and there was no such firm in Upper Town. He had not filed it in the cabinet. It had been locked in here two months. In the envelope was a stack of typed sheets, in the Assay Office’s format. He drew the sheets out and did not turn through them. He went straight to the one at the bottom. That one he had put at the very bottom himself, at the door of the chemical supplier, face down.

He turned it over.

In every report the verdict was one word: “Poisonous” or “Drinkable”.

In the Assay Office’s official format, the verdict column was to say “Pass” or “Fail”.

That one word he had seen in December, and put at the very bottom. That night what was wanted was the address. The address was right, the chemical supplier was sealed, twenty-seven men went to gaol, and an official letter of commendation came from the provincial capital. The handwriting stayed locked in the drawer two months. Tonight at the town hall he had come to the temperature for cutting the heads and looked up, and in the seat by the wall in the back row the Teacher was sitting, his coat still on, both hands on his knees. He had stopped for half a sentence.

He turned the typed sheets over one by one. Ten years of samples, and in the last column of every sheet, that one word. Done on a typewriter, not a word of it by hand, no signature.

The drawer above held official papers. On top was the inventory of the Assay Office takeover, sent down from the provincial capital, and pinned to it a list of those made redundant, seven names, in order of years of service. He did not look at the name on the first line. His finger counted down from the second line, counted to seven, and he turned the inventory over, face down.

Back in the bottom drawer, beside the envelope, a velvet-lined case. He took it out and opened it. The hydrometer lay in its velvet bed, the chamois folded in four and tucked in beside it. In October Young Zhou had put this case on his desk and said Master He had left it, with word to wipe it with the chamois once a month and not to let the new men measure soy sauce with it. “You aren’t a new man, Inspector,” Young Zhou had said. Qiao Zheng had accepted it. He had wiped it once a month, four times to date, and never used it.

He picked up the chamois, wiped the glass tube once and held it up under the lamp. There were no bubbles in the tube. He put it back in its velvet bed, shut the lid, put the case back in the bottom drawer with the envelope, and locked it. The key went into the pocket of his uniform trousers.

Outside the window the crane chains rattled once and stopped. He switched off the lamp.

---

On Monday morning Scarface Cheng opened his mouth.

The interrogation room was the old director’s office. The desk was the same desk. There was no teapot on it now. Qiao Zheng sat behind the desk with a book, a fountain pen and the brown paper envelope in front of him. On the other side of the desk was a chair, the one kept for outsiders, a few inches lower than the rest. Scarface Cheng sat in that chair, his handcuffs off, one foot cocked up on the other leg, the sole of his shoe towards the desk. He had drawn a match from the branch chief’s matchbox on the desk, pinched off the head, and was picking his teeth with the stick. The branch chief leaned against the wall by the filing cabinet. He did not sit. He smoked, and flicked his ash into an enamel mug. The clerk sat at a small table by the door, with a typewriter and a stack of paper; he was the one who had done two years of English at the university in the provincial capital, the one who coached Xiaoman in English on Sundays. The door was shut. Outside it in the corridor stood one of the Bureau’s agents.

The branch chief spoke first.

“Two months. The provincial capital has sent a third official letter to press us.” He flicked off his ash. “Mr Cheng, you said you wanted to see Inspector Qiao. Inspector Qiao is here.”

Scarface Cheng did not look at the branch chief. He looked at the envelope on the desk.

“Inspector,” he said slowly, a word at a time, with stops between, “what you people read out to me at this desk in December, drum by drum, four per cent, six per cent. The one behind the union, seven per cent. Inspector, those figures, did you assay them yourselves?”

Qiao Zheng’s fountain pen wrote a line in the book.

“You tell me.”

“You didn’t.” Scarface Cheng shifted the matchstick to the other corner of his mouth. “Three years of the Prohibition Bureau, and how many crates of mine has it sealed? Sealed, and the next day open as usual. You didn’t know where my cousin’s chemical supplier was. You didn’t know the one on the edge of the Old Town roundabout was mine. That one was open right opposite your checkpoint table, open three years, Inspector, and your cars went past it hundreds of times.”

The branch chief’s cigarette stopped at his lips.

“So?” said Qiao Zheng.

“So somebody gave you those figures,” said Scarface Cheng. “He knows where our industrial spirit comes from. He knows the address of every one of our drinking dens. Those reports, the reports your Prohibition Bureau got, they came out of the Assay Office.”

The typewriter rattled off a run and stopped.

“Mr Cheng,” said the branch chief, “you are here to give a statement about your drinking dens, not about our papers.”

“I’ll give it,” said Scarface Cheng. “Four drinking dens. You’ve sealed them all, Inspector. My cousin’s chemical supplier, sealed. The drums come from the provincial capital, eight a month, by water, landed at No. 3 Wharf, and the papers say paint. Inspector, you have all that already. What I’m telling you today is what you haven’t got.”

He put down the foot he had cocked up, and cocked up the other.

“There’s a man on the docks they call Teacher,” he said. “Since White Dew last year, the men on the docks have stopped drinking my liquor. I sent men to ask who makes what they drink now. A month, thousands of men on the docks, and not one opened his mouth. Inspector, have you ever asked questions on the docks? You know better than I do how tight the men on the docks keep their mouths shut.”

Qiao Zheng wrote.

“I looked for him for three months,” said Scarface Cheng. “The truck, my men followed it. Followed it to the level crossing at the goods yard, a train went through, and it was gone. On the Saltflat mudflats my men caught it once. A hundred and twenty bottles, soda bottles, the same as mine, the trade mark scraped off, a paper label with two words written on it, and the writing crooked. That time I thought I very nearly had him. The second time, the goods yard. Down came the barrier, through went a train, and they were gone. Inspector, you tell me, where does a delivery boy get train times?”

“I am not the one giving a statement today,” said Qiao Zheng.

Scarface Cheng laughed. The scar down the left side of his face moved with it.

“I burned half the teahouse’s signboard. He didn’t come out,” he said. “The business in the alley is not your affair, Inspector. That’s for the detectives. I’ll say only this: ten days after that, my four drinking dens were all sealed in one night. Twenty-seven pairs of handcuffs, Inspector, not one too many, not one too few. You knew how many men were in the building before you came.”

Qiao Zheng wrote it down as said. That night he had taken every pair of handcuffs the branch office could find, twenty-seven pairs. It was the branch office’s number, not the chemical supplier’s. He did not say so.

“Who is the Teacher?” the branch chief asked.

“If I knew,” said Scarface Cheng, “it wouldn’t be me sitting in this chair today.”

He took the matchstick out of his mouth, looked at it and laid it on the edge of the desk.

“But I know what kind of man he is,” he said. “He can assay spirit, and more exactly than you can. He has ten years of my accounts, drum by drum, down to the days they were landed at No. 3 Wharf. Inspector, how many men like that are there on the docks?” He stopped. His eyes lifted from the envelope and came to rest on Qiao Zheng’s face. “Those sheets you were sent. What does the last column say?”

Qiao Zheng’s pen moved on the paper. He did not raise his head.

“Not pass or fail,” said Scarface Cheng. “My cousin’s chemical supplier held a licence. It was assayed three times, and each time I saw the paper. Pass. The ones you spread on this desk in December, that isn’t what the last column says. Inspector, I can’t read many words, but that word I know. Poisonous.”

He put his foot down, braced both hands on his knees and leaned forward.

“Nobody but an Assay Office man could write a thing like that.”

Having said it, he watched Qiao Zheng’s face.

Qiao Zheng’s pen did not stop. He finished the line, turned the page and wrote the date on the new one.

“Clerk,” he said, “that last sentence. Take it down as spoken.”

The typewriter rattled off a run. The clerk stopped and looked up.

“Inspector, for the Teacher, what do I put under name?”

“Unknown,” said the branch chief.

The typewriter clacked twice more.

The branch chief stubbed out his cigarette in the enamel mug, came over from the wall, planted both hands on the edge of the desk and looked at Scarface Cheng.

“Mr Cheng, the man you have described today is an Assay Office man,” he said. “When the Assay Office was taken over there were eleven technical staff. Four kept on, seven made redundant. Inspector Qiao has the list.”

Scarface Cheng looked at Qiao Zheng and laughed again.

“The branch chief is not wrong. You have the list, Inspector,” he said. “You came out of the Assay Office. You knew who wrote those sheets before I did.”

The agent came in and the handcuffs went on. Scarface Cheng stood up, and the low chair went over behind him. No one picked it up. He walked to the door and looked back.

“Inspector, you’ve got me, and the docks aren’t one bottle short,” he said. “Think about who is really doing your business for you.”

The door shut. The branch chief set the chair upright and sat down, in the low chair, his knees against the edge of the desk.

“Inspector Qiao, seven men,” he said. “Go through them one by one. I want a name to answer the provincial capital’s third letter.”

“Of the seven made redundant, two have gone back to the country, one died last year, and one is dispensing at a chemist’s,” said Qiao Zheng. “That leaves three. I’ll go through them one by one.”

“Start with the one who assays best,” said the branch chief. He stood up, took his matchbox and went.

The clerk drew the paper out of the typewriter, checked it through once and laid it in front of Qiao Zheng. The statement ran to three pages. On the last page: the Teacher, name unknown, and under it one line: Nobody but an Assay Office man could write a thing like that. Qiao Zheng signed the corner of every page.

The clerk gathered up the pages, walked to the door and stopped.

“Inspector, Miss He has not come for her English since January,” he said. “I waited three Sundays.”

“I see,” said Qiao Zheng.

He picked up the matchstick from the edge of the desk and dropped it in the enamel mug. The envelope he took back across the corridor, put back in the bottom drawer and locked away.

---

The records room was at the other end of the corridor. It had been the storeroom where the Assay Office kept its samples, the window nailed shut, one lamp. At the takeover last August the Assay Office’s twenty years of counterfoils had been carried in volume by volume. No one had opened them. A layer of dust lay on top.

At one o’clock on Wednesday afternoon he pushed the door open and went in.

The smell of mothballs came first, and the smell of brown paper under it. The counterfoils were bound by the year, stitched with hemp thread, the year written with a brush on the cover of each volume, and they ran along the shelf from left to right, twenty volumes. He counted from the right, counted to the tenth, pulled it out and put it on the table in the middle. Then he pulled out the ninth and the eighth. Dust fell on the table. He swept it with the back of his hand.

From the stack of typed sheets in the envelope he picked one. The sample number was in the top right corner: wharf number, year, serial number. He turned through the volume for that year. The paper had yellowed, the corners curled, the ink gone brown. He turned to that serial number.

The same number. Place of seizure: drinking den behind the union. Specific gravity, boiling range, methanol content seven per cent. In the verdict column, one word, in fountain pen, each stroke separate: Poisonous. Under it the signature: He Qiusheng.

He laid the typed sheet beside the counterfoil. He checked them line against line, and not a figure differed. The typed sheet had no signature. The counterfoil had.

He had found that man’s handwriting.

He did not shut that volume. He picked another typed sheet and turned through another volume. It matched too. A third sheet. It matched. He checked seven sheets, and all seven were there, seven signatures, the same pen. On the seven counterfoils not one verdict column said Pass or Fail. On the pages beside them, the ones other men had signed, it said Pass, Fail, Pass. In twenty years of counterfoils only one pen did not write to the format; from fifteen years ago, there was a second.

He stacked the three volumes and pushed them to the corner of the table.

At the far left of the shelf were the oldest volumes. He did not touch them. The one he pulled out was the sixth from the left, the one from fifteen years before.

This one he turned through slowly. On the early pages the signatures were other men’s: Old Zhang, Old Wu, He Qiusheng, the director. In the autumn his own name appeared at the bottom right corner of a page. Qiao Zheng. The writing was crooked, the last stroke of the Zheng dragged out too long, the strokes heavier than anyone else’s beside them. On this page the specific gravity was wrong by one place, and someone had ringed it in red pencil, a very round ring, with nothing written beside it. He remembered that red pencil. That man wrote nothing. He only drew a ring, and when he had drawn it he pushed the counterfoil back and said nothing and waited for him to see it for himself.

He turned on. And on. In the winter pages the red rings were fewer.

Then came that page.

This page he remembered. He did not have to look for it. The sample was a crate of imported liquor seized on the docks, no duty receipt, no brand. Specific gravity, boiling range, methanol content three parts in ten thousand. It was the first time he had written a report through by himself, from beginning to end, with no one beside him. In the verdict column he wrote one word: Fail. He wrote it square and neat. When he had written it he took it over to that man’s bench.

That man gave it one look and pushed the counterfoil back.

“The verdict column.”

“No duty receipt, no brand,” he said. “By the format, it’s a fail.”

That man said nothing. He picked up his pen and drew a line through the word Fail, not heavily, so that the word could still be read underneath. Beside it he wrote one word. When he had written it, he signed on the ‘Checked by’ line.

“Teacher, why do you write it like that?”

“Because pass or fail is for the officials,” that man said. “Poisonous or not is for the people who drink it.”

“But the duty receipt—”

“The duty receipt is the Customs’ business.” That man pushed the counterfoil over to him. “What you assay is the spirit.”

He took a fresh form and wrote the report again, with that one word in the verdict column, and it was sent up. The one with the line through it stayed in the counterfoils.

This page of the counterfoils: a line through the word Fail. Beside it one word, in another pen: Drinkable. Checked by: He Qiusheng.

Fifteen years. For every bottle he had assayed he had written only one word in the verdict column, the way that man wrote it. No one at the Office had ever corrected him, not even the director. After the Assay Office was taken over, the new form had two boxes printed on it. He wrote between the boxes. Those above sent it back, and he wrote it again. Three times it was sent back, and then they stopped sending it back.

On the table was a wooden rule, the one the Assay Office had used for drawing figures, its markings worn faint. He laid the rule along the binding, held it down with his left hand, took the corner of the page between the fingers of his right and tore along the rule. It tore very straight.

He tore the page out and put it in his pocket.

The three volumes went back on the right, the sixth back on the left. There were still twenty volumes on the shelf. He blew the dust off the table, switched off the lamp and went out. In the corridor the doorman had already lit the lamps. Outside the windows it was dark.

“Young Qiao. In there the whole afternoon today.”

“Checking figures.”

The doorman nodded and did not ask. Qiao Zheng went past him. The strip under the sign was wet, and round each nail was a ring of rust.

---

Thursday began with fog. When he went out, the doorman’s tear-off calendar was turned to the nineteenth, with a line in red at the bottom: Rain Water.

He did not take the branch office’s car. The bus to the South Slope roundabout was ten cents. The flagstones of the slope were wet. Pupils from the girls’ school were walking up in twos and threes, blue skirts, umbrellas unopened under their arms. He kept to the side to let them pass. Below the wall of the municipal primary school was the market. He went round by the road outside and took one look over the wall. The pitch at the very back, against the wall, Su Qi’s, was empty. The treadle sewing machine was gone. On the wall was a nail, and the mark of something that had hung there.

The woman from the greens stall next door saw the uniform, wiped her hands on her apron and came over to the wall.

“Looking for Ah Qi, Inspector?”

“I’m looking for Mrs He.”

“She packed up before New Year’s Day,” the woman said. “Handed over the last of her customers’ clothes, rolled up her tape measure and said she was going to stay at her mother’s for a while. The pitch has stood empty ever since. The market people don’t dare let it. They say she’ll be back.” She glanced at the nail, and then at his collar. “One of the Bureau men came once before, to have a waist let out. Young. Not you.”

“Not me.”

“The Saturday before she went, Ah Qi packed up early,” the woman said. “Not a word more than she had to. She came on the Monday to hand over the last two pieces, and not a word more then either. Ah Qi never talks much, but never as little as that.”

Qiao Zheng nodded once. The woman went back to her own stall. The smell of spring onions stayed by the wall.

The flat, the second floor. The stairwell had one lamp, on the half-landing below, shining on the banister. He climbed these two flights once a year, at Mid-Autumn, two steps at a time, and slowed only at the second floor. Today he took them one step at a time.

The door was shut. He knocked three times.

No one answered. He waited a while and knocked three times again. Downstairs someone was taking down shutter boards, and at the foot of the slope a bus sounded its horn once. He knocked a third time, three knocks.

There was no telephone in this building. He knew that. Every Mid-Autumn he simply came.

Half a sheet of paper showed under the door. He bent to look. The school bill from the girls’ school, printed, and on the half that showed, one line: Tuition and sundry fees, next term: thirty-two dollars, with a red stamp below. The line for the addressee was inside the door and could not be seen. He did not pull it out.

The door of the flat at the foot of the stairs opened a crack. Mrs Chen stood leaning on a broom, and her eyes went first to his uniform.

“From the Prohibition Bureau?”

“I’m a friend of the He family. I come every Mid-Autumn.”

“The one who brings the pomelos.” Mrs Chen opened the door a little wider. “Mrs He left first thing on New Year’s Day, carrying a suitcase. Didn’t even stay to keep the New Year. She took Xiaoman. Mr He saw them to the foot of the slope and came back alone.”

Qiao Zheng looked at the half sheet of paper under the He family’s door.

“And Mr He?”

“Night shift. He comes back at daylight. Sometimes he doesn’t,” said Mrs Chen. “When he does, he doesn’t put the light on.”

“Where does he work the night shift?”

“The docks,” said Mrs Chen. “I don’t know which firm. Mr He doesn’t say much.”

She shifted the broom to her other hand and lifted her chin towards the He family’s door.

“Inspector, has something happened to Mr He?”

“No,” said Qiao Zheng. “I came to look in on him.”

Mrs Chen nodded once. The crack of the door narrowed and did not close. He went downstairs. Out on the slope he looked back once at the second floor. The balcony door was shut, and no washing hung on the rail.

---

The surgery was halfway up the slope, with a wooden board at the turn of the stairs: Physician · Diseases of the Chest. Under the board a slip of paper was pasted: Out on a call. Back at four in the afternoon.

He went back to the branch office. At four he climbed the slope again, and the slip had been taken down. A woman with a child in her arms sat on the waiting bench. The electric fan was still, with a cloth over it. The nurse asked his surname. He took out his papers and laid them on the nurse’s book. The nurse looked, wrote nothing, and went in.

Dr Lin sat behind his desk with the stethoscope round his neck. The light box was off. Qiao Zheng put his papers on the desk. Dr Lin picked them up, looked, and pushed them back.

“A doctor does not talk about his patients’ illnesses.”

“I am his student,” said Qiao Zheng. “The Assay Office, fifteen years. His name is He Qiusheng.”

Dr Lin looked at him, pulled open the drawer and took out a ledger. He opened it, glanced at it, and shut it. Having shut it, he opened it again. He turned to a page and laid his hand on the page.

“He Qiusheng. Diagnosed in July. Consumption, a cavity in the upper lobe of the right lung,” he said. “He needs to go into hospital, he needs to have air pumped, he needs to go up to the hills. He said, we’ll see. October to November he collected his medicine as usual. In November I told him to come in every two weeks to have air pumped, to press that side down. After November he did not come back to be seen.”

“Who paid for the medicine?”

Dr Lin turned the ledger round and pushed it in front of him. Under the name He Qiusheng, line by line: medicine, October; medicine, November. The payer’s column was the same for both: Nanji Teahouse. Below them one more line, December, with more writing in it than the ones above: medical fees paid in full, sanatorium in the hills paid half a year in advance, cash. Payer, Nanji Teahouse.

“At the end of December he came himself. In an old suit,” said Dr Lin. “Cash, paid in full. Half a year at the sanatorium, three hundred dollars, in advance. He said to write the Nanji as payer, and I wrote it. He has not gone up to the hills.”

Qiao Zheng looked at the name He Qiusheng. His finger came down on the payer’s column.

“Which teahouse is the Nanji?”

“A teahouse in the Old Town. The one beside the Tin Hau Temple,” said Dr Lin. “I only take the money. The name written against it is whatever the patient tells me.”

He took the ledger back. His hand went back on the page. He did not shut it.

“Inspector Qiao,” he said, “in November he sat in the chair you are sitting in, and I said one thing to him: if the Prohibition Bureau comes asking, I won’t lie. He said, tell them the truth.” He shut the ledger, put it back in the drawer and pushed the drawer to. “I have told the truth.”

Qiao Zheng’s hands lay on his knees.

“Dr Lin, how is he now?”

“The only film is the one from July. In November I listened to his chest. The right side was duller than in July. I told him to have another taken. He said, we’ll see,” said Dr Lin. “If he does not come, I cannot know how big the cavity is now. This disease waits for no one. It grows by itself.”

He pulled the prescription pad over, wrote one line, tore it off and pushed it across.

“The injection from the provincial capital. It works on cavities. A course of twelve. He has not had it,” said Dr Lin. “When you see him, Inspector, give him this. He will understand it.”

Qiao Zheng picked up the prescription. One line: the name of the drug, the dose, twelve. He folded it in four and put it in his pocket, where it lay against the page from the counterfoils.

“I’ll pay the fee.”

“A student asking after his teacher’s illness. No charge,” said Dr Lin. He stuck his pencil back in his pocket. “Next.”

---

From South Slope down to the Old Town was twenty minutes on foot.

The chemist’s at the foot of the slope was open, the small sign at its door, Liquor Sold on Prescription, wet with fog. Behind the counter the chemist saw the uniform and moved a row of brown bottles further in. Qiao Zheng did not stop. The door on the edge of the Old Town roundabout was shut, and across its boards was pasted the branch’s seal, December’s, signed with his own name, the edges of the paper already lifting. There were three stone steps at the door, and on the steps sat a man with an empty bowl in his hand. Seeing him come near, the man turned the bowl upside down on his knee. Qiao Zheng walked past him. Over on the docks the whistle went for the change of shift, a long one, across a stretch of Old Town roofs.

Under the arcade at the edge of the Tin Hau Temple square half the Nanji’s signboard hung, one character, Nan, its edge burned black, the broken end of the plank pointing down. The peanut seller’s stall was by the pillar, with a new bamboo tray. Qiao Zheng went in from under the arcade.

Five or six tables in the main room were taken. On the counter stood a wireless, Cantonese music, the erhu bowed thin. In the corner was a little stage, on the stage a stool, a pipa in its cloth cover leaning against the leg of the stool, the lamp unlit. At the table by the stairs sat an old man, his back to the wall, his face to the main door, in a railway uniform washed nearly white and buttoned to the top, his tea bowl at his left hand. As Qiao Zheng came in, the old man’s eyes went first to the door and then dropped back to the bowl. He did not raise his head.

Qiao Zheng sat down at a table by the board partition, his back to the partition, the stairs in front of him. The waiter came over.

“Tea, sir?”

“A pot. I’m looking for Mr Liang.”

The waiter glanced at his uniform, said yes, and brought the tea first. A pot, a bowl, and a small china jar besides, with a small spoon standing in its mouth. Customers down from Upper Town wanted sugar, the waiter said. Qiao Zheng pushed the sugar jar to the corner of the table and poured half a bowl of tea. He added none. The waiter went upstairs.

At the table by the stairs the old man lifted his tea bowl, took a mouthful and set it back at his left hand.

The stairs sounded. Counsel Liang came down, pale grey suit, handkerchief in hand. At the bottom his eyes counted over the tables in the main room, and only then came to Qiao Zheng. The gold tooth flashed.

“Inspector Qiao,” he said. “Last Thursday, the town hall, the fifth row. I was there. Well said. Before you take in a bottle, light a lamp—I act for three bars, and I went back and told them all.”

“You were in the fifth row, Mr Liang. I saw,” said Qiao Zheng. “Those cards went out to the bars. Did one go to a barrister’s chambers?”

“Where a bar owner has a lawsuit, his lawyer has a card,” said Counsel Liang. “Come and sit upstairs. The main room has ears.”

Qiao Zheng stood up and passed the table by the stairs. The old man was holding his tea bowl, halfway up, and did not raise his head. They went upstairs. The stairs had twelve steps.

The board partition of the private room was papered with old newspapers. One table, one lamp. Counsel Liang sat in the host’s seat, his back to the window; Qiao Zheng sat opposite, his back to the partition. The waiter brought up the pot of tea, and the sugar jar with it. Counsel Liang gave the sugar jar one look and told the waiter to take it away. The door shut.

“Inspector Qiao,” and Counsel Liang’s fountain pen tapped once on the table, “let me say this at the outset: my clients’ accounts I cannot speak of; my own accounts you may ask about as you please.”

“The surgery on South Slope,” said Qiao Zheng. “He Qiusheng’s medicine, October to November, payer Nanji Teahouse. Paid in full in December, with half a year at the sanatorium in the hills in advance besides. Mr Liang, a teahouse paying for a man’s medicine. Which going rate is that?”

“Dividend.” Counsel Liang drew out his handkerchief and wiped his forehead once. “Mr He has invested in the Nanji. He is a minor shareholder. A shareholder draws his dividend, and the teahouse settles a few expenses on his behalf, to save a sick old gentleman a journey to the bank. Half the teahouses in Upper Town keep their books that way.”

“An assayer who was made redundant. Severance pay is reckoned by years of service,” said Qiao Zheng. “What did he invest in a teahouse with?”

“Where a client’s money comes from, I do not ask,” said Counsel Liang. “I look only at whether what he brings is money.”

“But books are books, Mr Liang,” said Qiao Zheng. “The tax office can see the teahouse’s books. So can I.”

Counsel Liang picked up the fountain pen and put it down again.

“You saw the signboard as you came in, Inspector,” he said. “Half of it. That was November.”

“Scarface Cheng gave his statement on Monday,” said Qiao Zheng. “A bucket of firewater, one match, two men. He says the teahouse’s signboard burned and the man did not come out. Mr Liang, who was he waiting for?”

“Waiting for the insurance,” said Counsel Liang. “The insurance paid. Inspector Qiao, two teahouse signboards burn in the Old Town every year, and the Prohibition Bureau has never looked into one of them.”

“This one I have looked into.”

“And found a man who is in gaol,” said Counsel Liang. “That’s the going rate.”

Counsel Liang laughed. He spread the handkerchief on his knee.

“Inspector Qiao, Mr He came out of the Assay Office, and so did you. Last Thursday’s lecture was given on Mr He’s report of twelve years ago. That much I need not say; there is no one in Upper Town who does not know it.” He laid the fountain pen flat. “You come today, Inspector, to ask about an old gentleman’s medicine. Is that official business, or private?”

Qiao Zheng looked at him.

“Mr Liang,” said Qiao Zheng, “do you know what the charge is for harbouring the proceeds of bootleg spirit?”

“Inspector Qiao,” said Counsel Liang, wiping away the sweat, his smile unchanged, “do you know what the charge is for searching premises without evidence?”

Nothing was left in the private room but the erhu from downstairs.

“I have not searched,” said Qiao Zheng. “I am drinking tea.”

“Tea is ten cents,” said Counsel Liang. “It goes on my account.”

Qiao Zheng felt in his uniform pocket for ten cents, put it on the table and pushed it up against the pot. He stood up.

“Mr Liang,” he said, “when you see Mr He, give him a message from me: his student called.”

“Mr He has a great many students,” said Counsel Liang. “Thirty to a class at the night school.”

“He’ll know which one.”

He opened the door and went down. At the table by the stairs the old man was still there, the tea bowl at his left hand, its lid askew. Qiao Zheng went past him. The old man’s eyes were on the door. Not a word.

---

When Qiao Zheng came out of the Nanji it was full dark, and the fog was rolling in from the sea again.

He stood under the arcade and watched the lamps of the Old Town come on one by one. The incense burner at the temple gate showed first, a point of red; then the shopfronts along the arcade lit their lamps, and the light fell on the wet flagstones, a patch before each door. The peanut seller had packed up, his bamboo tray turned upside down by the pillar. The fog crossed the square. The point of red at the temple gate went first, then the lamps along the arcade, fading from the far end, fading as far as the one over his head.

Out towards the sea the foghorn sounded once.

Qiao Zheng put his hand in his pocket, took out the folded sheet of paper and opened it by the arcade lamp. It was the page he had torn from the report counterfoils the afternoon before. A line through the word Fail, and beside it one word, in another pen. On it was that man’s signature: He Qiusheng.

His finger pressed once on the badge at his collar.

He did not give the paper to the branch chief.

He put it back in his pocket and walked into the fog.
